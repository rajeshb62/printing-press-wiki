Anton Koberger

Anton Koberger

Overview

Anton Koberger (also spelled Anthoni Koburger; c. 1440 — 1513) was the most successful printer-publisher of 15th-century Germany and one of the largest printing entrepreneurs in all of Europe during the incunabula period. Operating from Nuremberg, he built a publishing empire that employed 24 presses and over 100 journeymen, produced more than 220 editions, and maintained an international distribution network stretching from Paris to Warsaw and from Rome to Antwerp. His most famous publication was the Nuremberg Chronicle (1493), a lavishly illustrated world history containing over 2,000 woodcuts. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Early Life and Family

Anton Koberger was born in Nuremberg around 1440, into an old patrician family of the city. One of his ancestors served as burgomaster of Nuremberg as far back as 1349 and took part in the successful effort to overthrow the rule of nobles over the city. For two centuries the Kobergers were among the leading citizens of Nuremberg. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

He was probably brought up as a goldsmith — an occupation he returned to in his later years. This metalworking background placed him in a tradition common among early printers: Gutenberg himself had been a goldsmith, and many of the first designers and engravers of typefaces came from the ranks of metalworkers. Albrecht Durer, whose original trade was also goldsmithing, served as godfather for one of Koberger’s children, and Koberger’s eldest son was apprenticed to Durer. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Koberger married twice and fathered more than twenty children. Several of his sons and nephews continued the business after his death, and some returned to the family’s original trade of working in gold and silver. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The Printing Enterprise

The first printing office in Nuremberg had been established in 1470 by Heinrich Kefer of Mainz (who had been an assistant of Gutenberg) together with Johann Sensenschmid. Koberger devoted himself to the new art in 1472 and issued his first dated volume in 1473: Boethius’s De Consolatione Philosophiae with the commentary of Thomas Aquinas — a dignified work representative of the scholarly character of his subsequent output. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

By around 1509, according to the contemporary writer Johann Neudorffer, Koberger was employing approximately 24 presses with over 100 workmen, including compositors, pressmen, binders, correctors, illuminators, and designers. All were provided with meals by their employer in a building apart from the works, and they moved between the two buildings at regular hours with military discipline — a remarkably organized and industrial-scale operation for its time. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Known editions from his presses number 220 or more, almost all in folio format of considerable size, noted for their correctness and elegance. He printed at least 15 editions or impressions of the Bible, including a German Bible in 1483 (illustrated with woodcuts, using Gothic types) and multiple Latin editions with different commentaries. His catalogue included 33 editions of the Bible in total. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Typefaces and Printing Technique

Koberger followed Gutenberg in adopting Gothic (Fraktur) style types. For his 1475 edition of the Reformation der Stadt Nurnberg, he used a refined semi-Gothic script that closely approximated the later Fraktur style, and which was also used for the major work Leben der Heiligen (1488). In 1492, for his edition of Virgil, he employed a type based on Venetian models, similar to the types used by the father-in-law of Aldus Manutius. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

His printing office did not possess Greek type, leaving him at a disadvantage compared to Venetian and Parisian printers. In the edition of Boethius issued in 1576, lines of Greek quotations were left blank to be filled in by hand — a reminder of the technical limitations even at Europe’s largest printing operation. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The Nuremberg Chronicle (1493)

Koberger’s most famous publication was the Liber Chronicarum (Book of Chronicles), commonly known as the Nuremberg Chronicle, compiled by the physician and scholar Dr. Hartmann Schedel. It appeared in 1493 in both Latin and German editions, and is one of the most remarkable printed works of the 15th century. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The book contained over 2,000 woodcut illustrations (many reused for multiple cities and figures), designed by Michael Wolgemut and Wilhelm Pleydenwurff — the workshop that included the young Albrecht Durer. A contract for the illustrations was made in 1491, demonstrating the considerable xylographic and typographic resources at Koberger’s disposal. The patrons were Sebald Schreyer and Sebastian Kammermaister, wealthy Nuremberg citizens who financed the illustrations. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The print run was estimated at 2,500 copies (1,500 in Latin, 1,000 in German). Koberger was paid four gulden for 500 pages of printing — approximately one pound sterling per page. The artists worked fourteen-hour days for an advance of 1,000 gulden (shared between them), and production took about two years. By 1509, 558 copies remained unsold, and the artists’ heirs were charged 1,200 gulden for unsold copies and debts. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The Nuremberg Chronicle included one of the first printed maps of Europe, reproduced from woodcuts. De Vinne describes it as a “large and thick folio … a summary of the history, geography and wonders of the world.” The reused woodcuts — the same portrait serving for Paris of Troy, Odofredus of Germany, and the poet Dante — reflected the immense production pressures of the period. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Publisher and Bookseller

Koberger’s activity as a publisher (Verleger) was so extensive that his own presses could not keep up with demand. He contracted work to other printers, notably Johannes Amerbach of Basel (who had begun his career as a corrector in Koberger’s shop) and Gruninger of Strasbourg. His surviving correspondence with Amerbach (the Koberger letter-book, covering 1493—1509) reveals him as a meticulous, orderly, and punctual businessman. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

He built an international distribution network with agents in Frankfurt, Leipzig, Vienna, Basel, Strasbourg, Cologne, Paris, Budapest, Warsaw, Venice, Florence, Rome, Antwerp, Bruges, and Leiden — a remarkable achievement given the risks and interruptions of travel and trade in the early 16th century. He was the first bookseller in Germany (and possibly in Europe) to issue a classified catalogue of current publications. Koberger’s bookshop was described by Kapp as the best-equipped repository for standard literature (Sortiments-Buchhandlung) in Germany. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Koberger’s network gave him a decisive advantage over his great contemporary Aldus Manutius of Venice, who struggled to maintain distribution north of the Alps. While Aldus relied on direct correspondence with individual scholars, Koberger worked through the organized book trade. For a number of years he was recognized as the natural leader of the German book trade. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Character and Reputation

Contemporaries spoke of Koberger in consistently glowing terms. Jodocus Badius (Badius Ascensius), the noted Paris printer, called him “that glorious Nuremberger … esteemed by honourable men everywhere as the prince of booksellers … the man who conducted his business with the most exact integrity, and with the highest ideals … with whom the production and distribution of good books was carried on as a sacred trust.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Conrad Leontorius and Jacob Wimpfeling described him as “a true Humanist,” despite his predominantly theological publishing list. He maintained close friendships with liberal thinkers such as Conrad Celtes, Albrecht Durer, and Willibald Pirckheimer. Emperor Maximilian addressed him as “our trusty Anthoni Koberger, whose great service entitles him to honour, alike from ourselves and from the realm.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

His associates emphasized his enormous capacity for persistent work, far-seeing enterprise, conscientious regard for the rights of others, large conceptions combined with careful attention to detail, keen sense of humour, and genial manner. He appears to have made many friends and no enemies, valued by Catholics as a serviceable ally and regarded by Reformers as a personal friend. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Decline and Death

Koberger died in 1513. His death came before the fiercest phase of the Reformation, which would transform the German book trade. The rise of Luther’s pamphlets and cheap vernacular literature (Flugschriften) undercut the market for the heavy theological folios on which Koberger’s business was built. His catalog was almost entirely devoted to Catholic theology in Latin — he printed only three German-language works in forty years. The new era demanded cheap books in the vernacular, not costly Latin folios. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The business continued under his nephew Johannes, and later under his sons Anthoni (the younger) and Melchior, but the energy and initiative of the founder had departed. In the 27 years after his death, only 53 works were added to the list. The last work bearing the Koberger imprint was the Bohemian Bible, issued by Melchior in 1540. A further factor in the decline was the impairment of capital from the withdrawal of shares due to the long series of heirs, few of whom wished to retain interest in the book trade. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Legacy

Koberger occupies a unique position in the history of printing. He was the first printer-publisher to organize book production on an industrial scale, anticipating the great publishing houses of later centuries. His operation — 24 presses, 100+ workers, international distribution, multiple typefaces, commissioning of external printers — was the largest and most sophisticated in 15th-century Europe. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

His imprint appeared on no fewer than 236 separate works between 1473 and 1513, nearly all substantial folios. In the words of Jacob Wimpfeling (1501): “We Germans practically control the intellectual world … It is for this work that we owe the largest acknowledgment for the service of a man like Koberger, who employs his publishing facilities only for the production of that which is best.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The typefaces and distribution networks he pioneered, the magnificent illustrated books of his press, and his model of the printer-publisher as a large-scale entrepreneur make Anton Koberger a defining figure of the incunabula age — the man who, more than any other, demonstrated that printing could be not merely a craft but a major industry. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Key Publications

  • 1473 — Boethius, De Consolatione Philosophiae (first dated work)
  • 1475 — Reformation der Stadt Nurnberg (with refined semi-Gothic type)
  • 1478 — Biblia Latina (printed twice)
  • 1483 — Die Deutsche Bibel, mit Bildern (German Bible, illustrated)
  • 1488 — Leben der Heiligen (Lives of the Saints)
  • 1491 — Schatzbehalter des Reichtums des ewigen Heils (woodcuts by Wolgemut)
  • 1493 — Liber Chronicarum (Nuremberg Chronicle; Latin and German editions)
  • 1494 — Gesta Romanorum (first printed edition)
  • 1497-1504 — Biblia cum Postilla Hugonis (8-volume folio Bible, Koberger’s most costly work)
  • 1511 — Biblia Latina
