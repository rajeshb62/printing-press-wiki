Overview

Nicolas Jenson (c. 1420—1480) was a French-born master engraver, typefounder, and printer who, after being dispatched by King Charles VII to learn the new art of printing in Mainz, established himself in Venice where he created the most admired Roman typeface of the fifteenth century. His types set the standard for Latin typography across Europe and directly influenced the work of Claude Garamond, William Morris, and Bruce Rogers. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Early Life and Background

Jenson was born in France around 1420. He served as master of the mint in Tours, where his skill as an engraver of coins and medals earned him a reputation as one of France’s most accomplished craftsmen. The precision required for die-cutting coinage translated directly into the punchcutting skills that would later define his typographic work. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Mission to Mainz (1458)

Following the appearance of Gutenberg’s Bible, the French court took notice of the revolutionary new art. On October 3, 1458, King Charles VII issued an order to the royal mint-masters to select an experienced man and send him to Mainz to learn the art of printing. The choice fell on Nicolas Jenson, described in the Arsenal document as a man “dexterous in the engraving of stamps and of letters.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Jenson was instructed to inform himself of the invention and report back to France. He succeeded in acquiring the art as ordered, but by the time he was ready to return, Charles VII had died (1461). His successor, Louis XI, showed little interest in the undertaking. Jenson, understanding that the new monarch would not support his mission, never returned to Paris. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Move to Venice (c. 1468)

Rather than return to a France indifferent to printing, Jenson carried his knowledge to Venice, the most powerful commercial republic in Europe. He arrived around 1468 and by 1470 had established his own press. Venice at the time was already home to German printers — Johannes de Spira (John of Speyer) had begun printing there in 1469 — but Jenson brought something they lacked: the eye of a master engraver and an intimate understanding of humanist letterforms. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The Jenson Roman Type (1470)

Jenson’s crowning achievement was the Roman typeface he cut for his edition of Eusebius’s De Praeparatione Evangelica, printed in 1470. This typeface is widely regarded as one of the most beautiful ever created. It combined:

  • Evenness of colour: Perfectly balanced stroke weights across all characters, giving the printed page a uniform grey texture far more legible than dense blackletter.
  • Clean serifs: Sharp, horizontal serifs derived from Roman monumental inscription capitals, providing a clear baseline.
  • Generous proportions: Open counters and generous spacing that made each letter distinct without sacrificing page colour.
  • Harmonious letterfit: Adjacent letters sitting together without awkward gaps or collisions.

De Vinne records that Jenson’s Roman “supplanted” the Black letter throughout Italy, France, Spain, and eventually England. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] The type was so perfectly proportioned that it remains a benchmark of type design to this day. Jenson’s letterforms reconciled the best traditions of humanist handwriting with the precision of metal punchcutting, achieving a synthesis that manuscript scribes could never match and that later printers have rarely surpassed. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The type became known in Italy as lettera antiqua tonda (round antique letter), while northern Europe called it Antiqua, France and the Netherlands called it Romain or Romeyn, and England called it Roman. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Printing Office and Major Publications

Jenson’s printing office became one of the largest in Venice, producing a steady stream of high-quality editions. He also cut Gothic types and a Greek type (without capitals) to suit the diverse demands of the market. His major publications include:

  • Eusebius, De Praeparatione Evangelica (1470): His masterpiece, printed in the celebrated Roman type.
  • Pliny the Elder, Naturalis Historia (1472): A landmark edition of the great natural history encyclopedia, printed with his finest Roman face.
  • Works of St. Jerome: A substantial folio edition of the Church Father’s writings.
  • Nosce Te (1480): A book of devotion by Giovanni di Dio, notable as the earliest example in Venice of an ecclesiastical testamur (imprimatur) printed in a book. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The quality of Jenson’s presswork was exceptional in every respect. Lorck notes that the ink in Jenson’s Venetian editions was “intensely black, velvet-soft and glossy” — superior even to the ink used in the Gutenberg Bible. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Business Enterprise

Jenson’s printing operation was one of the most heavily capitalised in fifteenth-century Venice. He was backed from an unknown date by two Frankfurt merchants: Johan “Big John” Rauchfass, a merchant whose diverse activities made his shares in Jenson’s company “little more than an exotic item in his portfolio,” and Peter Uglheimer, who became a considerable book-collector in his own right. This triangular partnership was ultimately liquidated by the payment of 1,000 ducats in gold to Uglheimer’s heirs. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Jenson’s will disposed of approximately 4,000 ducats, placing him among the wealthiest printers of his generation. His focus shifted increasingly from production to sales as his career progressed. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The Company (1480)

By the late 1470s, Jenson and his rival John of Cologne — who had inherited the press of Wendelin of Speyer after the overproduction crisis of 1473 — were the two dominant forces in Venetian printing. Together they produced 260 of the approximately 600 editions printed in Venice up to 1481 (43% of the total). Their nearest rivals averaged only seven editions each. As Victor Scholderer observed, there was “a sharp contrast between the few successful men at the top and the crowd of transient and financially embarrassed phantoms down below.” ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Fearing plague and market saturation, the two rivals joined forces. On May 29, 1480, a protocol created the five-year syndicate of Nicholas Jenson, John of Cologne, and Company — a partnership to which John contributed an initial 5,000 ducats worth of books. The syndicate was able to turn out some twenty folio editions in its first twelve months and exercised such absolute domination over the Venetian industry that it became known simply as “The Company.” Both main partners died almost immediately afterwards, and evidence of “The Company’s” overt activity vanishes from the end of 1481. But its influence persisted: shares in the partnership passed through the Torresani family and into the Aldine circle, and the model of “The Company” served as “a golden image of success, beckoning the next generation to imitate, and prosper.” ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The partnership structure involved Andrea Torresani as a printer, bookseller, and underwriter active for more than a decade before he formed his own partnership with Aldus. Jenson’s matrices ultimately passed to Torresani, who claimed to have worked with Jenson’s types — connecting Jenson’s Roman letterforms directly to the Aldine Press. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Rivalry and Competition

Jenson worked alongside and competed with the German printers who had preceded him to Venice. Johannes de Spira (John of Speyer) had received a five-year monopoly on printing in Venice in 1469, but died the following year. His brother Wendelin continued the press and eventually formed a partnership with Johann of Cologne, who in turn later united with Jenson himself. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] The Venetian printing scene was crowded and competitive; by the end of the century some 150 presses were operating in the city.

Recognition by the Pope

Jenson’s reputation extended beyond the printing trade. Pope Sixtus IV recognised his mastery and honoured him with the title of Count Palatine (Pfalzgraf), a rare distinction for a printer. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Death and Succession

Nicolas Jenson died in 1480, a wealthy and respected figure. His printing office was purchased in 1479 (or shortly before his death) by Andrea Torresani of Asola, who continued the business. Torresani’s daughter Maria married Aldus Manutius around 1500, and Aldus thus gained control of Jenson’s former press, inheriting both its physical plant and its typographic tradition. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] This direct line of succession connects Jenson’s workshop to the Aldine Press, the most famous publishing enterprise of the Renaissance.

Typographic Legacy

Jenson’s Roman type exercised an influence on typeface design that extends to the present day. It became the prototype for the Roman types used by:

  • Claude Garamond (sixteenth century): The great French typefounder whose Roman faces were themselves inspired by Jenson’s proportions.
  • William Morris (nineteenth century): Revived Jenson’s Roman for the Kelmscott Press, using it as the model for his Golden Type.
  • Bruce Rogers (twentieth century): Designed the Centaur typeface (1914) directly inspired by Jenson’s original.

The Jenson Roman established the convention that Latin and vernacular texts would be printed in Roman letters, while German-language works continued in blackletter — a typographic divide that persisted well into the twentieth century. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

One of the most beautiful books of the Renaissance, the Hypnerotomachia Poliphili (1499) printed by Aldus Manutius, used a Roman type directly derived from Jenson’s, and the type was later revived commercially as Poliphilus. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Key Achievements

  • Master of the mint at Tours, royal engraver to Charles VII
  • Dispatched to Mainz (1458) to learn Gutenberg’s printing art
  • Created the Jenson Roman type, arguably the most beautiful Roman typeface ever cut (1470)
  • Printed the Eusebius De Praeparatione Evangelica (1470), one of the masterpieces of incunable printing
  • Printed Pliny’s Natural History (1472), a landmark of classical publishing
  • Established one of the largest and most productive printing offices in Venice
  • Honoured by Pope Sixtus IV with the title of Count Palatine
  • His types passed to the Torresani/Aldus circle, directly influencing the Aldine Press
  • Posthumously inspired type revivals by Garamond, Morris, and Rogers ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]
