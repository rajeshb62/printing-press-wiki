Overview

Jodocus Badius Ascensius (1462 — 1535), also known simply as Jodocus Badius or Ascensius, was a Flemish scholar-printer who established the most important learned press in Paris during the first third of the sixteenth century. Born in Ghent, Flanders, and educated at the University of Louvain with further studies in Italy, he settled in Paris and founded his printing house around 1498. Over the course of approximately twenty-five years, his press produced nearly four hundred separate works, almost all in folio or quarto format. Badius was a libraire jure (sworn bookseller) of the University of Paris, a scholar-publisher of the highest standing, and the first printer in Paris to use Greek characters in a printed text (1505). ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Early Life and Education

Badius was born in 1462 in Ghent, then part of the County of Flanders in the Burgundian Netherlands. He received a thorough humanist education at the University of Louvain and subsequently travelled to Italy to continue his studies, where he absorbed the classical scholarship then flourishing in the Italian academies. The epithet “Ascensius” derives from his birthplace — according to Lorck, he was named after the town of Asch near Brussels. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Move to Paris and Entry into Printing

Badius first came to prominence not as a printer but as a teacher, giving lectures on the Latin poets, first in Paris and later in Lyons. It was in Lyons that he first became directly involved in the book trade, having been engaged by the printer Johann Trechsel as a corrector of the press. He married Trechsel’s daughter, cementing his connection to the printing profession. After Trechsel’s death, Badius moved to Paris and established his own press around 1498. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Paris Press

Badius’s press quickly became the leading publisher of classical and humanist texts in Paris. According to the bibliographer Panzer, his output reached nearly four hundred separate editions over a twenty-five-year career, nearly all in the large folio and quarto formats suited to scholarly readers. He was a libraire jure of the University of Paris, a formal recognition of his standing in the academic book trade. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The Paris Book Trade Context

The title of libraire jure was not merely honorary. The University of Paris exercised absolute authority over the book trade from the 13th century onward — confirmed by Charles VI in 1411, who declared that “it belongs and appertains to our said Daughter [the University], and to none other, to establish and institute all the booksellers.” From among the sworn booksellers, four were annually elected as the quatuor majores librarii or Grands Jurez, who governed the entire trade, set prices (the taxe), examined new shipments, and enforced the approval system for theological works. Badius, as a libraire jure, was both a beneficiary of this system — it protected established printers from unlicensed competition — and subject to its oversight, particularly where theological texts were concerned. ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

Greek Type in Paris

Badius holds the distinction of being the first Paris printer to include Greek characters in a printed book. In 1505, he issued the Annotationes in Novum Testamentum of Lorenzo Valla, which necessarily included several passages of Greek. The Greek type used in this edition was designed by a certain Hermonymus, a Lacedaemonian then sojourning in Paris. In 1519, Badius also printed the Institutiones Imperiales, which included a few Greek passages. This was two years before the establishment of Giles Gourmont’s dedicated Greek press in 1507, giving Badius priority in the introduction of Greek typography to Paris. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Key Publications

Badius’s list reflects his close connections with the leading humanist scholars of his age:

  • Erasmus: He published editions of Erasmus’s Adagia and The Praise of Folly (Moriae Encomium), with Erasmus himself attesting to the thoroughness of Badius’s scholarship. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]
  • Budaeus (Guillaume Budé): Badius published the works of Budaeus, the greatest French humanist of the generation and a scholar comparable to Erasmus. This included Budaeus’s great Commentary on the Greek Language. The friendship and scholarship of Budaeus were of marked service to Badius throughout his career. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]
  • The Ship of Fools: Badius published the first Paris edition of Sebastian Brandt’s Navis Stultifera (the “Ship of Fools”) and also printed a French translation, La Nef des Folz du Monde (the second French version), which he himself edited and to which he added his own variations. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]
  • Philobiblon: He produced an edition of Richard de Bury’s Philobiblon, the celebrated medieval treatise on the love of books. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]
  • Complete Works: His press issued the Opera Omnia of Politian and of Lorenzo Valla, among other substantial scholarly editions. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]
  • Censorial Works: As evidence of his accepted orthodoxy, Badius was employed by the University to publish certain censorial works against Martin Luther, including treatises on Luther’s doctrine (1521) and on “certain contentions of Dr. Luther” (1523). ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Printer’s Mark and Motto

Badius’s printer’s mark was a representation of a printing press of the time, one of the earliest pictorial depictions of a printing press used as a publisher’s device. Beneath it he occasionally used the motto:

Aere meret Badius laudem auctorum arte legentum.

According to Greswell’s interpretation, this is to be understood as meaning that Badius by his liberality elicits the praise of authors, and by his typographic skill and accuracy that of readers. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Scholarship and Character

Badius was unusual among early Parisian printers in uniting scholarly erudition with typographic skill. He was not merely a publisher but an editor and commentator: he provided many of his classical editions with his own annotations. Lorck describes his books as distinguished by beauty and correctness (Schonheit und Korrektheit), and notes that he was simultaneously a bookseller, printer, type cutter, and type founder — a complete master of every branch of the book trade. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Family and Succession

Badius’s three daughters married three of the most famous typographers of the sixteenth century:

  • Michael Vascosanus (Vascosan): Married one of Badius’s daughters and continued the business after Badius’s death, producing 297 works and eventually becoming Royal Printer in 1566. Vascosan’s books were noted for the beauty of their printing and paper, most printed in Roman type. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]
  • John Roigny: Another son-in-law who became a notable Parisian printer. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]
  • Robert Estienne (Robertus Stephanus): Married Badius’s daughter Perette, who was herself a learned woman fluent in Latin. Robert Estienne became the greatest of the Estienne dynasty and one of the most important printer-publishers in European history. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Badius’s son, Conrad Badius, was also a learned scholar and printer. Persecuted as a Calvinist, he fled to Geneva in 1549, where he continued both his literary and typographical work. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Death and Legacy

Jodocus Badius Ascensius died in 1535. His press, carried on by Vascosanus and linked by marriage to the Estienne dynasty, left an enduring mark on French humanist printing. He was the leading figure in Parisian scholarly publishing in the generation before Robert Estienne came to prominence, and his press set the standard for classical text production in France. His introduction of Greek type to Parisian printing in 1505 was a milestone in the spread of humanist learning north of the Alps, and his editions of Erasmus, Budaeus, and the Latin classics made him the indispensable publisher of the early French Renaissance. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]
