Venice

Venice became the leading printing city of fifteenth-century Europe. If mainz was the birthplace of European movable-type printing, Venice was the first great international capital of the printed book. By 1500 it had produced thousands of editions and had become a center for humanism, classical scholarship, commercial publishing, and the development of Roman and italic type. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Why Venice Was Ideal for Printing

Venice combined several advantages: long-distance trade networks, capital, paper supply from northern Italy, skilled artisans, a large literate elite, and access to Greek and Latin scholars. Its maritime commerce made books easier to distribute, while its republican institutions could grant privileges and monopolies to printers. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The first rag paper was made around 1300, and paper-making soon became important in Italy. In 1373 the Venetian Senate forbade the exportation of rags from the dominions of the Republic — an act that recalls the edict of Ptolemy Philadelphus in 290 BC forbidding the export of papyrus from Alexandria. This ensured a domestic supply of raw materials for paper production. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Venice’s watery defences made it nearly impossible to blockade or besiege, so its commercial undertakings went on practically undisturbed even during wars that devastated other Italian states. The Venetian government was also prompt to recognise the value of the new industry, giving more care to furthering printing by privileges, concessions, and recognition of the printers’ guild than any other Italian state. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Venetian Connections with Constantinople and the Greek Manuscript Trade

Venice possessed valuable relations with the Greek world through its old commercial ties with Constantinople and Asia Minor. Through these connections Venetian printers secured first pick of the manuscripts of many Greek texts that became known to Europe between 1490 and 1540. These texts were brought partly from monasteries spared by the Turkish conquerors and partly by scholars who fled Constantinople after the fall of 1453. As word spread that Venice offered compensation for editing Greek texts for the press, impoverished refugee scholars found their way to the city, bringing their manuscripts with them. This gave Venice a commanding lead in Greek publishing. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Opposition from Scribes: The Genoa Card-Makers Petition (1474)

The scribes and manuscript copyists recognised that their art was endangered by the new technology. In 1474, the copyists of Genoa went so far as to petition the Senate for the expulsion of the printers. The petition was disregarded, but the incident demonstrates the resistance the new art faced. The Genoese scribes had earlier petitioned (1471) to be protected from printer competition in producing breviaries, Donati, and psalters, claiming these were their livelihood. The Genoese printers were eventually driven out by a cabal of scribes, making Genoa the only Italian city where the old scriptores succeeded, even temporarily, in expelling the printers. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Cost and Scale of Early Venetian Printing

As an example of printing costs in Venice, an agreement from 1478 shows that 930 copies of the complete Bible were printed for 430 ducats (paper furnished separately). The selling price of Bibles in 1492 varied from 6 to 12 ducats (approximately 30to60 in late-19th-century valuation, depending on binding). ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

By the 1490s a demand had arisen for cheap popular books. Venetian printers increasingly used Gothic type because it saved space, enabling lower prices. The last decade of the fifteenth century was the peak of Venetian printing: it witnessed the founding of the Greek press by Aldus, Vlastos, and Caliergi, the first printing in Arabic and other Eastern languages, and the beginning of romance and novel publishing. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Venetian Printing Industry Structure

An Unregulated and Hazardous Trade

The printing industry grew up too quickly for the regulations that normally controlled medieval crafts. As Erasmus wrote acidly, becoming a printer was a great deal easier than becoming a baker. This freedom of access explains the bewildering variety of people involved in printing — from noblemen like Andrea Badoer and Francesco Viaro (whose names stand on copyrights of the 1490s) to debt-ridden former teachers and choir-masters like Gerard of Lisa. The “boom” of the fifteenth-century press in Venice was a mad scramble in which numerous competitors trampled one another underfoot for a vision of prosperity that few attained. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Survival Rates of Printing Companies

Rather more than 100 printing companies have been identified in Venice up to 1490: twenty-three were still active during the following decade; only ten survived the century. Some were so small and short-lived that the only surviving traces of their existence are two or three unattributed and often undated editions. As Lowry captures the contrast: “the printer, better informed, complained of the ‘treacherous rage and rivalry which are usual in this miserable trade.’ He knew that he was operating in a transient, hazardous and ruthless world.” ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Capital Investment and Labour Costs

The first obstacle for any printer was capital equipment. Jenson’s workshop contents were valued at 500 ducats — a very high figure for the time. The press itself was the least problematic item: a wooden printing press could be bought for as little as 16 ducats (as recorded in a 1474 sale in Padua). Far more costly was type — the punches, matrices, and type-metal required specialised skills. Goldsmiths figure largely in early printing because the filing of letter-forms onto steel punches was familiar from their work with hallmarks, coins, and seals. Subcontracting began very early: Gutenberg paid 100 gulden to the goldsmith Dunne. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Labour was also expensive. A compositor in Padua earned 3 ducats per month in 1475 — precisely the sum the Venetian government offered in 1492 to a skilled hydraulic engineer. The master printer was normally responsible for housing and feeding his workmen, costing between 5 and 50 ducats per year in rent, facing constant problems of fluctuating food prices. Disputes were frequent and domestic: a group of compositors in Padua once staged a walk-out because their beds had not been made. They were sacked. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The Rise of the Publisher-Underwriter

The need for capital investment soon created a special kind of entrepreneur — the publisher or underwriter. The Frankfurt merchant Johan Rauchfass (“Big John”) regarded even his shares in Jenson’s company as little more than an exotic item in his portfolio. Lucantonio Giunti and the Scotto family operated as professional underwriters, forming contracts with several different printers. Giunti more than doubled his company’s capital base between 1491 and 1499, and had almost doubled it again by 1509. Andrea Torresani was active as a printer, bookseller, and underwriter for more than a decade before forming a partnership with Aldus. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

From the first decade of its existence, the Venetian printing industry was acquiring a thoroughly capitalistic structure, with control passing into the hands of merchants and printer-booksellers. By the end of his life, Jenson was concerned almost entirely with sales. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Syndicate Structures

Printers banded together to spread risk. In 1507, the de Tortis brothers, Zorzi Arrivabene, Lucantonio Giunti, and Amadeo Scotto set out a joint plan for five years. Expenses and profits were divided into four equal shares: Giunti and Scotto provided only financial backing (one share each); Arrivabene and the de Tortis offered four presses and paper (third share). Printed sheets were stored in a warehouse to which each partner had a key, and decisions on prices and print runs were taken by majority vote. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Other contracts reveal the harsh realities of the financier-craftsman relationship. In 1478, Leonardus of Ratisbon and Nicholas of Frankfurt agreed to produce 930 copies of the Bible over nine months. Nicholas provided paper and 243 ducats expenses; Leonardus handled press-work but could draw the money only in instalments as each batch of sheets was delivered, and was barred from taking other work. As Lowry summarises: “the craftsman, in fact, was bound hand and foot to the financier.” In Padua, the printer Peter Maufer ended with debts of 225 ducats to his sponsor Bartolomeo Valdezocco, who took possession of the entire workshop. The new contract made him “the servant” of the financier. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The 1472-3 Overproduction Crisis

An uncontrolled enthusiasm for the classical revival, inspired by the scholars who advised printers, deluged the Italian market with more Latin classics than it could absorb. The Venetian industry reeled, its output declining by 65 per cent in 1473. In Rome, Sweynheim and Pannartz claimed to have printed 20,475 copies but were left with unsold stock, their workshop “full of printed sheets, empty of necessities.” This crisis taught printers caution: both Jenson and John of Cologne followed much more conservative policies after 1475. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The Academic Market and Legal Publishing

Law made the fortunes of the leading printers. Legal texts accounted for 29 per cent of Jenson’s output and 48 per cent of John of Cologne’s; later, 51 per cent of Arrivabene’s and 53 per cent of Torresani’s editions were legal. The Venetian industry was perfectly placed to supply the nearby universities of Padua, Bologna, Pavia, and Ferrara. A legal text represented a large investment in paper, time, and labour, but for established men who could sound out the market, profits could be sure and large. The printer Bonetus Locatellus avoided law entirely and concentrated on philosophy — which accounted for over half his total production — while Torresani brought out Aristotelian commentaries in the early sixteenth century. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The printers’ caution and conservatism extended beyond legal texts. They favoured established circles of readers and did little to encourage a popular market, playing follow-my-leader with academic trends. By the early 1490s they were operating in predictable grooves. Classical authors remained popular — Virgil, Horace, the elegiac poets, satirists, Livy, and above all Cicero — with Philippus Pincius reprinting Virgil four times during the 1490s alone. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

German Immigrant Printers and the Venetian Industry

For the first decade of its existence, the Venetian printing industry was dominated largely by German immigrants. John of Speyer came from Speyer; his brother Windelin continued after him; John of Cologne was from Cologne; Nicholas of Frankfurt and Leonardus of Ratisbon both operated in Venice. Even the humanist Giorgio Merula, while working enthusiastically for John of Cologne, could not forget that the invention had come from “that once rugged and brutish land of Germany.” ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

When Italian printers began to push themselves forward after 1480, they still came from outlying towns and kept their local origins before the public — Philippus Pincius of Mantua, Bernardinus Benalius of Bergamo, and Andrea Torresani of Asola. Differences of dialect and local prejudice were strong: it was perilously easy for sophisticated, metropolitan Venetians to treat the newcomers as boorish, grasping, and provincial. Until the age of Aldus, there was little sign of anything more than a business relationship between printers and scholars. The printer, with his grubby ground-floor workshop and drunken assistants, was regarded as a small artisan — a world apart from the humanists he served. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The Jenson-Torresani-Barbarigo Office and the Aldine Partnership

Andrea Torresani and the Jenson Inheritance

Andrea Torresani of Asola was active as a printer, bookseller, and underwriter for more than a decade before he formed a partnership with Aldus Manutius. His claim to have worked with Jenson’s types and the fact that some thirty founts similar to Jenson’s design can be traced across Italian centres suggest that Jenson’s technical legacy flowed directly into the Aldine Press. The tradition that Jenson sold matrices to other printers is strongly supported by this evidence. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The Barbarigo Partnership

The Aldine Press was not a small scholarly venture but a powerful organisation underwritten by one of the most successful publishers of the age (Torresani) and the nephew of the titular head of the Venetian state. A notarial act discovered by Mario Brunetti in the records of the Procuratori di San Marco reveals that Pierfrancesco Barbarigo — son of Doge Marco Barbarigo and nephew of Doge Agostino Barbarigo — was a formal shareholder in the company founded in 1495. The act speaks of “a company for the printing of books between the late lord Pierfrancesco Barbarigo…on the one side, and the late lord Andrea Torresani, bookseller, with the late lord Aldus de Manutiis…also taking a certain part.” ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Pierfrancesco Barbarigo was not merely a sympathiser who helped Aldus on one occasion, but a shareholder who put the weight of his capital and the influence of his ducal family name behind the company from its very beginning. Zuane Bembo, writing admiringly of Pierfrancesco’s son Santo, noted that Santo’s father had “helped Aldus with several thousand ducats.” The documents show payments divided squarely down the middle: one half going to the Barbarigo estate, the other to Torresani (whose share is linked with Aldus’s name). The working capital and profits were divided into two equal shares, with the Barbarigos controlling one and Torresani and Aldus jointly holding the other. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The Physical Book Trade and the Printers’ Quarter

The printers rapidly took over the parishes of San Zulian and San Paternian as their particular quarter. By the early 1490s, rank upon rank of bookstalls tempted the passer-by walking from the Rialto down the Merceria towards San Marco. Two of the largest and fastest-growing private libraries of the age belonged to Venetian citizens: the diarist Marin Sanudo and Cardinal Domenico Grimani. The look of the city itself was affected by the burgeoning trade in printed books. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

First Venetian Printers

The Decor Puellarum and the Earliest Books

The first book printed in Venice was the Decor Puellarum (A Treatise of Instruction for Young Girls), but its date is disputed. Venetians claimed it was printed in 1461, but the weight of authority places it at 1471. The printer was Nicolas Jenson, a Frenchman, and the contest for priority in Italian publishing rests between Jenson and the two Germans of Subiaco. Another early volume, Epistolae Familiares, appeared in 1470 from John of Speyer (Johannes de Spira), a German who received a monopoly from the Venetian government in 1469. A fourth early book, Miracoli della Gloriosa Verzine, was printed by an Italian, Lavagna of Milan, and was the only early printed Venetian book in the Italian language. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The 1469 Monopoly Grant to John of Speyer

The earliest official document relating to the printing-press in Venice was published in 1493 by the Abbate Jacopo Morelli, prefect of the Marcian Library. This document is an order of the Collegio (cabinet) of Venice, dated September 18, 1469, proposed by the Doge’s councillors, granting John of Speyer (Johannes de Spira), a German resident, a five-year monopoly on printing in Venice and its territories. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Lowry notes that petitions of this sort were very common in Venice — they covered everything from improved windmills to experiments with poison-gas — and were normally treated with the same polite encouragement John received; few came to anything. The thirty or so members of the College who voted on the privilege had much else on their minds: war with the Ottoman power in the Aegean, the manoeuvring of Italian neighbours suspicious of Venice’s rapid mainland expansion, and above all the preservation of the lucrative Eastern trade Venice had monopolised since her defeat of Genoa in 1381. John’s petition was a small item of extra business, and there was little to show that printing would transform the city more radically than the Sultan would ever do. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

John died within months of gaining the monopoly in 1470. His work was continued by his brother Windelin, but the privilege died with the original holder and competitors were soon thrusting themselves forward. A disgruntled scribe complained in 1473 or 1474 that the city was “stuffed with books” — and he was perfectly right: 176 editions had been published since the beginning of the decade; by the end of the same decade the figure would be 593; by the end of the century roughly 150 Venetian presses had turned out over 4,000 editions. This represented nearly twice the known production of Paris (Venice’s nearest rival), between a seventh and an eighth of total European output, and, roughly, twenty books per individual member of the Venetian population. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

John of Speyer was one of the few early printers to record his edition sizes. His first edition of the Epistolae Familiares comprised just 100 copies, but demand was so great that a second edition of 600 copies was begun four months later and completed (in two impressions) within three months. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Madonna Paola, Widow of John of Speyer

The widow of John of Speyer, Madonna Paola — daughter of the painter Antonello da Messina — played a remarkable role in the early Venetian printing industry. After John’s death, she brought her personal talents to bear on cementing and diversifying the interests of John of Cologne’s company, which had salvaged the remains of Windelin of Speyer’s operation after the overproduction crisis of 1473. She first moved in with the printer Manthen (though apparently without marrying him), then at an unknown date married another printer, Rinaldus of Nijmegen, who was promptly drawn into the syndicate. Finally, in 1477, she married off her daughter Hironima to the prosperous bookseller Gaspar of Dinslaken, who gained a dowry of 3,000 ducats — well above the limits permitted in Venice — and was immediately made a partner. Hironima, long after, still held stocks valued at 660 ducats in 1511 and was owed a further 207 ducats in gold by Lucantonio Giunti. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Nicolas Jenson and the Growth of Venetian Printing

Nicolas Jenson, born in Champagne c. 1420, was sent by Charles VII to Mainz in 1458 to learn the secrets of printing. He returned to France in 1461 but found no opportunity there; by 1465 he was in Venice, and in 1470 he began a printing and publishing business that became the most important in Italy. He perfected one of the most admired Roman types of the fifteenth century, giving printed pages a clarity and proportion that contrasted sharply with the dense Textura of the gutenberg-bible. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Lowry provides crucial new detail on Jenson’s career and business. If the tradition that Jenson was trained in the French royal mint is correct, this would explain much about his success, his influence, and the size of his investment. To the trained metallurgist, filing letter-forms onto steel punches was a familiar task from experience with hallmarks, coins, and seals — the punch, matrix, and mould processes were readily derived from existing mint techniques. He valued the contents of his workshop at the very high figure of 500 ducats, specifically excluding the steel punches which he bequeathed separately to one of his partners. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Marin Sanudo called Jenson “richissimo,” the most celebrated publisher in Venice during the 1470s. Jenson died in 1480 as a papal Count Palatine and the head of an international company, but his actual fortune — approximately 4,000 ducats as revealed by his will — was modest by Venetian standards: a mere tenth of the sum one spice-dealer made from a single year’s shortage, and nothing alongside the hundreds of thousands left by Venice’s merchant-noblemen. Sanudo’s epithet captured the reputation of printers’ wealth in a city where “the glitter of gold was more inviting than anywhere in fifteenth-century Europe.” ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Jenson’s Business Partnerships and “The Company”

Jenson was backed from an unknown date by two Frankfurt merchants, “Big John” Rauchfass and Peter Uglheimer (a considerable book-collector in his own right). Uglheimer’s shares in this triangular partnership were ultimately liquidated by the payment of 1,000 ducats in gold to his heirs. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

During the central and later 1470s, Jenson and the rival company of John of Cologne fenced cautiously — Jenson concentrating on legal texts, John of Cologne on commentaries — both anxious to avoid the overproduction that had choked the market in 1472-3. But in 1478 plague convulsed the Venetian industry: exactly half of the twenty-two active companies either suspended business or left the city. The two main rivals, among the few strong enough to survive, decided that there was more to be gained by joining forces. On 29 May 1480 a protocol brought into being the five-year syndicate of Nicholas Jenson, John of Cologne, and Company — a partnership to which John made an initial contribution of nearly 5,000 ducats worth of books. This syndicate was able to turn out some twenty folio editions in the next twelve months and exercised such absolute domination of the Venetian industry that it became known simply as “The Company.” Both main partners died almost immediately afterwards. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

By the end of his life Jenson was concerned almost entirely with sales rather than production, a sign of the capitalistic structure overtaking Venetian printing. Law made the fortunes of both Jenson and John of Cologne, accounting for 29 per cent of the Frenchman’s output and 48 per cent of the German’s. According to Victor Scholderer’s calculations, rather more than fifty presses turned out some 600 editions up to the end of 1481: of those, 260 (43 per cent) were the work of Jenson and John of Cologne’s companies alone; their numerous rivals averaged only seven editions each. As Scholderer commented, there was “a sharp contrast between the few successful men at the top and the crowd of transient and financially embarrassed phantoms down below.” ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Jenson’s Types and the Torresani Connection

In 1479, Jenson sold to Andrea Torresano of Asola (later Aldus Manutius’s father-in-law) a set of his punched matrices, which probably became the foundation of the Aldine Press plant. That same year Pope Sixtus IV made Jenson a Count Palatine. Jenson died in 1480. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Lowry adds that Andrea Torresani claimed to have worked with Jenson’s types, and some thirty founts similar to Jenson’s design can be traced in various Italian centres — the strongest suggestion that Jenson also sold matrices to other printers. In 1475 one of Jenson’s partners, Johan Rauchfass, directed Francesco da Bologna (possibly the later Francesco Griffo) to copy two of Jenson’s Gothic founts, probably by striking matrices from his punches. This provides a direct technical connection between Jenson, through Torresani, to Aldus, linking the most celebrated Venetian printing houses. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The three earliest Venetian printers — John of Windelin, John of Speyer, and Nicolas Jenson — employed Roman, Gothic, and Greek characters. Before Greek fonts existed, blanks were left in texts for Greek passages to be filled in by hand. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Aldus Manutius and the Portable Book

Aldus Manutius, active from the 1490s, made Venice central to the history of compact scholarly books. His Aldine Press issued Greek and Latin classics, popularized the small octavo format, and used italic type cut by Francesco Griffo. In this form, the printed book became not only a library object but a portable intellectual tool. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Lowry emphasises that the company of Barbarigo, Torresani, and Manutius represented a fusion of economic and intellectual power that could not easily have been copied. Aldus was the most active member of the most active printing industry in Europe, at least between 1499 and 1504, then again after 1512. He produced editions of up to 3,000 copies — and must be regarded on purely numerical grounds as the most important focus for the distribution of literature to contemporary Europe. He produced first editions of 94 classical and post-classical Greek writers, including Plato, Aristotle, Herodotus, Thucydides, Xenophon, Demosthenes, and all the Greek dramatists except Aeschylus. This amounted to an almost complete cross-section of the Greek literary heritage as reconstituted by the fifteenth-century humanists. Erasmus wrote that Aldus was producing a library not limited by place or time, and Thomas More sent his fictional traveler Raphael Hythlodaye beyond the known world with Aldine editions in his luggage. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The partnership operated with Aldus as the academic force — selecting material, hiring compositors who could work with Greek type, and preparing editions — while Barbarigo provided capital and political connections, and Torresani contributed commercial expertise. The Annulment of 1542 spoke of the company as the foundation of Torresani and Barbarigo, with Aldus only “taking a certain part.” Aldus’ share was never stated but cannot have been large: all payments were divided squarely down the middle between the Barbarigo estate and Torresani. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Significance

Venice shows the second stage of the printing revolution. Gutenberg’s system solved production; Venice solved scale, design, distribution, and cultural prestige. The city linked movable-type to commercial publishing, classical learning, and durable typographic standards. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]
