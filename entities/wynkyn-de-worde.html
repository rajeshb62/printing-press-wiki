Wynkyn de Worde

Wynkyn de Worde

Overview

Wynkyn de Worde (d. 1534), also spelled Wynken de Worde, was the dominant English printer of the early sixteenth century and the successor to william-caxton. Born in Wortheim (Worth) in Alsace (then part of Lorraine), he came to England around 1476 as Caxton’s assistant and foreman, inherited the business after Caxton’s death in 1491, and over the following four decades transformed English printing by moving it out of Westminster into the City of London, vastly expanding its output, and shifting the market from expensive folios to smaller, cheaper books for a broader readership. He printed over 800 editions during his career — far more than Caxton’s approximately 100 — and was the first to introduce Roman type to England. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Early Life and Move to England

De Worde was born in Worth or Wortheim, a town in Alsace (the German-speaking region of Lorraine), from which his adopted surname derives. The precise year of his birth is not recorded, but he was already active as a skilled craftsman by the time Caxton returned from Bruges to establish his press at Westminster in 1476. He appears to have accompanied Caxton to England, serving as his assistant and foreman through the fifteen productive years of the Westminster press. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Lorck records that he was born in Lorraine and came to England together with Caxton, becoming his collaborator and successor, and that as a printer he significantly surpassed his master. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Succession to Caxton (1491)

When Caxton died late in 1491, de Worde inherited the printing business — the presses, type, stock, and the lease of the Westminster premises at the Sign of the Red Pale in the Almonry of Westminster Abbey. He completed several works that Caxton had left unfinished at his death, including the Vita Patrum (the Lyves of Holy Faders) and the second edition of Chaucer’s Canterbury Tales. In the colophon to the Vita Patrum, de Worde paid tribute to his master, recording that Caxton had died while translating the work. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

For the first nine years of his independent career (1491—1500), de Worde continued to operate from Caxton’s old premises in Westminster, styling himself “Printer to Margaret, etc., the King’s Grandame” — referring to Lady Margaret Beaufort, the mother of King Henry VII, who became his patron. During this period he printed the Acts of Parliament with the Royal Arms, and a steady stream of books in the Caxton tradition. ^[raw/articles/pearson-gutenberg-and-art-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Move to Fleet Street (c. 1500)

Around 1500 (the exact year is debated; Lorck gives “probably 1499”), de Worde moved the press from Westminster to Fleet Street in the City of London, to a shop at the Sign of the Sun in the parish of St. Bride. This was the first printing press established within the City of London proper — a crucial step that brought the printing trade into the heart of the commercial and legal district. Fleet Street would remain the centre of English printing and publishing for the next four centuries. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Sign of the Sun became one of the best-known addresses in the English book trade. From this location de Worde produced the vast majority of his editions, building a printing and publishing operation that dwarfed anything Caxton had attempted. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Output and Publishing Strategy

De Worde’s output was prodigious. Modern scholarship attributes more than 800 separate editions to his press, compared to Caxton’s roughly 100. Pearson and Putnam both give the contemporary estimate of “over four hundred volumes” in forty years, but more recent bibliographic research has substantially raised that number. ^[raw/articles/pearson-gutenberg-and-art-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Where Caxton had concentrated on large folio volumes — richly decorated chivalric romances, substantial histories, and the works of Chaucer — de Worde shifted the business strategy decisively toward smaller, more affordable formats. He printed predominantly in quarto and octavo, producing:

  • Grammars and schoolbooks: Latin grammars by Donatus and Alexander de Villa Dei, and English instructional works. Lorck specifically notes that grammatical works formed a significant part of his output. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]
  • Popular romances: Bevis of Hampton, Guy of Warwick, The Four Sons of Aymon, and other chivalric tales that appealed to middle-class readers.
  • Religious works: Primers, books of hours, devotional tracts, and new editions of The Golden Legend.
  • Practical manuals: Books on husbandry, medicine, and conduct.
  • Poetry and literature: New editions of Chaucer, Gower, Lydgate, and Skelton.

This strategy of publishing smaller, cheaper books for a wider audience was de Worde’s most important commercial innovation. He identified and exploited a growing market among merchants, lawyers, students, and literate laypeople — readers who could not afford Caxton’s expensive folios but could buy a quarto or octavo pamphlet for a few pence. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Typographical Innovations and Illustration

De Worde significantly improved the technical quality of English printing. Where Caxton’s types had been crude by continental standards, de Worde cut new punches and cast superior type. Lorck describes his types as “completely different from those used before, distinguished by the beauty of their casting and excellent preparation,” and notes that de Worde was probably his own type founder, since his types are frequently found in the works of other printers. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

He was the first English printer to introduce Roman type into England, using it to mark striking passages in his texts, though he continued to use blackletter for most of his work. ^[raw/articles/pearson-gutenberg-and-art-of-printing.md]

De Worde made extensive use of woodcut illustrations and decorative borders. He was the first English printer to regularly use title-page borders, often repeating the same popular blocks across many editions. Many of his woodcuts were imported from the Continent, purchased at second hand from Flemish and German printers, and were often used indiscriminately — the same block might appear in a romance, a saint’s life, and a grammar, with little regard to its appropriateness to the text. The practice was widespread among early English printers and reflected the scarcity and expense of original illustration. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Major Publications

Among de Worde’s most notable publications were:

  • The Golden Legend (new editions, 1493 and later) — Jacobus de Voragine’s collection of saints’ lives, which Caxton had translated and first printed in 1483. De Worde issued several new editions, keeping this popular work in print.
  • Le Morte D’Arthur (1498) — Sir Thomas Malory’s Arthurian romance, following Caxton’s first edition of 1485.
  • The Canterbury Tales (1498, 1502) — Chaucer’s masterpiece, in editions that built on Caxton’s.
  • Polychronicon (1495) — Ranulf Higden’s universal history, in folio. Lorck singles this out as one of de Worde’s most beautiful books. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]
  • The Ship of Fools (1509) — Alexander Barclay’s English adaptation of Sebastian Brant’s Narrenschiff, with woodcut illustrations.
  • The Book of Hawking and Hunting (reprints of the St. Albans Press’s famous treatise).
  • Numerous editions of Alexander de Villa Dei’s Doctrinale and other Latin grammars that were standard school texts.

De Worde appears not to have undertaken any translating or significant editorial work himself, distinguishing him from Caxton, who had been his own translator for many of his publications. No English printer of the sixteenth century, Putnam notes, is to be placed among the scholar-printers of the calibre of Aldus Manutius, Froben, or the Estiennes. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Printer’s Mark and Relations with Rivals

De Worde’s printer’s mark combined Caxton’s monogram (the letter W with a cross) with his own name, consciously linking his press to that of his predecessor. In this he may have been asserting continuity and legitimacy in a trade where reputation was everything. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

His chief rival was Richard Pynson, another of Caxton’s former assistants, who became King’s Printer. Pynson was a more refined printer who introduced better types from France and produced some of the finest English books of the period. Despite being competitors, Lorck records that de Worde and Pynson maintained good relations. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Copyright and Privileges

In the early 1530s, de Worde became involved in what is considered the first recorded copyright dispute in English printing. A printer named Trevers reprinted, without authorisation, a work for which a privilege had been granted to de Worde. The preface to de Worde’s second edition (1533) contains a vigorous complaint against this “piratical interference with his rights,” though it does not record what action was taken. The work in question was probably Thomas Lupset’s Treatise of Charitie, though the identification is not certain. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Death and Succession

Wynkyn de Worde died in 1534 at an advanced age (Lorck says he died “hochbetagt” — very old). An edition of Aesop’s Fables bearing the date 1535 still carries his name, suggesting that some work may have been completed posthumously or that his name was used by his successor. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

His printing business was taken over by his apprentice and former assistant, John Byddell, who continued the press at the Sign of the Sun. Byddell had been working with de Worde since at least the 1520s and maintained the character of the press, though the output declined in volume. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Significance

Wynkyn de Worde was arguably more influential on the shape of English printing than Caxton himself, for three reasons:

  1. Scale: He multiplied the output of the English press by a factor of eight, producing over 800 editions and making printed books far more common and accessible than they had been in Caxton’s day.

  2. Format innovation: By shifting from folios to quartos and octavos, he created a market for cheap books that reached far beyond the aristocratic and clerical readers who had been Caxton’s primary audience. This was the beginning of popular publishing in England.

  3. Location: His move from Westminster to Fleet Street established the geography of the English book trade for centuries. Fleet Street became synonymous with printing and publishing, a connection that endured until the late twentieth century.

In the broader narrative of the spread-of-printing across Europe, de Worde represents the consolidation and commercialisation of the press in England. Where william-caxton brought the technology and established its viability, de Worde built the business model that made it sustainable. He was not a scholar-printer like the great continental figures, but a practical businessman who understood his market and supplied it efficiently. His early-typefaces and woodcut practices, while crude by continental standards, were appropriate to the English market of the time, and his output laid the foundation for the flourishing of English printing in the later sixteenth century. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]
