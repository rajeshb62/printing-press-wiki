Sweynheym and Pannartz

Overview

Konrad Sweynheym (also Conrad Sweynheym, Schweinheim; from Schwanheim near Mainz) and Johann (Arnold) Pannartz (from Prague) were two German printers who introduced the art of printing to Italy. They established the first press in the Italian peninsula at the Benedictine monastery of Santa Scholastica in Subiaco in 1464 and printed Italy’s first book, the works of Lactantius, in 1465. Sweynheym and Pannartz were probably students of Gutenberg and were among the many German printers who, after the Mainz civil wars, carried the new technology across Europe. Their types were transitional — a semi-Gothic blending German and Italian scribal traditions that evolved into one of the earliest Roman typefaces. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Background and Origins

Sweynheym came from Schwanheim (also spelled Schweinheim), a village between Mainz and Frankfurt. He may have been a protégé of Gutenberg himself. Pannartz was from Prague, and like Sweynheym, likely learned printing in the Mainz workshops that had been scattered after the 1462 sack of the city. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Lorck handbook calls them “wahrscheinlich zwei der überall hin verstreuten Schüler Gutenbergs” — probably two of Gutenberg’s students scattered everywhere. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The German monks at Subiaco and their abbot, Cardinal Juan de Torquemada (Johannes a Turrecremata), played a key role in summoning the printers south. Torquemada, who became a cardinal and was an admirer of the new art, arranged through German monks at the monastery to bring Sweynheym and Pannartz from Germany in 1464. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Subiaco: The First Press in Italy (1464—1467)

Sweynheym and Pannartz arrived at Santa Scholastica in Subiaco, some seventy kilometres east of Rome, in 1464. The Benedictine monastery, founded by Saint Benedict himself in a cave in the hills, was remote and difficult to reach — the Lorck handbook describes it as “in unwirtlichster Berggegend 14 Stunden von Rom” (in a most inhospitable mountain region, 14 hours from Rome), a monastery perched on naked rock that could only be climbed with great difficulty. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Connection to Nicholas of Cusa

There is good reason to believe the choice of Subiaco was not coincidental. Nicholas of Cusa (Nikolaus von Kues), a cardinal, philosopher, and reformer who had close ties to the Benedictine order, had been invited to Rome by his friend Pope Pius II. He had known Gutenberg and may have suggested Subiaco as a suitable location. The 1470 preface to the Sweynheym—Pannartz edition of Saint Jerome, written by Giovanni Andrea de Bussi (who had been Nicholas of Cusa’s secretary and was now Vatican librarian), states explicitly that it was Nicholas’s ardent desire “that this holy art, whose shoots became visible at that time in Germany, should be transplanted to Roman soil.” ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

First Printed Books

In 1465 they printed Italy’s first book from movable type: the works of Firmianus Lactantius (Divinae Institutiones), an early Christian apologist. This was followed later in 1465 by Cicero’s De Oratore (though some sources dispute order), and in 1467 by Augustine’s De Civitate Dei and a Donatus grammar. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Subiaco Type

The type used at Subiaco was a semi-Gothic transitional form — neither purely Gothic Textura nor fully Roman. It blended the rounded humanist letter with certain Gothic features and is known as the Subiaco type. The Lactantius was the first book printed in a type inspired by Roman handwriting. According to Lorck, a manuscript of Augustine’s De Civitate Dei written in Italy in 1459, preserved in Paris, shows the same script that was used for the Lactantius type. On the first sheets of the work, space was left for Greek quotations to be written in by hand; in later sheets the first Greek types were used. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

De Vinne judged the Subiaco Roman type imperfect: “It was not a successful effort, for the traces of Gothic mannerisms are noticeable in almost every letter.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Rome (1467—1472)

The isolation of Subiaco made business difficult. In 1467, Sweynheym and Pannartz accepted an invitation from the brothers Pietro and Francesco, the Marchesi Massimi (Massimi), to move to Rome. They set up their press in the Massimi palace, printing Cicero’s Epistolae Familiares as their first Roman work. This edition introduced what Lorck calls the “pure Antiqua” — a cleaner Roman face closer to the humanist handwriting of the papal chancery. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

In Rome, they were encouraged and supported by Giovanni Andrea de Bussi (also Andrea Bussi, Bishop of Aleria), Vatican librarian and a noted humanist scholar, who served as their editor and corrector. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Roman Press

Over five years in Rome (1467—1472), Sweynheym and Pannartz produced twenty-eight titles — a stately series of editions including:

  • Classical authors: Cicero, Apuleius, Aulus Gellius, Caesar, Virgil, Livy (1469, a celebrated edition of which one copy sold in 1815 for nearly 20,000 marks), Strabo, Lucan, Pliny, Suetonius, Quintilian, Ovid
  • Church Fathers: Augustine, Jerome, Cyprian
  • Biblical works: A Latin Bible and the Bible commentaries of Nicholas de Lyra in five volumes ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

They made new fonts of Roman, Greek and Round Gothic types for the Roman press. In their Roman editions they abandoned the Subiaco type for a purer Roman face, establishing a market for books printed in the new style. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Overproduction and Financial Ruin

Despite the quality and importance of their output, Sweynheym and Pannartz overestimated the market. Their editions ranged from 275 to 550 copies per work (the smaller number for specialized classical texts, the larger for popular works). By 1472 their house was filled with unsold, unbound books. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Their friend and patron Giovanni Andrea de Bussi petitioned Pope Sixtus IV on their behalf, setting forth that they had printed 11,475 copies of twenty-eight works, a very large portion of which remained unsold — and that they were in great distress. The petition is historically important because it records that the standard edition size of the period was 275 copies, and 550 for popular works. But Sixtus IV, who had just succeeded to the papacy and was more concerned with providing for his relatives than supporting a publishing business, declined to help. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The partnership dissolved in 1472. As Lorck puts it: “In dem Verhältnis, wie sich das Bücherlager von Sweynheim und Pannartz füllte, leerte sich ihre Kasse” — as the book warehouse of Sweynheim and Pannartz filled, their cashbox emptied. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Later Years

Pannartz

Johann (Arnold) Pannartz continued printing on his own until about 1475 or 1476, around which time he died. The record of his activities after the dissolution is sparse. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Sweynheym: Map Engraving and Ptolemy

After the partnership ended, Sweynheym turned to engraving maps on copper. He undertook to produce a landmark edition of Ptolemy’s Geography — one of the earliest attempts to illustrate a printed book with engraved copper plates. Sweynheym did not live to see its publication; he died around 1475 or shortly after (some sources suggest 1473 is the last date we hear of him). The Ptolemy was eventually completed with plates finished by Arnold Bucking and was published in 1478. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The Lorck handbook notes that Sweynheym “machte auch die ersten Versuche, Landkarten für die Buchdruckerpresse in Kupfer hoch geschnitten herzustellen” — he also made the first attempts to produce maps for the printing press in high-cut copper. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Typographic Significance

Sweynheym and Pannartz are pivotal figures in the history of typography. Their Subiaco type represents the first deliberate break with the Gothic tradition and the first attempt to create a printed letterform inspired by Italian rather than German manuscript models. The De Vinne account notes that “to please the tastes of their Roman readers they made a new font of Roman types,” though it was still imperfect. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Their typographic evolution followed a clear trajectory:

  1. Subiaco type (1465): Semi-Gothic, transitional — blending humanist rounded forms with Gothic angular features
  2. Roman type (1467 onward): A cleaner Antiqua, closer to Carolingian minuscule and humanist handwriting, first appearing in the Cicero Epistolae Familiares printed at Rome

This transition from Gothic to Roman was not merely aesthetic. It reflected a deeper cultural shift: German printers adapting their craft to Italian scribal traditions and reader expectations. As the spread-of-printing concept notes: “The German printers did not merely transplant a technique; they adapted it.” ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Historical Significance and Legacy

Sweynheym and Pannartz were the pioneers who established printing in Italy, a country that would surpass Germany itself in total book production before 1500. Within five years of their arrival at Subiaco, printing had spread to nearly every city in Italy, almost exclusively through German printers. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Their commercial failure demonstrated the economic risks of overproduction that would haunt the early book trade. Rome, despite being a centre of learning, lay outside the major European trade routes, and their unsold stock consumed their capital. Yet by their ambitious programme of classical and patristic publishing — twenty-eight titles in Rome alone — they established the market for printed books in Italy and proved that the press could serve humanist scholarship as well as religious devotion.

Their petition to the Pope survives as a unique document of early printing economics, recording edition sizes and the scale of overstock in a way no other source from the period does. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The maps Sweynheym engraved for Ptolemy’s Geography were among the earliest copperplate illustrations used in a printed book, pioneering a technique that would become central to Renaissance cartography and scientific illustration.
