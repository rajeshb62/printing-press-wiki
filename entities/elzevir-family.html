Overview

The Elzevir family (also spelled Elzevier) were the most celebrated Dutch printer-publishers of the seventeenth century, operating from Leyden and Amsterdam between c. 1580 and 1712. Their imprint became synonymous with compact, elegantly printed editions of the classics, and the name “Elzevir” passed into common usage as a generic term for small-format, well-printed books. Only Aldus Manutius among the early publishers achieved comparable prestige, and while the Aldine press preceded the Elzevirs by more than a century, the Elzevirs’ sustained output of typographically exquisite, affordable editions established a standard that endured for generations. The principal modern authority on the family is Alphonse Willems’s monograph Les Elzevir: Histoire et annales typographiques (Brussels, 1880). ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1880] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3543]

Louis Elzevir (c. 1540—1617): The Founder

Louis Elzevir, the founder of the dynasty, was born in Louvain (Leuven) in Brabant around 1540. He was raised as a bookbinder in the Flemish university town. Work records of Christopher Plantin indicate that a printer named Johann Helsevier from Louvain worked for Plantin from 1565 to 1588, possibly Louis’s father. Louis himself was employed by Plantin in Antwerp. Two of his children, Matthias (1564) and Louis (1566), were born in Antwerp to Mayke (Marie) Duverdyn. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3545]

Antwerp was the heart of the Reformation movement in the Netherlands, and Elzevir adopted the Protestant faith. When King Philip II sent the Duke of Alba in 1567 to stamp out heresy by fire and sword, thousands of families fled. Louis Elzevir’s family was among them, moving first to Wesel, where his third son Aegidius was born, and later to Douai, where sons Justus and Arnold were born. After a brief return following the Pacification of Ghent (1576), the renewed submission of the Walloon provinces to Spain in 1579 forced the Protestants to flee again. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1882] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3550]

Settlement in Leyden

In 1580, Elzevir crossed into Holland with his five sons and daughter Marie and settled in Leyden, then, after Amsterdam, the most populous city in the province. Leyden’s university had been founded by William of Orange in 1575 to commemorate the city’s heroic resistance during the Spanish siege, and it had quickly become a prestigious centre of European scholarship. Elzevir found support among the many Flemish Protestant exiles already established there, including Vulcanius, Drusius, Lipsius, Heinsius, and William Silvius, the first Printer to the University. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1882] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3552]

He began work as a bookbinder for the university community and soon added a bookshop. The venture proved unsuccessful — the student population was small and the practice of borrowing or sharing textbooks limited demand. Elzevir was forced to give up his first premises but applied to the university authorities for help. Recognising the value of a skilled bookseller, the University Council permitted him to construct a small shop within the university court and appointed him official bookseller and later publisher to the University. He was also made a beadle (pedell) in 1586 with a salary of 72 guilders. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1886] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3557]

Building the Business

With this fresh start, Louis Elzevir built an assured business foundation through persistent labour. He never mastered the art of printing himself — the typographical work bearing his name was done under contract with various printers and initially showed no special distinctiveness. His strength lay in his scholarly judgment and his extensive foreign connections. His correspondence throughout France, Germany, and Italy brought the name of Leyden University to literary circles across Europe and established commercial links with the major book-producing centres. For most of his lifetime, Louis was the only Dutch publisher and bookseller with such international reach. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1890]

The first work published as his own venture was an edition of Eutropius (1592), but regular publication began only after 1594, the year he became a citizen of Leyden. In 1595, he first used the trade-mark of an eagle grasping seven darts in its claw, the emblem of the Dutch Republic, with the motto Concordia res parvae crescunt (Small things grow through harmony). His name appeared for the first time that year in the list of publishers at the Frankfort Fair. From 1602 he made regular annual journeys to Paris, where foreign dealers were strictly regulated — they could visit only once a year, stay no longer than three weeks, and trade only with licensed members of the Booksellers’ Guild. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1894] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3571]

Death and Legacy

Louis Elzevir died in early February 1617, shortly after a fire in the university buildings (November 1616) had led to the suspension of his son Matthias as vice-beadle. The fire investigation blamed the beadles’ negligence, and the stress may have hastened Louis’s death. He was buried beside his wife, who had predeceased him by three years. During his thirty-four-year publishing career (1583—1617), he issued 101 separate works, nearly all in Latin, including editions of Scaliger, Aristotle, Horace, and Heinsius, as well as Grotius’s Mare Liberum (1609). ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#2031] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3584]

The Sons and the Second Generation

Louis left six sons. Two operated bookshops in Utrecht and The Hague in affiliation with the Leyden concern. One served the Dutch East India Company; another became a landscape painter. The eldest, Matthias (1564—?), and the youngest, Bonaventure (1583—1652), joined hands to carry on and greatly extend their father’s business. The famous imprint ex officina Elzeviriana dates from 1617, when the two young men assumed direction. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1898]

Louis II (c. 1566—1621) established a bookshop in The Hague in 1590, in a large hall of the Palace of the States-General. The Hague establishment was primarily a retail bookselling operation with limited publishing activity. After his death, the business passed to Bonaventure, then to Jacob Elzevir (third son of Matthias), who withdrew in 1636. The shop was finally closed in 1661, its stock transferred to Amsterdam or sold at auction. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1937] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3594]

Justus Elzevir (1575—?) became a bookseller in Utrecht. His eldest son, Louis III (1604—1670), would later found the Amsterdam branch.

Isaac Elzevir (1596—1651): The First Printer

Isaac Elzevir, second son of Matthias and grandson of Louis I, acquired a printing office in 1616, making him the first Elzevir to actually operate a press. He married Jaquemine Symons van Swieten in 1616, and her dowry likely enabled the purchase. From 1617 he printed primarily for his uncles Matthias and Bonaventure, and later for Bonaventure and Abraham. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3603]

On February 9, 1620, Isaac was appointed Printer to the University of Leyden, a post the family would hold until 1712. The arrangement required him to hold at least one and a half presses at the disposition of the faculty for printing theses and academic papers, to provide competent correctors for any language required, and to deposit one copy of every work in the university library. His annual stipend was fifty guilders. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1916] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3609]

After the death of the renowned Orientalist Thomas van Erpen (Erpenius) in 1624, Isaac purchased Erpenius’s entire collection of Oriental type punches and matrices, ensuring that this valuable typographic resource remained in Leyden. The university subsequently raised his stipend to one hundred guilders on condition that the Oriental material be kept available for academic use. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1922] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3618]

Isaac’s printer’s mark was an elm tree entwined with a grapevine bearing grapes, with a hermit standing beside it and the motto Non solus (Not alone). He also used a palm tree with the motto Assurgo pressa (Pressed down, I rise), inherited from Erpenius. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3622]

Despite the business’s favourable progress, Isaac retired in December 1625, ostensibly concerned about the long war in Germany. He transferred the office with five presses, one copperplate press, and ten thousand kilograms of type to his uncle Bonaventure and his nephew Abraham for 9,000 guilders, plus 2,000 for the premises. He resigned his university post in February 1626, entered naval service, and by 1648 was operating a brewery in Delft. He died in Cologne on October 8, 1651. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3627]

Bonaventure and Abraham (1625—1652): The Golden Age

Bonaventure (1583—1652), the youngest son of Louis I, and Abraham (1592—1652), son of Matthias, formed the partnership that brought the House of Elzevir to its greatest glory. Bonaventure, named after the scholar Bonaventura Vulcanius, had been sent on business travels by his father from an early age. Abraham had studied at Leyden and trained in typography with his brother Isaac. He married Catharina van Waesberghe in 1621, daughter of the Admiralty printer of Rotterdam, bringing independent means and connections. Bonaventure married Sarah van Keulen, daughter of the scholar Daniel Colonius, in the same year that the partnership acquired Isaac’s press (1625), gaining entry into a wide circle of learned contacts. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3634]

The following twenty-six years (1626—1652) were the most brilliant period of the House. From the outset, the partners aimed to elevate their work above the ordinary, and each year showed progress in type design, ornamentation, or presswork. Within ten years they were producing masterpieces such as their Caesar, Terence, and Pliny of 1635. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3643]

Major Initiatives

In 1625, they began the series of small “Republics” (Republiques), for which they obtained a privilege on May 15, 1626. In 1629, they inaugurated the celebrated series of Latin classics in duodecimo (16mo) with Horace and Ovid, a form modelled on the famous Aldine editions. In 1641, they began a collection of contemporary French drama with Corneille’s Le Cid, and in 1642 a series of French literary classics with the works of Regnier. Alongside these small formats, they continued to issue works in folio and quarto, including Oriental scholarship from the Erpenius types. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1910] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3643]

The Small-Format Revolution

The duodecimo classics were the Elzevirs’ most distinctive contribution to publishing. Well-edited by the finest critics and commentators of the age, carefully printed, and offered at remarkably low prices — typically one florin (about forty-three cents) for a volume of approximately five hundred pages — these handy editions won immediate and overwhelming popularity. A Virgil of 1636, a Pliny of 1640, a Cicero of 1642 — all sold at one florin. Quintus Curtius (1633) sold for sixteen sous, and Sallust, Terence, and Florus for fifteen sous. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1965]

Critics complained that such compact volumes diminished the dignity of great authors. The scholar De Put (Erycius Puteanus) wrote in 1629 that Horace deserved “a dignified form, with a certain majesty of appearance, not to have his thoughts buried in shabby little type.” Yet scholars and students across Europe welcomed the affordable format. Professor Matthew Berneggerus of Strasbourg, in the preface to his translation of Galileo’s System of the Universe (1635), called the Elzevirs “unquestionably the first typographers of the world.” The format became standard across Holland and Belgium and was adopted by several Parisian booksellers. Collectors soon became passionate about acquiring complete sets of Elzevir editions. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1967] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3650]

Typographical Excellence and Christophe Van Dyck

The beauty of the Elzevir editions owes much to Christophe Van Dyck, the punch-cutter who designed and engraved the fonts from which the exquisite Elzevir types were cast. Typographical experts consider these fonts to have surpassed, in the beauty of their proportion, the delicacy of their outline, and the distinctive grace of their general effect, anything previously produced in Europe. Van Dyck’s work was done between approximately 1630 and 1640. The discovery of his identity is due to Alphonse Willems’s research in the Plantin-Moretus archives, where a letter from Daniel Elzevir’s widow offering the type foundry for sale describes the types as “made by Christoffel van Dyck, the best master of his and our time, and consequently the most famous foundry that ever existed.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1957] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3754]

Printed Output

During their twenty-seven-year partnership (1625—1652), Bonaventure and Abraham published 461 separate works. These included a long series of Greek and Latin classics in the new duodecimo form (officially adopted for use in the schools of Holland and West Frisia), theological works (increasingly in Dutch, reflecting the expanding popular readership), legal codes and court decisions, the Oriental series of Erpenius, the sumptuous Academie de l’espee (1628, with 46 engraved plates), and the Latin translation of Galileo’s works (1635). ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#2097] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3643]

Bonaventure managed the commercial and bibliopolic side of the business, maintaining close relations with scholars and customers. Abraham devoted himself with equal care and dedication to the typographical department. The scholarly adviser Daniel Heinsius (1580—1655), professor at Leyden and a universal scholar, was the intellectual soul of the publishing enterprise, writing introductions, dedications, and advising on the selection of works. Heinsius was, however, a contentious figure whose personality alienated a number of scholars from the firm. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#2013] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3677]

Bonaventure died on September 17, 1652; Abraham had predeceased him by a month, on August 14, 1652. The university struck a gold medal in Abraham’s honour, an unusual mark of esteem. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3682]

John and Daniel (1652—1680): The Third Generation

Bonaventure’s son Daniel (1626—1680) and Abraham’s son John (1622—1661) inherited the business. Both had been trained through foreign travel — John to Paris (1638) and Denmark (1641), Daniel to Paris (1645—1648). Their early editions, including the Imitation of Christ and the Psalms (1653), were among the finest typographical achievements of the Elzevir press, described by Willems as “jewels of typography.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1912] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3693]

However, the partnership faced difficulties. Many of the learned friends and advisers who had sustained the previous generation had passed away, the university was in a period of decline, and the relationship with the university administration was strained. Daniel elected to leave the partnership after two and a half years and joined his cousin Louis III in Amsterdam in 1655. John continued alone in Leyden, engaging Karl Gerstekorn as a helper, but his publishing activity diminished. He died on June 8, 1661, aged only 39. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1912] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3701]

Eva van Alphen and the Widow’s Press

John’s widow, Eva van Alphen, assumed control of the Leyden printing office and successfully retained the university contract. She disposed of the publishing stock and the Hague inventory through auctions (1659—1661) and focused primarily on printing rather than publishing. The quality of the presswork remained high during her tenure. Under her management (1662—1681), 775 university theses were printed. After her death in 1681, the business passed to her son Abraham II. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1912] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3781]

The Amsterdam House: Louis III and Daniel (1638—1680)

Louis III (1604—1670), grandson of the founder (son of Justus of Utrecht), established the Amsterdam branch. He had studied in Leyden, living with his uncle Matthias, and had prepared himself through extensive travels across Europe. At age 33, seeing no prospect of an independent role in the Leyden business and unwilling to compete with it, he chose Amsterdam as his field of activity. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1939] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3711]

He opened a bookshop in 1638 and added a printing office in 1640. Louis III proved to be a publisher of bold independent judgment. He cultivated relations with Hugo Grotius in Paris, published all the works of René Descartes from 1642 onward (despite fierce opposition from Dutch Calvinist theologians who made it taboo even to mention Descartes’s name in Leyden), and issued the writings of the French Jansenists. He attracted scholars whom the contentious Daniel Heinsius had alienated from the Leyden firm. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#2019] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3716]

Between 1640 and 1645, the Amsterdam press issued 219 publications. The partnership with Daniel (from May 1, 1655) brought together the two ablest members of the family. During their nine years of collaboration, they produced about 150 works, including the magnificent Corpus Juris (1663, folio) and begun work on the great French Bible of Desmarets. Louis retired in 1664, leaving the business to Daniel, and died in 1670 from complications of a broken leg. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1939] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3720]

Their printer’s mark was Minerva with the aegis, an olive branch, and an owl, with the motto Ne extra oleas (Not beyond the olive trees), a reference to the ancient Greek custom of marking the finish line of a race with olive trees — meaning “keep within proper bounds; do not overshoot the mark.” The signboard of the Amsterdam premises, on the Water quay, was “In the Elm Tree.” ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3724]

Daniel Elzevir’s Later Years

After Louis’s retirement, Daniel ably managed the Amsterdam house alone through difficult times. The war with England (1665—1667) severely disrupted trade. Daniel engaged Jakob Zetter for the bookselling department and, in 1669, brought in Heinrich Wetstein (1649—?), who later became a noted Dutch typographer. Wetstein transformed the bookshop from a purely publishing and commission operation into a full retail and second-hand stock. In 1674, Daniel issued a comprehensive catalogue of over 20,000 works. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#2023] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3741]

The war with France (1672—1678) and the invasion of Holland brought almost all business to a halt. Daniel used the enforced idleness to prepare the great classified catalogue. Despite the difficult times, he published over 90 new works between 1675 and 1680. His correspondents included Nicholas Thoynard, who proposed an early form of stereotype printing to him in 1680 (Daniel dismissed it as “absolutely impossible”). ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#2023]

Daniel died on October 13, 1680, of a recurrent fever. John Locke wrote, “The death of Elzevir is a public misfortune.” With Daniel’s death, the history of the House of Elzevir as a major force in European publishing came to a close. The estate, after liquidation, realised about a quarter of a million marks in modern value. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#2027] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3749]

The End of the Leyden House (1681—1712)

Abraham II (1653—1712), son of John and Eva van Alphen, inherited the Leyden printing office in 1681. He had studied at Leyden, earned a doctorate (1679), and became a city magistrate (schepen) in 1695. Under his mismanagement, the press deteriorated completely. Complaints from faculty about the quality of thesis printing multiplied. His output was largely limited to university theses (1,899 between 1681 and 1712). Upon his death on July 30, 1712, the entire business was sold for less than 2,000 guilders — a fraction of its former value. The plant and material were auctioned in 1713 for the benefit of creditors and Abraham’s daughter. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1912] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3783]

The Leyden printing office had served the university for ninety-one years (1621—1712). The Elzevir type foundry eventually passed through Joseph Athias, Johann Roman, and Ploos van Amstel before being acquired by the Enschedé family of Haarlem, who preserved the matrices in their celebrated type specimen. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3762]

Foreign Connections and Unauthorised Publications

A distinctive feature of the Elzevir organisation was its extensive network of foreign agencies, initiated by the first Louis and maintained by two subsequent generations. The firm maintained depots in Frankfort (from 1595), Paris (from 1602), Copenhagen (a branch house established in 1632), and agencies in Italy and England. They were the primary suppliers of foreign literature to Germany for decades. In Copenhagen, a separate catalogue was printed in 1642. Daniel Elzevir visited Sweden in 1650 and received a flattering proposal from Queen Christina to establish a printing concern in Stockholm, which he declined. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1947] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md#3668]

With England, the Elzevirs had important relations both as booksellers and as printers of English authors who sought the superior quality of Elzevir typography, which could not then be equalled in England. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1945]

The Elzevirs were also known for issuing unauthorised editions (what would now be called pirated editions) under fictitious imprints. For sensitive political or religious works, they used bogus place names: Lugduni (Lyons) instead of Lugduni Batavorum (Leyden), and names such as Jouxte, Jean Sambix, and Nic Schouten (Cologne). Among the anonymously circulated works were Saumaise’s Defensio Regia, Milton’s Defensio Populi Anglicani, and Selden’s Mare Clausum. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1963]

Scholarly and Cultural Impact

The Elzevir publishing programme was distinguished by the range and quality of its authors. The Leyden house published the works of Scaliger, Grotius, Heinsius, Salmasius, and Galileo. The Amsterdam house published Descartes (the first complete edition of his works in 1643), Velthuysen, Vossius, Balzac, Corneille, and Moliere. Their editions of the Latin classics — Virgil, Horace, Cicero, Pliny, Caesar, Terence, Ovid, Livy, Sallust, and others in the compact duodecimo format — became the standard texts for scholars and students across Europe and remained benchmarks of typographical excellence for centuries. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1965]

The Elzevir achievement was to democratise access to classical and contemporary literature through a combination of editorial care, typographic artistry, and commercial shrewdness. As Balzac wrote to the firm in 1651: “For some architects reputation has come through the construction of pyramids and colossi, while others have gained no less fame through the art put into rings and seals… The excellence of work depends not upon its extent but upon its appropriateness and perfection.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md#1975]
