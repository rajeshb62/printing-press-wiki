Peter Schöffer

Peter Schöffer

Overview

Peter Schöffer (c. 1425 — 1503), also spelled Schoeffer, was a German scribe, type designer, punchcutter, printer, and publisher who served as Gutenberg’s most skilled assistant, later became Johann Fust’s partner and son-in-law, and eventually sole proprietor of the most important printing house in Mainz in the late fifteenth century. He designed the type for the 1457 Mainz Psalter (the first dated printed book), introduced the first printer’s mark, and made significant technical innovations in punchcutting, multi-color printing, and typographic spacing. Though he and Fust systematically downplayed Gutenberg’s role in the invention of printing, Schöffer was a master craftsman and shrewd businessman who kept the Fust-Schöffer press productive for nearly fifty years.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Early Life and Paris Years

Schöffer was born in Gernsheim, a village on the Rhine south of Mainz. The exact year is uncertain; some authorities place it around 1425, others closer to 1430. His father died when he was young, and he was taken under the protection of Johann Fust, a wealthy Mainz moneylender and goldsmith who effectively adopted him. Fust paid for Schöffer’s education at the University of Erfurt (probably Gutenberg’s alma mater as well) and then sent him to the University of Paris.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

In Paris, Schöffer worked as a manuscript copyist. A colophon in an old manuscript book records: “This book was completed by me, Peter, of Gernszheym, or of Mentz, during the year 1449, in the most glorious University of Paris.”^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] This training in calligraphy and manuscript production gave him the eye for letterforms that would later distinguish his printed work. It is uncertain whether he was also trained as an illuminator, but he was certainly qualified to read and correct proofs of Latin texts.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Work with Gutenberg

Around 1452, Fust brought Schöffer back to Mainz and placed him in Gutenberg’s workshop. Schöffer was perfectly suited for the role: his calligraphic training, engraving skills, and Latin literacy made him valuable both as a type designer and as a proofreader. He may have assisted in punchcutting — engraving steel punches used to strike matrices for typefounding — and in designing the textura types used in the 42-line Bible. Some traditions, preserved in later claims by Schöffer and his descendants, assert that he was the first to perfect the method of cutting steel punches and striking matrices, but this is almost certainly an exaggeration: Gutenberg’s types, cast well before Schöffer’s known arrival, already show the use of the punch-matrix system. Nevertheless, Schöffer did refine and systematize the technique.^[raw/articles/pearson-gutenberg-and-art-of-printing.md]^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

One account says Schöffer privately cut matrices for the entire alphabet and cast letters from them, presenting the result to Gutenberg and Fust as a demonstration of his skill — a story preserved in fictionalized form by Pearson but reflecting a genuine tradition of his technical competence.^[raw/articles/pearson-gutenberg-and-art-of-printing.md]

The Lawsuit of 1455 and Switch to Fust

The Helmasperger Notarial Instrument of November 1455 records the lawsuit in which Fust demanded repayment of 2,020 guilders from Gutenberg. Schöffer was present at the hearing in the refectory of the Barefoot Friars’ convent — not as one of Gutenberg’s designated witnesses, but as an independent observer. During the months the case took to conclude, Fust had quietly recruited Schöffer, promising him control of the workshop if the judgment went against Gutenberg.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Schöffer would not have needed much persuasion: he was closer to Fust than to Gutenberg, having been raised and educated by Fust. Talented, ambitious, and with a streak of pragmatism, he neatly bridged the gap between Gutenberg’s artistic obsessions and Fust’s business opportunism. After the judgment stripped Gutenberg of his printing office and types, Schöffer and Fust took over the Humbrechthof workshop.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The Mainz Psalter of 1457

The first major work of the new partnership was the Mainz Psalter, completed on 14 August 1457. It is a landmark in printing history on multiple counts:

  • First dated printed book — it carries the first printed colophon with a specific date.
  • First printer’s imprint — the colophon announces the book was “made by the masterly invention of printing and also of type-making, without the writing of a pen, through the industry of Johan Fust, citizen of Mentz, and Peter Schoeffer of Gernszheim.”
  • First printer’s mark — surviving copies (notably the Vienna copy) have the Fust-Schöffer device, two shields hanging from a twig, the earliest known printer’s emblem in the history of typography.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]
  • Multi-color printing — the Psalter’s 175 leaves contain two-color printed initials in red and blue, achieved through careful separate inking of text and ornament blocks, with remarkably accurate register.
  • Elaborate decorative initials — the large initial B of Beatus vir features intricate filigree work in metal as fine as mesh, with images incised within the letterform itself (a dog hunting a bird in the vertical stroke).^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Though the Psalter was almost certainly designed under Gutenberg’s direction before his expulsion, the execution and completion were the work of Fust and Schöffer. The colophon pointedly omits Gutenberg’s name — the first move in a deliberate campaign to rewrite the history of the invention.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

De Vinne notes that the presswork of the Psalter is not superior to the 42-line Bible and is sometimes rougher, with uneven inking and typographical errors. The multi-color initials, he argues, were likely painted onto separate blocks with brushes rather than printed in the modern sense.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The Fust-Schöffer Partnership (1457—1466)

After the Psalter, the firm produced a series of notable works:

  • Rationale Durandi (1459) — a guide to liturgy by Guillaume Durand, the first book in which Schöffer identifies himself as “clerk of the diocese of Mentz.” It became an astonishing success, with over forty editions following.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]
  • Constitutions of Pope Clement V (1460) — with commentaries by Bishop John Andrew, a complex layout of nested text and notes.
  • Bible of 1462 — a two-volume folio in the same tradition as Gutenberg’s Bible.
  • Papal bulls and proclamations during the Mainz civil war (1461—1462), printed for both sides — Fust and Schöffer took contracts from Archbishop Adolf von Nassau, while Gutenberg (backed by Konrad Humery) printed for Diether von Isenburg.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The sack of Mainz on 28 October 1462 interrupted printing throughout the city. Fust’s house and materials were reportedly burned, though the punches and matrices survived. No books bearing the Fust-Schöffer imprint appeared between 1462 and 1464. The dispersal of their workmen during this period carried the knowledge of printing to Cologne, Basel, Augsburg, Nuremberg, and beyond.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Technical Innovations

Schöffer introduced several genuine improvements to the printer’s craft:

  • Leading — in the 1465 edition of Cicero’s De Officiis, he inserted perceptible leads (about one-tenth of an inch thick) between lines of type, the first known use of leading to improve readability.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]
  • Greek type — the same Cicero edition printed Greek characters (crudely engraved on wood) for the first time in a printed book.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]
  • Punchcutting refinement — Schöffer systematized the method of cutting steel punches and striking brass matrices, giving typefaces a finish and uniformity that earlier hand-cut or soft-metal methods could not achieve.^[raw/articles/pearson-gutenberg-and-art-of-printing.md]
  • Multi-color registration — the Psalter initials, however achieved, demonstrated a method of registering two colors on the same printed sheet that was not surpassed for decades.

De Vinne, however, is skeptical of Schöffer’s reputation as a typefounder: his types were fewer in number and of inferior design compared to Gutenberg’s, and he never again matched the quality of the Psalter initials after Gutenberg’s departure.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Sole Proprietorship (1466—1503)

Johann Fust died of plague during a sales trip to Paris in 1466, and was buried at the Church of Saint Victor. Schöffer inherited the business and married Fust’s daughter, Christina, consolidating his control. He continued at the head of the firm for nearly forty more years, becoming the first truly international bookseller-printer.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

He established sales agencies in Frankfurt (where he became a freeman of the city) and Paris, supplying missals and liturgical works across Central Europe. He also acted as a bookseller for other printers’ output. In 1474, Louis XI of France intervened personally to protect Schöffer’s Paris stock from seizure by the licensed book-dealers’ guild, a sign of Schöffer’s high standing at court.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Schöffer bound his workmen by oath to secrecy about the technical processes — an attempt to preserve a monopoly that was ultimately broken by the dispersal of printers after the 1462 sack of Mainz. His later output declined in quantity: only six books appeared from his press between 1490 and 1502. His last publication was a fourth edition of the Psalter (1502), the book with which his typographical career began.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

In his later years, Schöffer served as a judge in Mainz, a civic honor that reflects his status as one of the city’s leading citizens. He died in 1503, rich, respected, and eminent. His sons Johann Schöffer and Peter Schöffer the Younger carried on the family printing business into the Reformation era.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Legacy and Controversy

Schöffer’s reputation is entangled with the long-running controversy over who invented printing. Beginning with the colophon of the 1457 Psalter, and amplified by the statements of his son Johann and of the historian Johannes Trithemius (who claimed to have heard the story from Schöffer himself), the Fust-Schöffer family propagated the claim that Fust was the true inventor and Schöffer the man who perfected the art. Trithemius wrote:

“They discovered a method of founding the forms of all the letters of the Latin alphabet, which they called matrices, from which they again founded types, either in tin or in brass… Before Schoeffer’s name is mentioned, it is said that ‘they’ [Fust and Gutenberg] discovered a method of making matrices.”^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

De Vinne dismisses these claims as self-serving: Schöffer’s types show no evidence of the inventive genius he claimed, and his boasts were not published until after Gutenberg and Fust were both dead and could not contradict him. Nevertheless, Schöffer’s genuine contributions — the Psalter design, the refinement of punchcutting, the introduction of leading, the first printer’s mark — constitute a real, if secondary, chapter in the invention of printing.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Significance

Schöffer occupies a unique position in early printing history: he was Gutenberg’s apprentice and successor, the bridge between the inventor’s genius and the commercial viability of the press. Where Gutenberg was a visionary who died in debt, Schöffer was a pragmatist who built a lasting business. He ensured that the books the Church needed — missals, psalters, liturgical guides — were produced in quantity and quality. His introduction of the printer’s mark and colophon established conventions that printers still follow today. His sons and their descendants carried the Schöffer name in printing for another century, though Mainz itself never regained its primacy as a center of the new art.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]
