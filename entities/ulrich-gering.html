Overview

Ulrich Gering (d. 23 August 1510) was a German printer from Constance (Konstanz) who, together with Michael Friburger and Martin Crantz, established the first printing press in France at the Sorbonne in Paris in 1470. Invited by the Sorbonne professors Guillaume Fichet and Johann Heynlin (also known as Johann von Stein), Gering and his partners printed the first book produced on French soil — the Letters of Gasparino of Bergamo — in a Roman type modelled on the Venetian work of Nicolas Jenson. Gering continued printing in Paris for four decades, eventually adopting Gothic type for a wider commercial market and amassing a fortune that he bequeathed to the university at his death. His press laid the foundation for the Paris book trade, which by 1500 had grown to sixty-six printing offices. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Origins and the Sorbonne Invitation

Gering was born in Constance, a city on Lake Constance in the German-speaking lands. According to the Lorck handbook, he learned the art of printing in Mainz itself. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The impetus for bringing printing to Paris came from the Sorbonne. In the late 1460s, two distinguished scholars of the university — Guillaume Fichet, a doctor of the theological faculty, and Johann Heynlin (Johann von Stein, after his birthplace Stein bei Konstanz), known in the records of the Sorbonne as Jean de Lapierre — saw printed books being produced in Germany and resolved to introduce the art to France. ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md] Chevillier, writing in 1694 as Doctor and Librarian of the Sorbonne, calls them “les deux Theologiens de la Societe de Sorbonne Guillaume Fichet & Jean de Lapierre, qui prirent soin de faire venir les Allemands a Paris, pour pratiquer dans l’Universite l’Art de l’Imprimerie.” ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

Fichet wrote to the historian Robert Gaguin describing “a wonderful new method for the production of books” that had been discovered in Germany. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Heynlin, a native of the Constance region, summoned his countryman Ulrich Gering to Paris. Gering brought with him Michael Friburger (from Colmar) and Martin Crantz (from Basel), and the three Germans set up the first printing office in France within the buildings of the Sorbonne itself, around 1469—1470. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Chevillier’s Sorbonne Archive Account

Chevillier, drawing directly on the Sorbonne’s own records, provides the most detailed account of how the invitation was made. He writes that Jean de Lapierre (Heynlin) wrote to Germany to summon the printers, having the contacts and knowledge of his native country to do so. The epigram on the first book confirms this with the words: A tuis Germanis Impressoribus, quos tua Germania in hanc Urbem librarios ascivisti (“From your German printers, whom your Germany brought to this city”). ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

Chevillier speculates that Lapierre may have been from the same town or diocese as Gering, and that this common origin was a powerful motive for the printers to come to Paris: “je ne doute point que ce ne fut un puissant motif a ces premiers Imprimeurs pour se resoudre a venir a Paris, s’y voyant invitez & attendus par un habile homme de leur Nation, qui parloit le meme langage, qui leur offroit le College de Sorbonne pour y exercer leur Art, ses soins pour leur preparer des Copies, voir les Epreuves, & les aider dans leurs Editions; outre le gain & le profit qui paroissoit leur dans une grande riche Ville comme Paris.” ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

The press was set up in a hall of the Sorbonne at the beginning of 1470, the year in which Jean de Lapierre was Prior of the Sorbonne for the second time (his first priorate was in 1467). Chevillier establishes this from the registers of the Faculty of Theology. ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

The exact year is further confirmed by eight verses the printers placed at the end of their Sallust edition, which refer to the war that Louis XI declared against Charles Duke of Burgundy in 1470. ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

Fichet’s letters to Pope Sixtus IV, Cardinal Bessarion, Cardinal Rolin, and Rene King of Sicily are all dated from the Sorbonne. The letter to Cardinal Rolin is dated “Ex aedibus Sorbonae Idibus Julii” 1470, proving the press was active by mid-1470 at the latest. ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

The First Books and Roman Type

The first book printed in Paris was a collection of the Letters of Gasparino of Bergamo (Gasparini Pergamensis Orthographiae Liber), issued in 1470. It was printed in Latin, using a Roman-form type despite the German origin of the printers. Putnam records the opinion of bibliographer Humphreys that specimens of the beautiful volumes printed in Venice by Nicolas Jenson had been sent to Paris and served as models for the earliest Paris press. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Chevillier’s First List (Sorbonne Editions, 1470-1471)

Chevillier, cataloguing books still preserved in the Sorbonne library in his own time (1694), provides the following list of early Sorbonne imprints, all printed in the same round Roman type from the same matrices ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]:

  1. Gasparini Pergamensis Epistolarum Liber — one volume in 4to (two copies in Sorbonne)
  2. Lucii Annaei Flori de Tota Historia Titi Livii Epitome (Florus’ epitome of Livy)
  3. Caii Crispi Sallustii de Lucii Catilinae Conjuratione Liber and De Bello Jugurthae — one volume in 4to on vellum
  4. Guillelmi Ficheti Alnetani Rhetoricorum Libri Tres (Fichet’s own Rhetoric) — one volume in 4to on vellum
  5. Gasparini Pergamensis Orthographiae pars prima & secunda with Guarini Veronensis Libellus de Diphthongis — one volume in 4to
  6. Epistolae Cynicae (Letters of Phalaris translated by Francesco Aretino, Letters of Brutus with Mithridates’ reply, Letters of Crates the Cynic) — one volume in 4to
  7. Roderici Zamorensis Episcopi Speculum Humanae Vitae — one volume in folio
  8. Bessarionis Cardinalis Epistolae (three letters and two orations, including a translation of Demosthenes’ First Olynthiac) — one volume in 4to
  9. Guillelmi Ficheti Epistolae (to Cardinal Bessarion, Pope Sixtus IV, Rene King of Sicily, Cardinal Rolin, and Guillaume Chartier Bishop of Paris) — one volume in 4to
  10. Laurentii Vallae Elegantiarum Linguae Latinae Libri Sex with preface by Petrus Paulus Senilis and a letter from Jean de Lapierre — one volume in folio
  11. Jacobi Magni Sophologium — one volume in folio

Chevillier notes that this list is not complete — some books have been lost, and there are reasons to believe not everything was preserved. He mentions, for instance, that Johann Saubert catalogued a Quintilian “de l’impression de Paris 1471” at Nuremberg, which would have been printed by Gering if genuine. ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

Typographic Description of the First Editions

Chevillier gives a precise physical description of these first Paris editions:

“Tous ces Livres sont imprimez de memes Lettres, fondues dans les memes Matrices. C’est un Caractere rond, de gros Romain. Comme l’Impreffion ne faisoit que de naistre, & que ces premiers Livres sont comme des essais de l’Art, il se trouve en quelques-unes des Lettres a demi formees, & des mots a moitie imprimez, qu’on a achevez avec la main. Il y a meme quelques Epitres imprimees, dont l’inscription n’est que manuscripte. Il n’y a point de Lettres capitales. Les premieres Lettres des Livres & des Chapitres sont omises, on y a laisse de la place pour y peindre une premiere lettre en or ou en azur. Il y a plusieurs mots abregez. … Le papier n’est pas bien blanc, mais il est fort, & bien colle. L’Encre est d’un beau noir. Ils imprimerent aussi quelques lignes en lettres rouges sur velin.” ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

The books have no titles, no foliation (page numbers), and no signatures (collation marks). Signatures — alphabetical letters at the bottom of pages — were not introduced in Paris until 1476. Titles first appeared in 1477. Catchwords (reclames) were adopted late, around 1520. ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

Fichet’s Preface Letter

Fichet placed at the head of the Gasparino volume a letter addressed to Jean de Lapierre, serving as a preface. In it he thanks Lapierre for “the great advantage he has procured for the study of the Sciences, by means of the Germans he has brought.” The letter includes the key phrase: A tuis Germanis Impressoribus, quos tua Germania in hanc Urbem librarios ascivisti (“From your German printers, whom your Germany brought as librarians/booksellers to this city”). ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

The printers added an eight-verse epigram to Paris itself, including the lines:

Primos ecce Libros, quos haec industria finxit / Francorum in terris, aedibus atque tuis. / Michael, Udalricus, Martinusque Magistri / Hos impresserunt, ac facient alios.

(“Behold the first books, which this industry produced in the lands of the Franks, and in your buildings. Michael, Ulrich, and Martin, the masters, printed these, and will make others.“) ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

Chevillier uses this evidence to refute the claim that Nicolas Jenson was the first to bring printing to France — noting that Jenson’s books were all printed in Venice, not in Paris. ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

Move to Rue Saint-Jacques and the Second Press

Around 1473, after Fichet had left for Rome (called by Pope Sixtus IV at the end of October 1471) and Lapierre was preparing his return to Germany, Gering and his partners moved their press from the Sorbonne to a house on the rue Saint-Jacques at the sign of the Soleil d’or (Sun of Gold). ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

Chevillier’s Second List (Rue Saint-Jacques Editions)

Chevillier provides a second list of books printed at the Soleil d’or, including ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]:

  • Guido de Monte Rocherii, Manipulus Curatorum (folio, completed 1473 — names all three printers)
  • Bartholomaei Pisani, Summa de Casibus Conscientiae (folio — with verses naming Martin, Michael, and Ulrich as “quos genuit Germania, nunc Lutetia pascit”)
  • Guillelmi Durandi, Rationale Divinorum Officiorum (folio, 1475)
  • Roderici Zamorensis, Speculum Vitae Humanae (folio, 1475, reprinted from the Sorbonne edition)
  • Biblia Sacra (folio — the first Bible printed in France, with verses naming the three printers)
  • Gregorii Magni Homiliae (folio, 1475)
  • Leonardi de Utino, Sermones de Sanctis (folio, 1476)
  • Angeli de Aretio, Tractatus de Criminibus (folio, 1476)
  • Legenda Aurea Jacobi Januensis (folio)
  • Francisci de Platea, Tractatus de Usuris (folio, 1476)
  • Jacobi Magni, Sophologium (folio, 1477)
  • Leonardi de Utino, Sermones Quadragesimales (folio, 1477, near Saint-Benoit)
  • Aristotelis Opera quaedam Logica (folio, printed by Gering alone)
  • Guidonis de Monte-Rocherii, Manipulus Curatorum (4to, 1478, Gering alone)
  • Alberti Eyb, Margarita Poetica (folio, 1478, Gering alone)
  • Joannis Nider, Consolatorium Timoratae Conscientiae (4to, 1478, Gering alone)
  • And many others through the 1480s, including works by Nicolas de Lyra, Gregory the Great, and Hugues Cardinal

New Types for the Second Press

Chevillier notes that the books of this second list are not in the same type as the first. “Ces Editions de la rue S. Jacques, sont toutes nouvelles Lettres, fondues dans de nouvelles Matrices. On ne voit plus dans leurs autres Impressions ces Caracteres de Sorbonne, qui ont este les premiers essais de l’imprimerie de Paris: & il semble qu’en retirant leurs Presses de cette Maison, ils rompirent tous les instruments qui n’avoient servi qu’a faire voir le nouvel Art dans son enfance.” ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

He identifies three or four kinds of characters in these editions. Some books (the 1473 Manipulus Curatorum, Bartholomew of Pisa, Durand) use a type resembling common handwriting of the time, “comme une escriture a la main” — not Gothic, similar to the Mainz impressions of Peter Schoeffer. Others (the Bible, the Zamora, the Sophologium, the 1475 Gregory) use a larger version of the same type. Still others (the Eyb, the 1478 Guido, the Aristotle, the Nider) use a large, clear, well-formed Roman letter. Chevillier judges these later Roman types to be as beautiful as the best Venetian work of John and Wendelin of Speyer, Nicolas Jenson, John of Cologne, and others. ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

“De sorte que c’est une louange due avec justice a Ulric Gering, qu’il est non-seulement le premier Imprimeur de Paris & de France, mais qu’il est celui qui a porte cet Art dans un haut degre de perfection en ce Royaume, & qui a imprime de la plus belle Lettre qui ait paru en son temps.” ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

The first Bible printed in France was Gering’s edition (c. 1476), as Chevillier notes: “Ulric imprima la sainte Bible l’annee 1475. C’est la premiere Bible imprimee en France.” ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

Naturalisation and the Partners’ Departure

In 1474, following the example of Peter Schoeffer’s agent (whose stock had been seized by the Paris guild on the grounds of alien ownership), Gering applied for and received letters of naturalisation from King Louis XI, securing legal protection for his business. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

In 1477, after the printing of Leonard of Udine’s Sermones Quadragesimales, Crantz and Friburger abandoned printing and returned to Germany. Chevillier confirms this: “depuis cette annee-la il n’est plus parle d’eux nulle part, au lieu qu’on voit Gering passer le reste de ses jours a Paris, y faire de nouveaux Associez, avec qui il fait beaucoup d’Editions.” ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Move to Gothic Type and Later Career

When Gering moved his printing office from the Sorbonne to the rue Saint-Jacques, he adopted the Gothic (Textura) type that was more popular with the general reading public — the so-called lettres de forme or Allemand (German) letter. This shift from Roman to Gothic marked a strategic turn: the Sorbonne scholars preferred Roman type for humanist and classical texts, but the larger commercial market favoured the familiar Gothic script. Smaller type used for schoolbooks was called lettres de somme, decorative initial capitals were lettres cadeaux, and the rounded chapter initials were lettres tourneurs. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Chevillier on Gering’s Gothic Period (Part II)

Chevillier devotes the entire second part of his dissertation to the question of Gothic printing. He states that Gering “commenca l’Imprimerie a Paris, il ne donna que de bons Caracteres, & tint ferme long-tems contre le torrent des autres Imprimeries, qui introduisoient la Lettre Gothique. Mais enfin il se laissa entrainer lui-meme.” ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

Gering was not the first to use Gothic in Paris — Chevillier notes that a Gregory of Rimini on the Sentences was printed in Gothic as early as 1482, and Louis Martineau printed in Gothic in 1484. The third list of Gering’s books shows a mixture: the Holkot, the Alberc, Guy Juvenal, St. Gregory on the Canticles, and Nicolas Perot are in Roman letters; the rest — except Virgil — are in Gothic letters, which Gering employed for books used in Church service and others. ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

Chevillier’s Third List includes Gering’s works with his partner Berthold Rembolt at the Soleil d’or, rue de Sorbonne (after 1489), including ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]:

  • Rob. Holcot, Librum Sapientiae (4to, 1489)
  • Albertus Magnus, De arte vivendi (4to, 1491)
  • Guidonis Juvenalis, Interpretatio in Elegantias Vallae (4to, 1494)
  • Psalterium ad usum Parisiensem (4to, 1494, vellum)
  • S. Gregorii, Moralia in Job (folio, 1495)
  • Diurnale Ecclesiae Parisiensis (8vo, 1495)
  • Breviarium Ecclesiae Cameracensis (8vo, 1497)
  • Missale ad usum Ecclesiae Parisiensis (folio, 1497, vellum)
  • P. Virgilii Maronis Opera (folio, 1498, corrected by Paulus Malleolus)
  • S. Gregorii, Expositio super Cantica (4to, 1498)
  • Horae B. Mariae Virginis (8vo, 1498)
  • S. Augustini Sermones (folio, no date)
  • Guillelmi Peraldi, Sermones (8vo, 1498)
  • Venerabilis Bedae, Expositio in Epistolas Pauli (folio, 1499)

Chevillier describes the Corpus Juris Canonici with gloss — the largest work Gering printed — as having been printed in red and black, and notes that a later edition of it perished because it was Gothic (“a peri parce qu’il etoit Gothique”). ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

De Vinne remarks that Gering, like Jenson, found it expedient to cut and cast types in the Round Gothic fashion, because “the Roman character was most admired by scholars” but the market demanded Gothic. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Later in his career, Gering took on Berthold Remboldt as a partner. Remboldt’s edition of Virgil was so carefully corrected by scholars that it was considered error-free. Gering’s masterpiece was the Corpus juris canonici, a monumental work printed in five columns, using multiple typefaces in red and black. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Competition and Rival Presses

As early as 1473, two of Gering’s former employees — Peter Keyser (Caesaris) and John Stol — set up a rival printing office in Paris, having served a three-year apprenticeship with Gering. This competition drove down book prices. Gering responded by reprinting their titles as soon as he could procure copies, and each house boasted of the superior accuracy of its editions. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Chevillier’s Account of Caesaris and Stol

Chevillier provides a much more detailed account of these rival printers. They were “deux Allemands qui etudioient dans l’Universite” — two Germans studying at the University. Caesaris had taken the degree of Master of Arts. They were instructed by Gering and established the second printing press in Paris in 1473. ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

Their first work was the Manipulus Curatorum of Guy de Montrocher (1473), which the University later cited as evidence that the earliest printers were Masters of Arts. Their other works included:

  • Speculum Vitae Humanae of Zamora (folio, identified by colophon: Presserunt Petrus Caesaris, simulgue Johannes)
  • A collection of opuscules (includes Tractatus de Origine Nobilium, Poggio’s letter on the infelicity of princes, Aeneas Silvius on the miseries of courtiers, Seneca on remedies of fortune)
  • Seneca’s Epistles (4to, 1473)
  • Valerius Maximus (folio, 1475)
  • The beautiful Dialogue of Ockham (2 vols. folio, 1474) — which Naude had attributed to Gering but Chevillier, comparing the types, convincingly assigns to Caesaris and Stol

Chevillier notes that all these books are in Roman letters, “mais beaucoup moins belles que celles des premieres Editions d’Ulric Gering rapportees cy-dessus dans la premiere Liste.” ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

Caesaris lodged on the rue Saint-Jacques at the sign of the Swan (le Cygne), in a house belonging to the Sorbonne doctors who granted him a life lease in 1473. He held it until 1505. ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

Despite this rivalry, the number of presses in Paris grew rapidly. By the end of the fifteenth century, there were sixty-six master printers in Paris. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Death and Legacy

Ulrich Gering died on 23 August 1510 in the house of the Sun of Gold, rue de Sorbonne, after working at printing in Paris for forty years. ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

Chevillier records that he was assisted in his final illness by the doctors of the Sorbonne, with whom he had lived for more than thirteen years “en qualite d’Hote & de Bienfaicteur.” ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

His burial place was disputed: du Boulay and Mentel claimed he was buried in the Chapel of the College of Montaigu, pointing to a painting of Gering among the benefactors there. But Chevillier argues this painting — showing Gering among more than twenty benefactors, dated 1546 — proves only that he was a benefactor, not that he was buried there. Chevillier suggests he was more likely buried at Saint-Cosme, his parish church. The testamentary executor asked for three brass tablets recording Gering’s legacy: one in the Sorbonne chapel (still there in Chevillier’s time), one in the Church of the Mathurins (where the University assembled), and one in Saint-Cosme (where he was probably buried). ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

The Society of the Sorbonne kept his memory annually with an Office of the Dead: Vespers, Matins with nine psalms and nine lessons, Lauds, a High Mass, and two low Masses. The Necrologe read: “Obiitus Ulrici Gering, Civis ac Incolae Parisiensis, insignis Benefactoris hujus Domus.” ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

By his death he had amassed a considerable fortune, the bulk of which he willed to the Sorbonne — a final gesture of loyalty to the institution that had first brought him to Paris four decades earlier. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Gering’s historical significance rests on three foundations:

  1. Pioneer of French printing: He established the first press in France, a full generation before printing was widely adopted in the country.
  2. University press model: His press operated under the direct auspices of the Sorbonne, producing scholarly texts selected by university authorities — an early model of the academic press.
  3. Typographic transition: His career traced the arc from Roman to Gothic and back, reflecting the tension between humanist scholarly taste and commercial market reality that would define European printing for centuries.

Though the first book printed in the French language (Les Grandes Chroniques de France, 1476) was issued by Pasquier Bonhomme rather than Gering, the foundation that Gering laid in the rue Saint-Jacques and the Sorbonne enabled Paris to become, within a generation, one of the largest book-trade centres in Europe. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]
