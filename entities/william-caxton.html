Overview

William Caxton (c. 1422 — 1491) was the first English printer and the man who introduced the printing press to England. Born in Kent, he spent most of his adult life in Bruges as a successful cloth merchant and Governor of the English Nation, before taking up printing late in life. He learned the art in Cologne (1471—1472), set up a press in Bruges with Colard Mansion (1473—1474) where he printed the first books in the English language, and in 1476 moved his press to Westminster, England, where he established it at the Sign of the Red Pale in the Almonry of Westminster Abbey. Over the following fifteen years he printed about 100 titles, many of them translations he made himself, and played a crucial role in the development and standardisation of English as a literary language. He died in 1491, and his business was taken over by his successor, Wynkyn de Worde.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Early Life and Apprenticeship

Caxton was born in the Weald of Kent, probably around 1422. The exact year is uncertain; the conventional range of 1421—1424 is inferred from the fact that he was apprenticed in 1438 to Robert Large, a wealthy mercer of London and a future Lord Mayor. Apprenticeships typically lasted seven years and ended at age 24, which suggests a birth year of about 1421—1422.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Robert Large was one of the most eminent merchants of the City of London, and admission to his household was, under the conditions of the time, evidence that Caxton came from a family of good standing. William Blades, the great Caxton bibliographer, believed Caxton to be a descendant of the Caunstons, who held the manor of Caunston in Kent. The young Caxton received the education of a merchant’s apprentice — practical training in accounting, correspondence, and the cloth trade, rather than the university learning he would later acquire on his own.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Bruges and the English Nation

In 1441, after Robert Large’s death, Caxton was sent to Bruges, then the commercial capital of northern Europe and the seat of the Burgundian court. There he became a member of the “English Nation” — the term used for the association of English merchants residing in Bruges, who controlled the wool and cloth trade between England and the Continent. He rose steadily in this mercantile community and was eventually elected Governor of the English Nation in Bruges, a position roughly equivalent to a consul, responsible for representing and regulating English commercial interests in the city.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

A colony of Flemish wool-manufacturers had long been established in the Weald of Kent, so Caxton’s early environment had given him some familiarity with Flemish language and customs before his departure — an advantage that would serve him well throughout his long residence abroad. He lived in Bruges for about thirty-three years, during which time the city rivalled Paris as the literary capital of northern Europe. The Burgundian court under Philip the Good and later Charles the Bold was a great patron of the arts, and the ducal library contained nearly three thousand magnificent illuminated manuscripts.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The Burgundian Court and the Turn to Literature

After the marriage of Charles the Bold, Duke of Burgundy, to Margaret of York (sister of King Edward IV of England) in 1468, Caxton’s position brought him into close contact with the Burgundian court. Margaret took a keen personal interest in Caxton and induced him to give up his mercantile career and enter her service as a kind of literary adviser and secretary. He received a yearly salary and devoted himself to literary pursuits.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

It was in 1469, in this new role, that Caxton began the translation of Le Recueil des Histoires de Troye (The Collection of the Histories of Troy), a popular romance compiled by Raoul Lefevre, chaplain to Philip the Good. The translation was a labour of love that he undertook in his spare time, writing by candlelight after his official duties. When the completed translation found favour at court — Margaret herself is said to have encouraged and corrected it — Caxton conceived the idea of multiplying copies by the new mechanical art of printing.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Learning to Print in Cologne (1471—1472)

The critical question of where Caxton learned the art of printing was long debated. Earlier German historians maintained that he was trained in Cologne, and the evidence now strongly supports this view. In 1471, Caxton was in Cologne, where Ulrich Zell (one of Peter Schoeffer’s proteges) had set up a press in 1466. Caxton contracted with one of Zell’s assistants, Johann Veldener, “at his great charge and dispense” either as a teacher, a printer for his book, or both. The expense was considerable, but Caxton, now in his early fifties, was determined to bring the art to England.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

It is possible that a book known as the Cologne Chronicle (1499), which records that “the art of printing was first invented in Mainz, by Johann Gutenberg,” also notes that Caxton was in Cologne learning the trade. At any rate, the types Caxton later used show clear affinities with those of the Cologne school.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Printing in Bruges with Colard Mansion (1473—1474)

Returning to Bruges from Cologne, Caxton set up a press in collaboration with Colard Mansion, a skilled scribe and illuminator who had been active in Bruges since at least 1450. Mansion had received commissions from Duke Philip for illuminated manuscripts and was a member of the Guild of St. John the Evangelist, the book-makers’ guild. Together, they produced the first six books from Caxton’s press, which are conventionally listed as:^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

  • The Recuyell of the Historyes of Troye (Caxton’s own English translation, c. 1473—1474) — the first book ever printed in the English language, 351 folio pages.
  • Le Recueil des Histoires de Troye (the French original, printed shortly after).
  • The Game and Playe of Chesse — a moral treatise on chess, translated by Caxton, with a prologue.
  • Les Meditacions sur les Sept Pseaulmes Penitenceaux.
  • Les Fais et Processus du Chevalier Jason.
  • Les Quatre Dernieres Choses Advenir.

The Recuyell in particular was a landmark: it established that a market existed for printed books in English, and the copies sent across the Channel sold well enough to encourage Caxton to make the move to England. Mr. Blades was of the opinion that these Bruges books, while nominally Caxton’s productions, were actually printed by Mansion with Caxton acting as publisher and translator — but in learning to supervise the work, Caxton gained sufficient technical knowledge to carry on a printing office of his own.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Move to Westminster (1476)

In 1476, after an absence of about thirty-five years, Caxton returned to England, bringing with him a printing press, type, and equipment. He established his workshop in the precincts of Westminster Abbey — not within the church itself, but in the Almonry, a space within the abbey enclosure where alms were distributed to the poor. The tenement he occupied was called the “Sign of the Red Pale” (the Rood Pale, a reference to a red-painted post or paling). In the same enclosure stood the almshouses built by Lady Margaret Beaufort, mother of the future Henry VII. Caxton described his address as “in thabbey of Westmynstre” or “at the reed pale,” and his proximity to the court gave him access to the nobility and clergy who became his patrons and customers.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The First Printed Book in England (1477)

The first book printed by Caxton in England that carries a complete imprint is The Dictes or Sayengis of the Philosophres (November 1477), a translation by Anthony Woodville, Earl Rivers (the brother-in-law of Edward IV), of a French compilation of sayings from the philosophers. This is generally regarded as the first book printed on English soil. It is a folio volume, printed in Caxton’s Type 2, and its colophon reads: “Emprynted by me William Caxton at Westmestre the xviii day of Novembre the yere of our lord M.CCCC.lxxvii.”^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

By comparison, the first printed book in Italy appeared in 1464 (a Donatus), the first in France in 1469 (the Letters of Kaspar von Bergamo), and the first in Germany proper (after Gutenberg’s Bible) in various cities from the 1450s onward. England was a latecomer, but Caxton’s press quickly became productive.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Caxton’s Output and Major Works

Over the fifteen years of his Westminster press (1476—1491), Caxton produced approximately 94 to 100 separate editions. Blades listed 98 identified works, plus 8—10 doubtful ones, including 6 or 7 printed in Bruges and 3 that appeared after Caxton’s death, probably completed by his successor Wynkyn de Worde. Of these, at least 33 survive as unique copies, often in imperfect condition, suggesting that the actual number of editions was substantially larger.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The most notable of Caxton’s publications include:

  • Chaucer’s Canterbury Tales (1477, first edition) — This was the first printed edition of Chaucer’s masterpiece, a folio of 742 pages. Caxton printed it in two editions; the second, of 622 pages, corrected from a superior manuscript. Chaucer’s works dominate Caxton’s list: he also printed The Parliament of Fowls, Anelida and Arcite, Boethius de Consolacione Philosophiae (translated by Chaucer), The Complaint of Chaucer to His Purse, and others. Caxton’s Chaucer editions were instrumental in establishing Chaucer’s canonical status.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

  • Malory’s Le Morte d’Arthur (1485) — Caxton printed Sir Thomas Malory’s great prose romance of King Arthur, the only manuscript source for the work that survives. Caxton divided Malory’s narrative into twenty-one books and added his own preface, explaining that he had printed the book “to the entente that noble men may see and lerne the noble actes of chyvalrye.” Without Caxton’s edition, Malory’s work might have been lost entirely.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

  • The Golden Legend (1483) — At 892 pages, this was Caxton’s largest and most ambitious work, a translation of Jacobus de Voragine’s Legenda Aurea with additional lives of English saints. It is profusely illustrated with woodcuts, and although no complete copy survives intact, it is considered Caxton’s masterpiece. The illustrations, however, are crude by continental standards: wood-engraving in England was still in its infancy.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

  • Polychronicon (1482) — Ranulf Higden’s universal history, translated by John of Trevisa, continued by Caxton to the year 1461. A folio of 890 pages.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

  • The Chronicles of England (1480) — Often called “Caxton’s Chronicle,” based on the old Brute chronicle, brought down to the Battle of Towton (1461).^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

  • The Mirrour of the World (1481) — Caxton’s own translation of a French encyclopaedic work, with woodcut illustrations.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

  • Reynard the Fox (1481) — Caxton’s translation from a Flemish version of the popular beast-epic.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Other publications included editions of Lydgate, Gower, Cicero’s De Senectute and De Amicitia (translated by Worcester and Tiptoft), Cato (the first English book with woodcuts — two crude cuts that Blades believed to be the earliest specimens of wood-engraving in England), and numerous service-books (breviaries, missals, and psalters following the Use of Salisbury).^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Caxton as Translator

Caxton was not merely a printer but a prolific translator. He translated many of the works he printed, often adding prologues and epilogues in which he speaks in his own voice — a novelty in the history of the book. These personal remarks are one of the chief sources of information about his life and working methods, and they give the reader a vivid impression of a modest, earnest, and self-deprecating man. He frequently apologises for his “symple” and “rude” English, and laments the rapid change of the language:

“Certaynly our langage now used varyeth ferre from that which was used and spoken when I was borne.”^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

In the prologue to the Recuyell, he describes the labour of translation in affecting terms:

“I have practysed and lerned at my grete charge and dispense to ordeyne this said book in prynte after the maner and forme as ye may here see, and is not wreton with penne and ynke as other bokes ben.”^[raw/articles/pearson-gutenberg-and-art-of-printing.md]

Caxton’s English is, for modern readers, much more accessible than Chaucer’s. His printing fixed a version of the London-Midlands dialect that became the foundation of modern standard English. The choices he made in spelling, vocabulary, and idiom — sometimes conscious, sometimes accidental — were propagated through his hundreds of copies and influenced generations of writers and readers.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Caxton’s Advertisement

Around 1480, Caxton issued what is generally regarded as the first printed advertisement in English. It reads:

“If it plese any man spirituel or temporel to bye ony pyes of two and thre comemoracios of Salisburi ose emprynted after the forme of this present lettre whiche ben and truly correct, late hym come to Westmenester in to the almonesrye at the reed pale and he shall have them good chepe.”^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

This handbill — a single printed sheet — advertises service-books (breviaries or ‘pie’ books) for the Salisbury (Sarum) Use, printed in the typeface shown on the sheet itself.

Typefaces

Caxton used a series of eight distinct typefaces during his career (often catalogued as Type 1 through Type 8), all of them Gothic or Blackletter in design. The earliest — used in the Bruges books — was a Burgundian or Bastard script, a form of cursive Gothic popular in the Burgundian court circle. Over the course of his career in London, his types evolved toward a heavier, more angular textura better suited to the English market. His types were mostly cut by himself or by his assistants, rather than imported from the Continent. They are generally considered crude in comparison to the sophisticated types of Gutenberg, Schoeffer, or the Venetian printers, but they were serviceable and legible.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Lorck’s assessment is blunt: “Die Technik sowohl als die Korrektur seiner Bücher waren hochst mangelhaft und die Holzschnitte sehr untergeordneter Natur” (The technique as well as the proofreading of his books were extremely deficient, and the woodcuts of a very subordinate quality). This was typical of English printing at the time: there was no tradition of fine book-production in England comparable to that of Italy, Germany, or the Low Countries.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Character and Business Methods

Caxton was not, like many continental printers, an enthusiast who loved the art for its own sake. He was a practical English businessman who printed books that he hoped would sell. He did not seek scholarly editions of the classics or impose exacting standards of typographical correctness. His selection of titles — chivalric romances, moral treatises, edifying histories, popular saints’ lives, and devotional works — reflects the taste of the middle-class and courtly audience he served. If the survival rate of his books (only 5—600 copies of his editions survive, most in British collections) is any guide to the rate of consumption, his business judgment was sound: copies were read to pieces.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

At the same time, he was conscious of his historical role. He describes himself with characteristic modesty as “the symple person William Caxton,” and marvels that “the most excellent and renowned art of printing has come into my hands.” His prologues and epilogues reveal a man of genuine piety, loyalty to the crown, and gratitude for the opportunity to serve the commonwealth by disseminating useful knowledge.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]^[raw/articles/pearson-gutenberg-and-art-of-printing.md]

Death and Succession

William Caxton died late in 1491, honoured and respected. The exact date is not recorded, but the evidence from documents and from the dates of his last publications suggests the end of that year. He was buried in the churchyard of St. Margaret’s, Westminster, within the shadow of the Abbey where he had plied his craft. The site is now lost.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

His business was taken over by his successor, Wynkyn de Worde (originally a native of Wortheim in Alsace, who had come to England as Caxton’s assistant). De Worde continued the Westminster press at the Sign of the Sun in Fleet Street, publishing well into the sixteenth century and expanding Caxton’s legacy. Some authorities list three books that appeared after Caxton’s death but were evidently in preparation while he lived, and these are attributed to de Worde’s completion of Caxton’s work.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The 400th anniversary of Caxton’s introduction of printing into England was celebrated in 1877 with great splendour; a Caxton Celebration Exhibition was held in London, and bibliographers like William Blades produced definitive studies of his life and work.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Significance

Caxton’s significance lies not in technical innovation — by continental standards his presswork was mediocre — but in three profound contributions:

  1. The introduction of printing to England — He brought the art across the Channel fifteen years after the first presses were set up in Paris, and within a generation England was part of the European print network.

  2. The printing of English literature — Without Caxton’s editions, many major works of Middle English literature, including Malory’s Morte d’Arthur and multiple works by Chaucer, might have survived in only a handful of manuscripts or been lost entirely. He fixed the canon of English literature at a critical moment.

  3. The standardisation of the English language — Caxton’s choice of the London-Midlands dialect as the medium for his printing had incalculable consequences. English was changing so rapidly that Caxton himself noted how “the language now used varies far from that which was used and spoken when I was born.” His press both fixed the language on the page and accelerated the process of standardisation, laying the foundation for modern written English.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

In the broader narrative of the spread-of-printing across Europe, Caxton represents the penetration of the new art into the Atlantic archipelago. Like the printers who carried the invention from Mainz to Cologne, Basel, Rome, Venice, and Paris, Caxton was a crucial node in the network of knowledge: a businessman who, in his fifties, mastered a new technology and used it to serve the needs of his country. The fact that he was not a German, not a scholar, and not a trained artisan made him an unlikely apostle of the press; but he succeeded precisely because he understood the English market and supplied it with what it wanted.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]
