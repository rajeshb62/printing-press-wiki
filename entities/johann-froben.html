Johann Froben

Johann Froben

Overview

Johann Froben (c. 1460 — October 1527) was a Basel printer, publisher, and scholar who ranks among the most enlightened and enterprising publishers of the sixteenth century. His press was celebrated for exceptional typographic quality — clear Roman typefaces, meticulous proofreading, and clean presswork — and for its close partnership with the great humanist scholar Erasmus of Rotterdam, who lived in Froben’s household for many years. Erasmus called Froben “the Aldus of Germany,” and his press was widely regarded as the northern equivalent of Aldus Manutius’s Aldine Press in Venice. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Early Life and Training

Froben was born in 1460 in Hammelburg, a village in Franconia (present-day Bavaria). He studied at the University of Basel, which had been founded the year before his birth in 1459, and achieved distinction as a scholar in Latin, Greek, and Hebrew. He was trained as a type-setter and press-corrector by the renowned printer Johann Amerbach, one of the leading scholarly printers of the period. Thus equipped, he secured citizenship in Basel in 1490 and began business in his own name as a master printer and publisher in 1491. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The Press in Basel

Froben’s first publication was a Latin Bible in the convenient octavo format — the earlier printed Bibles had all been issued in expensive folios, and this smaller, more affordable edition won immediate popularity. His undertakings were restricted to books in Latin and Greek aimed at scholars. He left the task of printing vernacular works for the common reader to later publishers, believing that the educational influence of the Reformation was needed before a genuine popular demand for reading matter would emerge. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

In 1500, at age forty, Froben married Gertrude, the daughter of the prosperous bookseller Wolfgang Lachner. Lachner supplied capital for the expansion of the printing office, which by 1504 had seven presses in operation. Over the thirty-six years of his business career, Froben printed no fewer than 257 separate works, many of them of considerable scope and importance. His more important publications included the complete works of Saint Jerome (1516, in nine folio volumes), Saint Augustine (completed posthumously in 1529, ten folio volumes), and the first printed edition of the Greek New Testament (1516), edited by Erasmus and later used by Martin Luther for his German translation. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Relationship with Erasmus

The defining relationship of Froben’s career was with Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam. Erasmus was the only contemporary author whose books Froben systematically undertook, and the partnership proved immensely profitable for both. Erasmus’s Encomium Moriæ (The Praise of Folly), first printed by Froben in 1515, sold 1,800 copies in its first six months and went through twenty-seven editions during the author’s lifetime, establishing Erasmus as the first European author to earn a significant income from his pen. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Erasmus lived in Froben’s house in Basel from 1521 until Froben’s death in 1527. Froben paid Erasmus two hundred guldens per year for his services as editor and reviser, and the scholar also received returns from the sale of his own works. The circle of intimates gathered around the two men during this period included the brothers Amerbach, Beatus Rhenanus, Henricus Glareanus, Johannes Oecolampadius, and other leading humanists. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The Erasmus-Aldus-Froben Triangle

Erasmus’s publishing career connected the two greatest scholarly presses of the age. He worked first with Aldus Manutius in Venice (1506-1509), where he produced the expanded Adagia under the intense conditions of the Aldine household. When Erasmus moved north and began working with Froben in 1514, the relationship between the two publishers became a competitive and sometimes delicate triangle. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The two presses divided the market for Erasmus’s works: Aldus continued to produce authorised editions for the Italian and Mediterranean market, while Froben handled northern Europe. Erasmus appears to have encouraged this arrangement, maintaining good relations with both houses. The competition was not entirely amicable: Aldus’s heirs were reportedly unhappy when Froben reprinted the Adagia in 1513, and Erasmus had to mediate. But the arrangement benefited all parties — Erasmus secured maximum exposure and revenue, while both presses benefited from association with the most famous living author. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Lowry notes that the relationship between Aldus and Froben was shaped by the very different business environments of Venice and Basel. Aldus operated in a fiercely competitive capital-intensive market where speed was essential and pirates were always at the gates. Froben, with less immediate competition and a lower cost base, could afford the slower, more meticulous approach that produced the landmark nine-volume Jerome and the Augustine edition. The difference mirrored the broader contrast between Italian commercial printing and northern European humanist publishing. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

After Froben’s death, Erasmus wrote of him: “We have lost Froben, that most exceptional man. His life was devoted to earnest and conscientious labours and he died at his work… For the past eight years I have been his house guest and have had with him the most cordial friendship. A truer friend than Froben, I could not wish from the Gods.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Major Publications

Froben’s press produced some of the most important scholarly works of the early sixteenth century. Among them:

  • The Greek New Testament (1516) — The first printed edition of the Greek New Testament, edited by Erasmus. A second edition followed within three years, totalling 3,300 folio copies by 1519. The text served as the basis for Luther’s German translation and was a landmark both in biblical scholarship and in the Reformation. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]
  • Works of Saint Jerome (1516) — Nine folio volumes, a monumental undertaking that brought great prestige to the Basel publisher. A papal privilege was secured for five years through Erasmus’s influence. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]
  • Thomas More’s Utopia (1518) — Froben published the second edition (the first Basel edition) of More’s celebrated work. This edition was the occasion for Hans Holbein the Younger’s introduction to Erasmus and, subsequently, to More himself. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]
  • Works of Saint Augustine (1529) — Ten folio volumes, completed after Froben’s death by his son Hieronymus Froben with the cooperation of Erasmus and Beatus Rhenanus. Froben himself had been working on this edition in his final hours. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]
  • Erasmus’s Colloquia (1518) — The Familiar Colloquies, one of Erasmus’s most popular works, sold some 24,000 copies through authorised editions in ten years, with many more unauthorised editions. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]
  • First Latin editions of the Greek Fathers — Including Cyprian (1519), Hilary (1523), Irenaeus (1526), and others, often prepared by Erasmus with enormous effort from corrupt manuscripts. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Typography and Book Design

Froben’s types, particularly the Froben Roman, were influenced by Venetian models — especially the work of Aldus Manutius and Nicolas Jenson. His Roman typefaces were clear, well-proportioned, and notably more readable than many of the gothic types then in use in German-speaking lands. He was one of the first northern printers to adopt the Aldine octavo format for scholarly works, making books more portable and affordable. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Froben’s title-page borders and decorative initials were designed by none other than Hans Holbein the Younger, whom Froben brought to Basel around 1517. Holbein produced over 300 woodcut drawings for Froben, including title-page borders, ornamental alphabets, and the printer’s own device. This collaboration between printer and artist set new standards for book design in northern Europe and contributed significantly to the development of early typefaces and book illustration. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Froben’s printer’s mark — a caduceus-like staff entwined by two serpents, with a dove perched on top — was famously interpreted by Erasmus as combining the wisdom of the serpent with the innocence of the dove. The device reflected Froben’s ideal of the scholarly publisher: learned, shrewd, and principled. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Scholarly Network and Business Methods

Froben gathered around him a remarkable circle of learned associates who served as editors, revisers, and press-correctors. The most important was Beatus Rhenanus, who acted as literary counsellor and to whose suggestions many of Froben’s scholarly undertakings were due. Others included Johannes Oecolampadius, Sigismund Gelenius, Wolfgang Musculus, and the Amerbach family. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Froben frequently collaborated with other publishers to share the financial risk of major projects. His principal associates included Johann Amerbach and Johann Petri of Basel, and Franz Birckmann of Cologne. Erasmus, writing in 1523, described three methods Froben used for bringing a book into print: Froben bearing the entire risk; publication on commission for an interested patron; and temporary partnerships of two or more firms. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Religious Position

Although Froben remained within the Catholic Church, his press occupied a complex position during the Reformation. His publications of the Scriptures, the Church Fathers, and Erasmus’s writings contributed materially to the intellectual conditions that made the Reformation possible. He printed one collection of Luther’s writings in 1518, which sold widely in France, Spain, Italy, Brabant, and England, but after 1520 he printed no further works for Luther, probably influenced by Erasmus’s counsel and his own relations with Pope Leo X. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Froben believed the Church was to be reformed not by being broken into fragments but by being brought back to the teachings of its founders and the Fathers. His editions of Jerome, Augustine, and other Fathers, together with his publications of Erasmus, represented his personal contribution to the great controversies of the age. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Business Realities and Financial Constraints

Despite his reputation as one of the greatest scholarly publishers of the age, Froben did not grow wealthy from his press. Lorck records that “Trotz aller Thätigkeit erwarb Froben kein Vermögen” (despite all his industry, Froben acquired no fortune), attributing this to “eine nicht gut geregelte Wirtschaft” (poorly managed household economy) and the enormous sums he spent on careful proofreading. His correctors — whom he called “Kastigatoren” — included Erasmus himself, his father-in-law Wolfgang Lachner, Marc Heiland, Wolfgang Musculus, and Johannes Oecolampadius. Such an editorial staff was expensive, but Froben considered textual accuracy the hallmark of his press. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Luther Challenge

Froben faced a peculiar commercial problem during the Reformation. His colleague Adam Petri became the Luther pirate of Basel: Petri’s reprints of Luther’s works followed the originals “blitzschnell” (lightning-fast) — Luther’s New Testament appeared in September 1522, and Petri’s reprint was out by December. Petri made far better business than Froben because Froben, remaining loyal to the Catholic Church, printed no Lutheran works after 1518. This was a deliberate choice: Froben believed the Church was to be reformed by returning to the Fathers, not by schism. But it cost him the most lucrative market of the age. Meanwhile, Erasmus noted with frustration that Luther’s writings “crowded out” everything else, including Froben’s scholarly editions. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Death and Succession

Johann Froben died in October 1527, collapsing after a fall from a ladder while at work. His death was a severe blow to Erasmus, who wrote: “I cannot endure the loss of Froben.” Erasmus ensured that Froben’s unfinished projects were completed for the benefit of his children: the landmark edition of Saint Augustine (ten folio volumes, 1529) was pushed through by Erasmus with the cooperation of Beatus Rhenanus. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The business was continued by Froben’s eldest son, Hieronymus Froben (1501-1563), who maintained the relationship with Erasmus. Erasmus continued to live in the Froben family house “Zur Luft” (At the Air) in Basel, where he died in 1536. Hieronymus printed in partnership with his brother-in-law Nikolaus Episcopius the Elder (Bischoff) and later with his own sons Ambrosius and Aurelius Froben. Together they produced a series of important scholarly works, maintaining the press’s reputation for typographic quality and textual accuracy into the later sixteenth century. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Legacy

Froben’s influence on the book trade was profound. Erasmus ranked him with Aldus Manutius, declaring that “the world has never seen a publisher who has striven so earnestly and so unselfishly in the cause of scholarship.” He set new standards of textual accuracy, typographic elegance, and scholarly integrity that influenced printers and publishers across Europe. His press was a key conduit through which Italian Renaissance book design — the Aldine octavo, Roman type, and humanist publishing — reached northern Europe and contributed to the spread of printing throughout the continent. Froben’s partnership with Erasmus established a model of author-publisher relations that was centuries ahead of its time. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Key Achievements

  • Founded one of the most important scholarly presses in northern Europe (Basel, 1491)
  • First printed edition of the Greek New Testament, edited by Erasmus (1516)
  • Published the complete works of Saint Jerome in nine folio volumes (1516)
  • Published Thomas More’s Utopia (1518)
  • Published Erasmus’s Praise of Folly, Colloquies, and numerous other works
  • Commissioned Hans Holbein the Younger for title-page borders, initials, and illustrations
  • Introduced the Aldine octavo format and clear Roman typefaces to northern Europe
  • Established the printer’s mark of the caduceus and dove, one of the most recognisable of the period
  • Created the model of the scholar-publisher, combining humanist learning with commercial enterprise
