Overview

Aldus Manutius (c. 1449 — February 6, 1515) was a Venetian printer, publisher, and scholar who revolutionised the book trade by making classical literature widely available in compact, portable editions. His Aldine Press, founded in the 1490s, was the first to specialise in printing Greek classics, developed the italic typeface, introduced the octavo format as a portable book, and created one of the first recognisable printer’s marks — the anchor and dolphin. He is the most influential publisher of the early modern period after johann-gutenberg. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Lowry’s study revised the traditional portrait of Aldus as a pure humanist patronised by like-minded princes, revealing instead a complicated figure who was a minority partner in a hard-nosed commercial syndicate, constantly struggling to balance scholarly ideals against the demands of investors and the brutal realities of the early printing trade. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Early Life

Aldus Manutius was born Teobaldo Manucci in Bassiano in the Romagna, around 1449 (Putnam gives 1450, the year Gutenberg completed his press). He studied in Rome and Ferrara, mastering Latin and then, under the tutorship of Guarini of Verona, devoting himself to Greek. Later he delivered lectures on both Latin and Greek classics. One of his fellow students in Ferrara was the precocious young scholar Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, whose friendship was afterwards of material service to Aldus’s career. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

In 1482, when Ferrara was besieged by the Venetians and scholarly pursuits were interrupted, Aldus was the guest of Pico at Mirandola. There he met Emanuel Adramyttenos, one of the many Greek scholars who, driven from Constantinople after the fall of 1453, had found refuge in the courts of Italian princes. Aldus spent two years at Mirandola, and under Adramyttenos’s guidance he deepened his knowledge of Greek language and literature. Adramyttenos had brought from the East a number of manuscripts, many of which entered Pico’s library. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Around 1482 Aldus took charge of the education of the young princes Alberto and Lionello Pio of Carpi, the nephews of Pico della Mirandola. The princess of Carpi (Pico’s sister) and her sons became lifelong friends and patrons. It was in Carpi that Aldus developed the scheme of using his scholarly connections to print Latin and Greek classics. The funds required for the undertaking were furnished by the Princess of Carpi and her sons, probably with some co-operation from Pico. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Through Pico, Aldus was introduced to Ermolao Barbaro of Venice, a distinguished Greek scholar, and to the broader circle of Venetian humanists. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Lowry emphasises that Aldus’s early life as a “wandering scholar” shaped his lifelong conviction that the printing press could serve as a vehicle for humanist education. His Greek studies under Guarini of Verona and later Adramyttenos gave him both the linguistic skills and the contacts among Greek refugee scholars that would define the Aldine project. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The Barbarigo-Torresani Partnership: Business Structure of the Aldine Press

Before 1495 Aldus was experimenting independently, possibly with capital from Alberto Pio, while Andrea Torresani remained an interested spectator. Some preliminary contract between Aldus and Torresani may have preceded the full partnership. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The formal business partnership was established in 1495 as “a company for the printing of books between the late lord Pierfrancesco Barbarigo, son of the most Serene Prince, on the one side, and the late lord Andrea Torresani, bookseller, with the late lord Aldus de Manutiis, a Roman, also taking a certain part.” This notarial act was discovered by Mario Brunetti in the records of the Procuratori di San Marco. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

To nineteenth-century scholars, even the partnership of Aldus and Andrea seemed to begin only in 1508. The association of doge Marco Barbarigo’s son with the company was known only from a rambling letter written more than forty years after the event by the antiquarian Zuane Bembo, who mentioned that Santo Barbarigo’s father “helped Aldus with several thousand ducats.” Brunetti’s archival discovery proved that Pierfrancesco Barbarigo was not merely a sympathiser but a shareholder who put the weight of his capital and the influence of his ducal family name behind the company from its very beginning. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Pierfrancesco Barbarigo’s father Marco had been doge for less than one year in 1485, succeeded by his uncle Agostino, who exercised power until 1501. Pierfrancesco himself rose to become a member of the Senate but died in 1499. After 1501 the Barbarigo family fell into disrepute due to corruption scandals involving the late doge. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The company was a powerful organisation underwritten by one of the most successful publishers of the age and the nephew of the titular head of the Venetian state. But the basic initiative was Aldus’s: Barbarigo and his family were interested only in dividends, and Torresani lacked the academic skills to direct the scholarly programme. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

However, Aldus’s control was limited. All payments recorded in the Barbarigo account book are divided squarely down the middle, one half going to the Barbarigo estate and the other to Torresani, to whose name Aldus’s is usually linked. The working capital and profits were divided into two equal shares: one controlled completely by the Barbarigo estate, the other held jointly by Torresani and Aldus. When Aldus and Andrea formally unified their property and assets in 1506, four-fifths of the total was Andrea’s. Aldus’s holding in the company was probably one-fifth of Andrea’s half-share, or one-tenth of the total — at most twenty-five per cent. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

This meant Aldus never had the decisive word on business policy. In 1499 he wrote apologising that books could not be offered at a reduced price “because they were a joint venture with certain others.” In 1505 John Cuno reported that Torresani was blocking further publication in Greek. Isabella d’Este wrote that she was returning vellum copies because they were worth barely half the sum asked and Aldus’s partners would accept no less. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Jenson-Torresani Connection

Andrea Torresani of Asola was active as a printer, bookseller and underwriter for more than a decade before forming a partnership with Aldus. He had purchased the matrices of Nicolas Jenson after Jenson’s death in 1480 and claimed to work with Jenson’s types. The colophon to his edition of Panormita’s commentaries on Decretals (1482) states: “Exactum est hoc opus inclytis instrumentis formosisque litterarum characteribus quondam in hac arte Magistri Nicolai Jenson Gallici.” The combined Aldine house thus brought together the typographical legacy of Jenson, the financial support of Torresani, and the scholarly direction of Aldus. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Lowry notes that a “Franciscus de Bononia quondam Caesaris aurifex” was directed by Jenson’s partner Johan Rauchfass in 1475 to copy two of Jenson’s Gothic types, raising the possibility that Griffo — the later Aldine punchcutter — had connections with Jenson’s workshop that linked the most celebrated Venetian printing houses. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Move to Venice and Founding of the Aldine Press

In 1490 Aldus settled in Venice, at that time the printing capital of Europe with some 150 presses in operation. Although the city was crowded with printers, Aldus spotted a gap: no one was publishing books in Greek. The Greek-speaking community of Venice, swelled by refugees from the fall of Constantinople in 1453, had the scholarship but lacked the means to print. Greek handwriting had never been standardised for type, and the technical difficulty was considerable.

Aldus found backers — his former pupils among the Pio family, the Venetian nobleman Pierfrancesco Barbarigo, and the established printer Andrea Torresani of Asola. In 1494, with their support, he founded the Aldine Press. He married Torresani’s daughter Maria, cementing the partnership and eventually gaining control of the printing office that had earlier belonged to Nicolas Jenson. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Aldus set up his workshop in the S. Polo area of Venice, near the church of Sant’ Agostin, some distance from the booksellers’ district of San Zulian and San Paternian but convenient for the Rialto business centre and Andrea Torresani’s shop “at the sign of the Tower” by the bridge. The building at No. 2311 is traditionally identified as Aldus’s house and workshop — an unpretentious fifteenth-century Gothic building on the square of Sant’ Agostin, beside the baker’s. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Writing of his visit in 1508, Erasmus described a household of some thirty people, including workmen, servants, the family, and eight or nine guests. This refers to the years after Aldus’s marriage to Maria Torresani, when the combined families lived together at the Torresani home in San Paternian. The conditions reflected an incredible mixture of sweatshop, boarding house, and research institute. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The Greek Classics and the Aldine Aristotle

Aldus’s great ambition was to preserve the entire corpus of Greek literature in print before the manuscripts decayed or were lost. In 1495 he obtained from the Venetian government a twenty-year monopoly on the printing of Greek texts — one of the earliest copyright privileges in history. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

His first publication, issued in 1495, was the Greek and Latin Grammar of Constantine Laskaris, a suitable forerunner for his great classical series. Laskaris was a refugee from the East and at the time of publication an instructor in Messina; his Grammar was probably the very first work by a living author printed in Italy. The second issue was the complete works of Aristotle, issued in five folio volumes between 1495 and 1498. This was the first printed edition of Aristotle’s complete works and a landmark in classical scholarship. It was followed in 1496 by the Greek Grammar of Theodore Gaza, himself a native of Greece who was for a time associated with the Aldine Press as a Greek editor, and in 1497 by a Greek-Latin Dictionary compiled by Aldus himself. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Before editors or proof-readers could prepare Greek texts for the press, these foundational dictionaries and grammars had to be created. Aldus’s own later grammars continued this work: in 1502 he published, in a handsome quarto volume, a comprehensive grammar titled Rudimenta Grammatices Linguae Latinae (with an introduction to Hebrew), the product of years of labour. He also wrote the Grammaticae Institutiones Graecae, which was not published until 1515, after his death. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Aldus demanded extraordinary rigour from his workforce: his Greek typesetters and proof-correctors were required to speak classical Greek on the premises or face dismissal. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Typographical Innovations

Francesco Griffo and the Development of Aldine Types

The type-fount was always the most expensive item in a printer’s capital equipment, and a Greek fount posed particular problems. Greek uses oxytone or perispomenon accents on all words except enclitics, and rough or smooth breathing-symbols over all initial vowels. These had either to be cast in every possible combination (requiring over 1,400 separate letter forms) or cast separately and set above the letter, slowing composition and increasing costs. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Aldus faced these difficulties with the aid of Francesco da Bologna (Francesco Griffo), arguably the most important single figure in the establishment of the Aldine company but now so utterly obscured by time that both his name and identity remain in doubt. He was once thought to have been the goldsmith Raibolini, but the goldsmith died in 1517 and the type-cutter was still active a year later. A more probable candidate is the “Franciscus Griffus de Bononia incisor Litterarum stampae” mentioned in a Perugian notarial act of 1512. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

There are only three fixed points for Griffo’s connection with Venetian printing: (1) the three-line tribute added by Aldus to the first text printed in italic cursive, the Virgil of 1501; (2) the claim by Gerolamo Soncino of Fano in 1503 that he had secured the services of the expert who had cut all Aldus’s types and deserved the real credit for their design; (3) the repetition of that claim by Francesco himself in an edition of Petrarch’s Canzonieri published on his own account in 1516. Shortly afterward Griffo brained his son-in-law with an iron bar — possibly an incomplete type punch — faced a murder charge in Bologna with unknown results, and vanished from history. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Soncino described Griffo as “a most excellent cutter of Latin, Greek and Hebrew letters, a man whose genius finds no equal in this craft.” If Griffo cut all Aldus’s types, he must have begun experimenting with Greek designs early in the 1490s, years before the publishing company existed. Aldus’s last distinct type was the small Greek cursive introduced for the Sophocles of August 1502; Griffo was in Soncino’s employ by July 1503, so he must have left Venice late in 1502, close to the date (14 November 1502) when Aldus was granted a comprehensive monopoly over all Greek publication and all Latin printing in italic within the Venetian dominions. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The connection between Aldus and Griffo lasted for ten or twelve years at most, and for only seven of these was the publishing company in production. During this period Aldus commissioned and used twelve type-founts in three languages: six rounded Roman Latin types plus the famous italic; four Greek cursives, similar in conception but different in detail; and one Hebrew fount, used sporadically throughout Aldus’s lifetime. After 1502 no new designs were introduced — experiment ended with Griffo’s departure. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Italic Type

In 1500, Aldus commissioned the punch-cutter Francesco Griffo of Bologna to cut a new typeface based on the cursive script used in the Vatican chancery. This became the first italic type — a slanted, compact letterform designed to fit more words per page. It was first used in 1501 for an edition of Virgil. In 1501 Aldus obtained a ten-year copyright for all works printed in this cursive or italic character. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

There was contemporary disagreement over who truly invented italic. Aldus’s petitions for privilege claimed he had “devised a new kind of letter called cursive,” but Soncino and Griffo himself countered that Griffo deserved the credit. By 1514 the Giunti of Florence were also claiming to have been first to print in italic. Aldus’s privilege, by outlawing imitation of his Greek and italic styles, effectively prevented Griffo from selling his most original designs to other printers, which likely caused their quarrel. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Lowry notes that Aldus’s Greek and Latin cursives followed sophisticated academic tastes so exactly that a lively argument has raged over the scribal models used. At least one critic attributes the pattern for the Greek cursive founts to Aldus himself. That Griffo both designed and cut the types as he claimed is most unlikely; the reality is that the credit for this complex operation — scribe, type-cutter, and intermediary — cannot be disentangled. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The Octavo Revolution

Before Aldus, scholarly books were printed almost exclusively in large folio or quarto formats — heavy, expensive volumes suited to libraries and lecterns. Aldus introduced the octavo format (roughly the size of a modern paperback), small enough to be carried in a pocket or saddlebag. These compact editions of the classics, printed in italic type to save space, dramatically reduced the cost of books and made them accessible to students, travellers, and the emerging middle class. They were the first true portable books and a direct forerunner of the modern paperback. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Lowry offers a significant revision: Aldus did not invent the portable book. Smaller formats already existed in manuscript and print, but for devotional works only. Jenson had printed minute Office books in the 1470s; Lazzaro di Soardis applied for copyright on octavo editions in 1497. Aldus’s originality lay in applying the small format to classical literature. His Latin octavos were all safe, established favourites: Horace, Valerius Maximus, Ovid, Catullus. By pruning away the commentaries that lumbered folio editions and combining the smaller format with the most acceptable titles, Aldus freed literature from the study and the lecture-room. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Lowry also challenges the assumption that Aldine octavos were cheap. No contemporary source describes them as inexpensive; Aldus himself emphasised that they were beautiful and convenient, not cheap. They were quickly undercut by pirated imitations. The notion that Aldus introduced both the octavo and the italic as a means of price-cutting is a modern inference unsupported by contemporary evidence. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The small volume, mass-produced in editions of up to three thousand copies, represented a social extension of the humanists’ conviction that literature could enlighten wherever it went. The shameless haste with which the model was copied by competitors is the clearest proof of its success. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The Anchor-and-Dolphin Device

Aldus adopted as his printer’s mark the image of a dolphin entwined around an anchor — a symbol of the classical adage festina lente (“make haste slowly”), meaning activity tempered by restraint. The device was taken from an illustration in the Hypnerotomachia Poliphili and is one of the first recognisable publisher’s logos in the history of printing. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Hypnerotomachia Poliphili (1499)

In 1499 Aldus printed one of the most beautiful books of the Renaissance: the Hypnerotomachia Poliphili (“Poliphilo’s Struggle for Love in a Dream”), a mysterious and erotically charged allegorical romance written in a bizarre invented language mixing Italian, Latin, and Greek. The author was Francesco Colonna, a Venetian monk. The book was financed by a patron, Leonardo Grassi.

Commercially it was a disaster — an expensive, deliberately obscure work that sold very few copies. But typographically it was a masterpiece. Its 172 woodcut illustrations, its elegant Roman type (derived from Jenson’s), its decorated initials, and its harmonious integration of text and image have made it the most celebrated illustrated book of the fifteenth century. Some copies sell for over half a million dollars today. The typeface was later revived as Poliphilus. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Lowry notes that the Hypnerotomachia contains Hebrew characters used as early as 1498, predating Aldus’s formal Hebrew fount. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The Aldine Academy (Neacademia)

Aldus gathered around him a circle of Greek and Latin scholars known as the Aldine Academy (Neacademia, or New Academy). The academy’s rules were unusual: only Greek was to be spoken at meetings, with fines imposed on anyone caught speaking Latin or Italian. Members included Hieronymus Aleander (later a cardinal), Marcus Musurus (whose handwriting was used as the model for Aldus’s Greek type), and the great Dutch humanist Erasmus, who lived in Aldus’s household while preparing an edition of his Adagia and who was paid by Aldus for his editorial work. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Lowry provides a far richer account based on the Academy’s statute. The forty-nine line document, known as NEAKAAHMIAZ NOMOXZ (Statute of the New Academy), survives on a single sheet in the binding of a volume in the Vatican library. It lists seven names: Aldus as “leader”; Scipio Fortiguerra, who appears to have drafted it; a certain “John the Cretan” (possibly Rhosos or Gregoropoulos); Baptista Egnazio; Paolo da Canal; Hironimo Menochio; and Francesco Rosetto. The signatories bound themselves to speak only Greek when together, to pay a small fine for lapses, and to collect the fines until sufficient to finance an “Academicians’ banquet.” There were clauses on admitting other “philhellenes” or visitors who wished to learn Greek. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The statute appeared in print for the first time with the Sophocles of August 1502, which was published with the colophon “Venetiis in Aldi Romani Academia.” In November 1502 the ducal letter of Leonardo Loredan mentioned among Aldus’s services to literature that “he now even has a New Academy.” ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Lowry argues that the statute was designed to advertise as well as organise. Aldus’s editorial activity reached its zenith between 1502 and 1504, but the loss of Giorgio Valla and Brugnolo meant there was no longer a Hellenist among the public lecturers to provide discreet publicity. The Academy served to keep Greek scholarship in the public eye. Foreigners were taught Greek as promised: the German Dominican John Cuno lived and studied with Aldus at Sant’ Agostino, and his notes from John Gregoropoulos’s lectures on Aristophanes survive. The colophon “In Aldi Academia” vanished at the end of 1504, probably linked to the appointment of Leonico Tomeo to the vacant Greek lectureship. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The Academy also involved ambitious plans for an imperial academy under Maximilian. In June 1503 Aldus wrote to Conrad Celtis about making Germany “another Athens for the men of our time.” In August 1505 Aldus thanked the imperial secretary John Collauer for supporting his plan for founding an Academy at the court of Maximilian Caesar. John Cuno’s letter to Willibald Pirckheimer (21 December 1505) reported that Aldus was preparing to move to Germany to found a New Academy where scholars would instruct German youth in Greek, Hebrew, and even military exercises. The project collapsed due to Maximilian’s lack of funds. By the time of Erasmus’s visit in 1508, the word “academy” had become a household joke: Aldus would utter it in a squeaky, broken voice, hinting he would be senile if he ever lived to see such an institution. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Aldus never abandoned hope. Lucretia Borgia offered to establish the Academy at her own expense in Ferrara. The election of Leo X in 1513 inspired Aldus to write one of his most eloquent humanist addresses, dedicating the first edition of Plato’s complete works to the Pope and asking for funds to establish the Academy. The Pope founded a Greek College in Rome, but Lascaris and Callierges ran it, not Aldus. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Erasmus and the Aldine Press

Erasmus’s relationship with Aldus is one of the best-documented aspects of the Aldine Press. He arrived in Venice in 1507 and lived in the Torresani household while preparing the greatly expanded edition of his Adagia (proverbs), published by Aldus in 1508. Erasmus was paid twenty ducats for his various editorial services. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The conditions were memorable. Erasmus sat in one corner of the printing room, writing the Adagia from memory and handing his text sheet by sheet to the compositors, too busy, by his own account, to scratch his ears. In another corner sat Aldus, quietly reading proofs and countering all pleas that they had been checked already with the words “I’m studying.” Between them the types rattled in the compositors’ hands, and ink was daubed over everything in the way, far into the night, while the Dutchman’s stomach, unused to this pressure of work on short commons, rumbled in protest. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

In 1531, long after Aldus’s death, Erasmus was attacked by several Italians for alleged ingratitude to Aldus and gluttony during his stay. He replied with the dramatic dialogue Opulentia Sordida (“How to be Rich without Showing It”), in which he portrayed “Gilbert” (Erasmus) returning from “Synodium” (Venice) to a friend who comments on his wan appearance. Gilbert explains this by the rigorous life in the workshop of “Antronius” (Andrea Torresani) and his son-in-law “Orthrogonus” (Aldus). Erasmus maintained there had been nothing to eat in the Torresani household anyway. Lowry notes that this dialogue, once read as an attack on Aldus, becomes a compliment when Aldus’s true minority position in the partnership is understood. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Privileges and Business

The Venetian government granted Aldus exceptional privileges. In 1496 he secured a twenty-year monopoly on printing Greek texts. In 1501 he obtained a ten-year copyright covering all works printed in italic type. On 14 November 1502 he was granted a comprehensive monopoly over all Greek publication and all Latin printing in italic within the Venetian dominions. These privileges were among the earliest forms of intellectual property protection in the printing trade. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Despite these privileges, enforcement was weak. Papal privileges covered Catholic states in theory but lacked enforcement machinery. The privileges secured from Venice had effect only in Venetian territory. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Aldus has been compared to Atticus, the Roman publisher of the first century who earned that appellation for reproducing Greek classics for Italian readers. But Atticus was a man of large means derived from banking and tax-farming; Aldus began business without capital and died a poor man, reinvesting all receipts into fresh scholarly ventures. The payments Aldus made to editors and scholars were probably the first ever made in Italy for literary work connected with printing, and included compensation for the use of manuscripts that Greek refugees had saved from the Turks. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

In 1500, Aldus married Maria, the daughter of the printer Andrea Torresano of Asola, who had purchased Jenson’s matrices in 1479 and succeeded to his business. In 1507 the two printing concerns were united, and Torresano’s savings strengthened Aldus’s strained resources. The combined house thus brought together the typographical legacy of Jenson, the financial support of Torresano, and the scholarly direction of Aldus. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Lowry provides detailed cost estimates: wages for compositors (2.5-4.5 ducats/month), operators (2-3 ducats), and inkers (about 2 ducats), with a total wages bill of at least 40 ducats per month. Paper costs were roughly equivalent to labour costs. The monthly expenditure approached 100 ducats with only these two items. Erasmus is known to have earned 20 ducats for his services, Fra Giocondo 10 for his work on the Scriptores Rei Rusticae. In 1503 Aldus claimed to be producing 1,000 copies per month at a cost of around 234 ducats per edition. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Production Methods and Workforce

The Aldine workshop probably operated between four and six presses. Each press required three men: a compositor, an operator (torculator), and an inker. Fifteen or more workmen would have been employed. Erasmus said the workers never had more than half an hour throughout the day to spend on their meals. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Aldus had a turbulent relationship with his workforce. In 1501 he referred in a preface to “the conspiracies of my damned runaway slaves.” In 1503 he complained: “Four times now my hired men and workers have conspired against me in my own house, led on by that mother of all evils, Greed: but with the help of God I so smashed them that they all thoroughly regret their treachery.” Only two workers are known by name: Hilarius of Parma, a trusted servant, and Federigo da Ceresara, a distant relation who killed his brother and was seized by partisans in Mantua, provoking an international incident in which Aldus called on the imperial ambassador. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Distribution and Book Fairs

Aldus’s distribution network relied on international book fairs at Frankfurt and Lyons, which provided detailed information about production in different centres. The Barbarigo account book records block purchases by major customers such as Tadio Contarini. The Fugger family acted as intermediaries between Aldus and German scholars. In 1502 Reuchlin wrote to Aldus complaining about block-purchases that reduced his ability to sell individual copies. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Piratical Competition

Despite his privileges, Aldus faced constant competition from piratical editions. Piratical copies of his Rudimenta Grammatices (1502) promptly appeared in Florence, Lyons, and Paris. More broadly, pirated Aldine editions were issued in Cologne, Tubingen, and especially Lyons, where printers produced close imitations of the Aldine cursive type and issued volumes without imprint, date, or place of publication. The privileges secured from Venice had effect only in Venetian territory; papal privileges covered Catholic states in theory but lacked enforcement machinery. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Lowry identifies Lyons as the main centre for counterfeit Aldine editions. The pirated imitations quickly undercut Aldus’s prices, and his own former workers sometimes absconded with advance copies to sell to rivals. In his complaint about “the activities of the Lyon press-pirates,” Aldus revealed that his hired men had conspired against him four times. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Aldus also planned a polyglot Bible in several languages, but did not live to complete it. The project was eventually realised by Christophe Plantin in the 1560s. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Death and Legacy

Aldus Manutius died on February 6, 1515, in Venice. His press passed to his son Paulus Manutius (1512—1574), who maintained the family tradition and later established an Aldine branch in Rome under the patronage of Pope Pius IV. Paulus’s son, Aldus the Younger (1547—1597), was the last of the line. The firm operated under the name “Aldi Filii” (Sons of Aldus) and continued to produce distinguished editions for nearly a century after its founder’s death. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Aldus’s influence on the book trade was transformative. He created the first systematic program of classical publishing, invented the italic typeface, pioneered the portable book format, and established the principle that a printer-publisher could be both a businessman and a scholar. The Aldine Press set standards of textual accuracy, typographic elegance, and scholarly integrity that later printers such as the Estiennes of Paris, Froben of Basel, and the Elzevirs of Leyden sought to emulate. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Lowry offers a more complex assessment. Aldus’s company was not a club for literary dilettanti but a business organisation that had to make its way in a savage competitive world. Aldus may have been the mind that stirred the matter, but he had bills to pay and shareholders to satisfy. His achievement must be judged not only as the expression of an ideal but as an exercise in maintaining a delicate balance between tensions and pressures. The preponderance of intellectual evidence over economic evidence has made it tempting to speak of an “Aldine circle” as if Aldus were simply a humanist who turned printer; this does him much less than justice. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Key Achievements

  • Founded the Aldine Press in Venice (1494)
  • First printed edition of the complete works of Aristotle, 5 volumes (1495—1498) ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]
  • Commissioned the first italic type from Francesco Griffo (1500) ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]
  • Introduced the octavo format, creating the first portable books ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]
  • Printed the Hypnerotomachia Poliphili (1499), the most beautiful Renaissance illustrated book ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]
  • Established the Aldine Academy for Greek scholarship ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]
  • Created the anchor-and-dolphin printer’s mark, the first modern publisher’s logo ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]
  • Secured early copyright privileges for Greek texts and italic type ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]
  • Commissioned twelve type-founts in three languages (Latin, Greek, Hebrew) from Francesco Griffo ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]
  • Published Erasmus’s Adagia (1508), one of the most influential scholarly works of the Renaissance ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]
