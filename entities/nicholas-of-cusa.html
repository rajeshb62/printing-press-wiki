Nicholas of Cusa

Nicholas of Cusa

Overview

Nicholas of Cusa (1401 — 1464), known in Latin as Cusanus, was a German cardinal, canon lawyer, philosopher, church reformer, and cartographer. Born in Kues (Cusa) on the Mosel River eighty kilometres west of Mainz, he was a contemporary of Johann Gutenberg and is suspected by several historians of having been the hidden force behind Gutenberg’s invention. His life overlapped with Gutenberg’s in ways that Man describes as “intriguing” and that “act as a lens through which to observe his world.” His career spanned the Council of Basel, the Ferrara-Florence Council, the Bursfeld monastic reforms, and the literary revolution that followed the rediscovery of Plautus. Karrow’s Mapmakers of the Sixteenth Century documents him as “theologian, philosopher, diplomat, church reformer, cardinal, maker of a single map, and, possibly, a globe.” ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Early Life and Education

Nicholas was born in 1401 to a father who was no aristocrat but well off, with a boat business and several houses. According to tradition, he was educated at a school set up in the late fourteenth century by the Brethren of the Common Life in Deventer in the Netherlands. The Brethren were mystics who devoted themselves to a simple, communal life and the care of the poor, and who also promoted scholarship by producing books — hand-copied and then block-printed ones. Known widely as the “Brothers of the Quill,” they were proud to spread the Word “not by word but by script.” Mysticism, scholarship, writing, and the idea of reproducing information were passions built into Nicholas in his youth. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

He studied at Heidelberg and Padua, where he learned law, mathematics, and astronomy. At Padua he formed a lasting friendship with the physician, mathematician, and astronomer Paolo Toscanelli, with whom he studied astrology. He returned to the Rhineland as a newly qualified canon lawyer — a specialist in Church law — and in 1427 became secretary to the archbishop of Trier, and then, as word of his legal skills spread, secretary to the papal legate in Germany, Cardinal Giordano Orsini. This was the beginning of his career as an ecclesiastical lawyer and statesman. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Career at the Council of Basel

In February 1432, Nicholas arrived in Basel to put the case for a new claimant to the archbishopric of Trier. Though he lost that case, he settled into the anti-papal camp and made himself the council’s chief legal adviser. In November 1433 he presented De Concordantia Catholica (Of Catholic Harmony), arguing that society should be based on an order in which the parts submit to the whole, the whole being the combination of Church and Empire, of Pope and emperor, united by the council. The work drew on recent history: the Council of Constance had ended the Great Schism by deposing three Popes and imposing a fourth. If that was valid, Nicholas reasoned, then the council — not the Pope — represented the true spirit of the Church and could advise, reprimand, and in extreme cases depose him. Authority, he wrote, should arise from the consent of those governed, summing up the principle as: “Quod omnes tangit debet ab omnibus approbari” (“What touches all should be approved by all”). ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Nicholas also helped negotiate with the Hussites in 1433, suggesting a compromise by which they could receive Communion “in two kinds” while agreeing not to use that as an argument to break the unity of the Church. The agreement was signed in Prague the following year, effectively resolving the Bohemian-Hussite crisis. He also turned his attention to calendar reform, presenting De Reparatione Calendarii (On Revising the Calendar) to the council in 1437, proposing a new lunar cycle and the omission of a week. The proposals were not adopted but were later echoed in the Gregorian calendar of 1582. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Switch to the Papal Side

Around 1436-37, Nicholas abruptly abandoned his conciliarist position and joined the pro-papal minority. Man identifies several factors: the council’s growing dysfunction; the prospect of reunification of the Eastern and Western Churches; proposed reforms that would have abolished the Pope’s power to grant benefices, on which Nicholas depended; and the glass ceiling of his non-noble birth, which blocked him from becoming a bishop through the German Church’s aristocratic leadership. He helped create a document bearing the council’s stamp that supported the Pope’s order to transfer the council to Ferrara — a document widely seen by scholars as a forgery. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

From that moment he became one of the Pope’s ablest champions, called the “Hercules of the Eugenians” by his friend the scholar and future Pope Aeneas Sylvius Piccolomini. He carried the invitation to the Byzantine emperor and patriarch in Constantinople and escorted the Greek delegation of 700 bishops, monks, and scholars back to Italy. Nicholas argued the papal cause in town after town across Italy and Germany, eventually helping Frederick III turn against the rump council. Just before his death in 1447, Pope Eugenius IV made Nicholas a cardinal, a confirmation that was ratified by his successor Nicholas V. Shortly afterwards, the new Pope appointed him bishop of Brixen (now Bressanone) in the Tyrol. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The Bursfeld Missal Project

The idea of a standardised missal came from Johann Dederoth, the Benedictine abbot of Bursfeld. Presented at the Council of Basel, his aim was to cleanse the Church of abuses and centralise authority through a new missal, the Ordinarius. The task was inherited by Dederoth’s successor, Johann Hagen, who in 1446 formed a union of six monasteries, the Bursfeld Congregation, which later linked eighty-eight abbeys and monasteries in northern and western Germany, including St Jakob’s in Mainz. In December 1448, Pope Nicholas V sent the Spanish cardinal Juan de Carvajal to Mainz to approve Hagen’s new Ordinarius. Accompanying Carvajal was his friend and co-cardinal, Nicholas of Cusa. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Conflict arose when Mainz’s archbishop backed an alternative liturgy produced by St Jakob’s monastery. The dispute came to a head in 1451-52 during Nicholas’s tour of Germany, when his aide, the Scottish prelate Thomas, favoured Hagen’s text and declared the St Jakob version deviant “both from Benedictine rites and from those of the established Church.” Nicholas obtained a papal bull allowing him to raise an army if necessary. He called a synod in Mainz and then in Cologne in March 1452, at which seventy Benedictine abbots promised to reform their monasteries, including stocking their libraries with good editions of the Bible. Hagen’s text was approved, and St Jakob’s prior was replaced by a Bursfeld nominee. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The 1451 Cyprus Indulgences

In 1450, King John of Lusignan of Cyprus, fearful of the Turkish advance, appealed to the Pope for help. In August 1451, Pope Nicholas V granted the king the right to produce plenary indulgences for the next three years, raising cash to pay mercenaries to protect Cyprus from the Turks. In early May 1452, Nicholas of Cusa — newly made Bishop of Brixen and still on his grand legation of Germany — ordered his new appointee, the prior of St Jakob’s in Mainz, to prepare two thousand indulgences for sale in Frankfurt by the end of the month. Man writes that “Nicholas’s order must have come as music to Gutenberg’s ears,” providing a cash-flow solution just when the planned missal had “hit the buffers.” ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Possible Connection to Gutenberg

Man presents the connection between Nicholas and Gutenberg as circumstantial but significant. Both were born to well-off, non-noble families in the same region (Kues is eighty kilometres west of Mainz). Nicholas was in Mainz for a court case in 1424, when both were twenty-four-year-olds. A later meeting between 1428 and 1432, after Gutenberg had left Mainz and before Nicholas arrived in Basel, could have planted the germ of an idea: Nicholas wanted Christian unity, and unity required uniform texts across all Christendom. “What if Nicholas of Cusa and Johann Gutenberg shared this vision of somehow, no one knew how, duplicating texts to perfection?” ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

When Carvajal and Nicholas arrived in Mainz in December 1448 to approve the Bursfeld missal, Gutenberg had just returned to the city that same year. Man suggests Nicholas would have been gratified to discover the means of printing the new missal was to hand. During Nicholas’s 1451-52 visit to Mainz to settle the liturgical dispute, Gutenberg was already hard at work. At the synod of seventy Benedictine abbots, Nicholas emphasised the need for monastic libraries to possess a well-translated and edited Bible. Man notes that “as he should have known by then — because Gutenberg had been at work in the city for the last three years — there was only one way to achieve this: by printing from a single source, fixed in metal.” Nicholas ordered a copy of Gutenberg’s Bible for his own church in Brixen, now in Vienna. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Giovanni Andrea de Bussi, who had been Nicholas’s secretary and became chief librarian of the Vatican, wrote in the preface to the 1470 Sweynheym-Pannartz edition of St Jerome: “It is that which the soul (rich in honours and meriting heaven) of Nicholas of Cusa … so fervently desired: that this holy art, whose shoots became visible at that time in Germany, should be transplanted to Roman soil.” ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The Plautus Rediscovery

In 1429, Nicholas brought to Rome manuscripts containing twelve plays by the Latin comedy writer Plautus. When published — they went through thirteen editions by 1500 — they influenced European comedy from that point onward. Man traces the debt through Nicholas Udall, author of the first English comedy Ralph Roister Doister (1552), to Shakespeare (The Taming of the Shrew), Moliere (The Miser), and the modern French playwright Jean Giraudoux (Amphitryon 38, 1929). ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Philosophy and Character

Nicholas’s philosophy was built on the concept of the coincidentia oppositorum — the coincidence of opposites — in which God, as the infinite maximum, coincides with the minimum. His notion of docta ignorantia (“learned ignorance”) held that the purpose of knowledge is to learn how inadequate all learning is when seeking God. His whole life was defined by the “obsession to make opposites coincide, to establish the unity foreshadowed by ancient Rome and Charlemagne.” In practical terms, this meant incessant travel, persuasion, and writing as tools to stitch together a fragmented Christian commonwealth. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Cartography

Karrow’s Mapmakers of the Sixteenth Century (entry no. 23) documents Cusanus as a significant figure in cartographic history: “theologian, philosopher, diplomat, church reformer, cardinal, maker of a single map, and, possibly, a globe.” His map of central Europe is one of the earliest printed maps of the region and “one of the most remarkable maps of the fifteenth century.” ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

The 1491 Map of Central Europe (Eichstatt Map)

The only surviving evidence of Cusanus’s map comes from a printed copper engraving called the Eichstatt map, an impression apparently made between 1514 and 1518. The map proper (copper engraving, ca. 397 x 570 mm plate mark) consists of two distinct parts: a main section (section D) showing all of central Europe from Sweden in the north to the Italian Alps in the south, from Brussels in the west to the Black Sea in the east, and a later extension (section E) carrying coverage as far south as the fortieth parallel in central Italy. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Three inscriptions in the top margin provide the historical record:

  • Inscription A (added last, engraved rather than punched): “For the shared use of learned people, Conrad Peutinger, a legal scholar from Augsburg, has entrusted the printing of this copper-plate (which he bought with his own money) to his fellow-citizen, the painter Hans Burgkmair, who has rendered him great service.” Only one copy with this full inscription existed (in GyMW, destroyed in World War II); all seven surviving copies were pulled after the inscription had been cut from the plate. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

  • Inscription B: Twelve hexameter verses crediting Cusanus with the map: “because the whole of Germany has been depicted on a small map, and also the side of Italy which faces the icy Alps, and the wild tribes of the Sarmatians… let thanks be given to Nicolaus of Cusa who was formerly clad in Tyrian purple [i.e., a cardinal], and was a great splendour of the Roman senate, and who ordered places, unexplored by any predecessors, to be engraved on a copper-plate of modest size” (trans. O. A. W. Dilke). ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

  • Inscription C: “Completed at Eichstatt the 12th day before the Calends of August [21 July] 1491.” This date has been much debated since Cusanus died in 1464. Hildebrand (1892) theorised the engraver misread a handwritten “5” for a “9,” implying 1451, the year Cusanus was in Eichstatt — but his visit occurred in April, not July. The inscription likely marks completion of the engraving, not the manuscript. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Seven extant copies survive: FPBn, GyBS, GyDiS, GyNGm, GySwftS, GyWZ, UkLBI. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Manuscript Origins and Surveying Methods

Cusanus probably completed a manuscript of his map sometime in the 1450s. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md] As papal legate for Germany (1450), he traveled extensively through Germany and the Low Countries in 1451-1452, collecting geographical data. He established many locations himself using an astrolabe and a torquetum (“Turkish instrument,” a device rather like a theodolite) that he acquired in 1444. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

His inspiration for mapping likely came from Ptolemy’s Geography (in one of its early Latin editions) and from his Padua friend Paolo Toscanelli, who was a close friend of Leon Battista Alberti, whose treatises on surveying and the mapping of Rome (Descriptio urbis Romae, ca. 1445) and on “mathematical games” (Ludi matematici, ca. 1450) were precocious for the period. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

No manuscript map that can be unequivocally attributed to Cusanus survives, although Sebastian Munster apparently had such a map in his possession when he wrote his account of it in 1530. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Connection to the Rome Ptolemy and Conrad Sweynheim

The Eichstatt map is lettered with the same set of letter-punches that Conrad Sweynheim perfected in Rome between about 1474 and 1477 for the 1478 Rome edition of Ptolemy’s Geography. In this technique, individual capital letters were punched into the copperplate instead of being cut with a graver. No other fifteenth-century engravings are known to have used these punches, except the Eichstatt map and an engraved coat of arms of the bishop of Eichstatt. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

This suggests that Sweynheim may have carried out the engraving of the Cusanus map separately from the Ptolemy project — perhaps because of a personal pledge to Cusanus. Cusanus almost certainly knew the German clerics Sweynheim and Pannartz and may even have been responsible for their setting up the first printing establishment in Italy, at Subiaco, the year after his death. Cusanus may have come to know Sweynheim in Mainz, where the latter probably apprenticed with the printers Fust and Schoffer. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

The map is drawn on the trapezoidal projection attributed to Nicolaus Germanus, giving it a superficial resemblance to the regional maps in the 1478 Rome Ptolemy. However, the Eichstatt map was not included in any Ptolemy edition. The punches might have continued in use for some time: the Rome Ptolemy was reissued in 1490 (still without modern maps), and in 1507 a new edition included seven modern maps lettered with the 1478 punches plus one additional small font that appears in section E of the Eichstatt map. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Printing History

The plate had a long, complex printing history:

  • The engraving was completed at Eichstatt in 1491, perhaps only a few proof copies pulled on that occasion.
  • Conrad Peutinger (1465-1547) acquired the plate, probably after 1491. Hans Burgkmair (1473-1531), who became a master printer in 1498, printed it around 1514. A letter by Peutinger from January or February 1514 mentions a map: “I have not sent the map to your abbot… I had just a few printed.” ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]
  • In 1518, the editor Johannes Eck wrote in his forward to Maciej z Miechowa’s Tractatus de duabus Sarmatiis that “the excellent prince and lord, Lord Nicolaus Cusa, showed the learned German kingdom in a little map.” ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]
  • The last known printing was by Sebastian Munster in 1530, published by Andreas Cratander of Basel. Cratander apparently removed the Peutinger/Burgkmair inscription (A) by cutting it from the plate. All seven extant copies were printed from this state. The plate remained in Peutinger’s possession; his brother-in-law Christoph Welser noted in 1535 that Peutinger still had the “bronze archetype.” Based on watermark evidence, sporadic printings may have been made as late as the 1560s or 1570s. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Related Maps and Influence

The Cusanus map had some influence on the later mapping of Germany but “was not decisive” and “probably not widely circulated” until 1530, by which time better maps were becoming available. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md] Related maps include:

  • 23/1.1 — Henricus Martellus Germanus (ca. 1485): Manuscript of central Europe (460 x 575 mm) in Florence, thought by Ptolemy scholar Joseph Fischer to be a copy of the Cusanus manuscript. It shows Cusanus’s tiny birthplace, Cues. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]
  • 23/1.3 — Francesco Rosselli (ca. 1492): Copper engraving (ca. 330 x 470 mm), possibly engraved in Florence, showing affinities with the Leiden manuscript. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]
  • 23/1.4 — Germania in the Nuremberg Chronicle (1493): Woodcut map (392 x 579 mm) drawn by Hieronymus Munzer and cut by Michael Wolgemut (teacher of Durer), showing traces of the Cusanus map in its depiction of rivers. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]
  • 23/1.5 — Marcus Beneventano (1507): Map titled “Tabvla moderna Polonie, Vngarie, Boemie, Germanie, Russie, Lithvanie” in the 1507 Rome Ptolemy, strikingly similar to the Rosselli version. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]
  • 23/1.6 — Martin Waldseemuller (1513): Woodcut map of Sarmatia, Hungary, Poland, Russia, Prussia, and Wallachia in the 1513 Strasbourg Ptolemy. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]
  • 23/1.7 — Giovanni Andrea Vavassore (ca. 1530): The only later map showing clear evidence of copying directly from the Eichstatt map (as opposed to a manuscript prototype). Letterpress in rectangular cartouche quotes the hexameters from inscription B. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]
  • 23/1.9 — Ferando Bertelli (1562): Copper engraving (373 x 503 mm) published in Venice, referencing a “new and exact description” of Germany and surrounding regions. Listed in the table of contents as “Central and Eastern Europe, by Paolo Forlani and Ferando Bertelli, after Nicolaus Cusanus (Venice, 1562).” ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Celestial Globe

The library in Bernkastel-Kues holds two fifteenth-century celestial globes: a large wooden globe (272 mm diameter) that Cusanus acquired in 1444 (at the same time as the torquetum), and a smaller, incompletely engraved copper globe (166 mm diameter) that Cusanus almost certainly commissioned and may even have made himself. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Significance in Cartographic History

Despite being the maker of only a single known map, Cusanus holds an important place in cartographic history. His map of central Europe was among the first printed maps of the region; it used systematic latitude determinations via astrolabe and torquetum; it was one of the earliest maps engraved on copper using Sweynheim’s punched-letter technology; its 1491 completion date makes it a landmark in early German copper engraving. The map has generated “a vast literature and much speculation,” with Tony Campbell (1987, 35-55) providing “an informed and judicious guide, while adding important findings of his own” on its physical manufacture. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Death

Nicholas of Cusa died at Todi, in Umbria, on 11 August 1464 and was buried in Rome. His later years after being made bishop of Brixen were a continual political struggle with the German prelates whose aristocratic ranks he had been thrust into by papal favour. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Legacy

Nicholas of Cusa was famous in his lifetime but mostly forgotten until a Cusanus renaissance in Germany in the mid-nineteenth century. The University of Trier maintains a major research institute devoted to him, based on the library of books and manuscripts he left on his death. Cusanus societies exist in both America and Japan, where he is regarded as an honorary Buddhist. His role in bringing the Plautus manuscripts to Rome, his push for liturgical uniformity through the Bursfeld missal, his orchestration of the Cyprus indulgence campaign in Mainz, and his possible influence on Gutenberg’s invention combine to make him a pivotal figure in the story of the printing revolution. His 1491 map of central Europe ranks as one of the most remarkable maps of the fifteenth century and a landmark in the history of European cartography. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Key Achievements

  • Brought twelve Plautus manuscripts to Rome (1429), influencing European comedy through Shakespeare, Moliere, and others
  • Wrote De Concordantia Catholica (1433), a landmark of conciliarist political theory
  • Helped negotiate the 1434 agreement with the Hussite moderates at Prague
  • Proposed calendar reform (1437), later echoed by the Gregorian calendar
  • Played a central role in the Ferrara-Florence Council and the failed reunification of Eastern and Western Churches
  • Became the only German cardinal of his era, “Cardinal Teutonicus”
  • Championed the Bursfeld missal, standardising the liturgy across eighty-eight monasteries
  • Ordered the preparation of two thousand Cyprus indulgences (1452), providing a potential commission for Gutenberg
  • Ordered a copy of Gutenberg’s Bible for his see in Brixen
  • His secretary, Giovanni Andrea de Bussi, recorded his desire to transplant printing to Italy
  • Created a manuscript map of central Europe (ca. 1450s), later engraved and printed as the Eichstatt map (1491/1514), one of the earliest printed maps of the region and “one of the most remarkable maps of the fifteenth century”
  • Used astrolabe and torquetum for latitude determinations during his travels as papal legate
  • Commissioned a copper celestial globe (ca. 1444-1464), possibly made by his own hand
  • His map influenced later cartographers including Henricus Martellus, Francesco Rosselli, Marcus Beneventano, and Giovanni Andrea Vavassore
  • The map’s lettering connects it directly to Conrad Sweynheim’s Rome Ptolemy (1478), one of the earliest uses of punched-letter engraving
