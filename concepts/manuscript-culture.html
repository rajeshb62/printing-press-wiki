Manuscript Culture and the Book before Print

Manuscript culture describes the world of handwritten book production that dominated Europe from the fall of the Roman Empire until the diffusion of movable-type in the late fifteenth century. This was not a static tradition but one that evolved from monastic scriptoria to commercial urban workshops, from the liturgical codex to the illuminated romance, from scribal monopoly to the gradual displacement by print. The manuscript book was simultaneously a sacred object, a work of art, a commodity, and a vessel of knowledge — and every one of these roles was transformed by the arrival of the printing press.

The Monastic Scriptorium

For the first seven centuries of the medieval period, the production of books was almost exclusively the work of monks. The Rule of St. Benedict (c. 540) required daily reading as a spiritual discipline, and from this grew the tradition of the scriptorium — a dedicated room in the monastery where monks copied texts by hand. The Benedictine maxim laborare est orare (“to work is to pray”) made copying a holy act. As De Vinne records: “It was ordered that every abbot, bishop and count should keep in permanent employment a qualified copyist who must write correctly, using Roman letters only, and that every monastic institution should maintain a room known as the scriptorium, fitted up with desks and furnished with all the implements for writing.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Cassiodorus and the Foundation of the Scriptorium

The first European monastery in which literary labour was carried on as part of the prescribed rule of monastic life was not a Benedictine house at all, but the monastery of Vivarium (Vivaria or Viviers) in Calabria, founded in 531 by Cassiodorus. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] Cassiodorus — Magnus Aurelius Cassiodorus Senator (c. 479-575) — had served as Quaestor, Consul, and Praetorian Prefect under Theodoric the Great and his successors, and was the author of the first official utterance of religious toleration in Europe: Religionem imperare non possumus, quia nemo cogitur ut credat invitus (We must not enforce a creed, since no one can think or believe against his will). ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] After a half-century of political life, he retired at age sixty to his monastery, where he lived another thirty-six years organizing what became the model scriptorium for all of Europe.

It was Cassiodorus, not Benedict, who first insisted on intellectual labour as a monastic duty. The historian Hodgkin writes: “It was the glory of Cassiodorus that he first and pre-eminently insisted on the expediency of including intellectual labour in the sphere of monastic duties… Here, on the one hand, were the vast armies of monks… Here, on the other hand, were the accumulated stores of two thousand years of literature… perishing for want of men with leisure to transcribe them. Cassiodorus perceived that the boundless, the often wearisome leisure of the convent might be profitably spent in arresting this work of denudation.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] The German scholar Franz concurs: “The credit of having first included the cultivation of science within the range of monastic life may justly be claimed for Cassiodorus.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Cassiodorus described the work of the scriptorium with soaring rhetoric: “As the antiquarius copies the words of Christ, so many wounds does he inflict upon Satan. What he writes in his cell will be scattered far and wide over distant provinces. Man multiplies the words of Heaven, and, if I may dare so to speak, the three fingers of his right hand are made to express the utterances of the Holy Trinity.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] He emphasized binding worthy of the contents, preparing a pattern volume with samples of covers from which scribes could choose. He equipped the scriptorium with mechanical self-trimming lamps for night work, sun-dials for bright days, and water-clocks for cloudy days and night hours. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] He himself transcribed the Psalter, the Prophets, and the Epistles to set an example.

Though Cassiodorus adopted the Rule of Cassian rather than that of Benedict, “the Benedictines, influenced probably by the example of the monastery of Vivaria, commenced that long series of services to the cause of literature which they have never wholly intermitted. Instead of accepting the formula from which some scholars have contended that Cassiodorus was a Benedictine, we should perhaps be rather justified in maintaining that Benedict, or at least his immediate followers, were Cassiodorians.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] The continuity of classical civilization through the Middle Ages, Putnam concludes, “was due, more than to any other one man, to the life and labours of Cassiodorus.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Benedictine Rule and the Duty of Copying

The Rule of St. Benedict, composed around 540 at Monte Cassino, became the code of monastic Christendom for centuries. Chapter 48, “Concerning Daily Manual Labour,” reads: “Idleness is the enemy of the soul: hence brethren ought at certain seasons to occupy themselves with manual labour, and again at certain hours with holy reading… During Lent, let them apply themselves to reading from morning until the end of the third hour, and in these days of Lent, let them receive a book apiece from the library and read it straight through.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] The routine of the monastic day was to include seven hours for manual labour and two hours for reading. Since books were scarce and monks many, the injunction to read necessarily carried with it an instruction to write — copies had to be produced before they could be read. “Wherever a Benedictine house arose, or a monastery of any one of the Orders which were but offshoots from the Benedictine tree, books were multiplied and a library came into existence, small indeed at first, but increasing year by year, till the wealthier houses had gathered together collections of books that would do credit to a modern university.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

It was this provision, uttered by one whose far-reaching influence has rarely been equalled, that “gave an impulse to study that grew with the growth of the Order, and that secured a continuity of intellectual light and life through the dark ages.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] Scribe work was accepted not only as the “manual labour” prescribed but, for skilled scribes, in lieu of some portion of religious observance. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Irish Scribal Tradition

The earliest significant monastic scholar of Northern Europe was S. Columba (521-597), the Apostle to Caledonia. Before age twenty-five he had founded twenty-seven monasteries in Ireland, including Darrow and Derry. He is traditionally said to have transcribed three hundred copies of the Psalter with his own hand. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] The most famous story concerns a clandestine copy he made of Abbot Finnian’s Psalter. When discovered, Finnian claimed the copy belonged to the original’s owner — “the transcript is the offspring of the original work” — in what may be the first recorded copyright dispute in European history. King Diarmid ruled in a rustic phrase that became an Irish proverb: “To every cow her calf, and consequently to every book its copy.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] The dispute escalated into civil war. The manuscript itself, the Cathac (“the fighter”), was preserved for 1,300 years in the O’Donnell family and was exhibited at the Royal Irish Academy as late as 1867. Scholars O’Curry and Reeves believed the famous Book of Kells was also the work of Columba. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Exiled as penance for the bloodshed caused by the copyright war, Columba settled on the island of Iona, where his monastery became a centre of missionary and literary activity. From Iona came scholarly teachers to France and Germany, influencing monasteries including Luxeuil (in the Vosges), Corbie (on the Somme), Bobbio (in Lombardy), and St. Gall (in Switzerland). ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] Columba also famously intervened at the Parliament of Drumceitt to save the Bards — the poets, genealogists, and historians of Celtic Ireland — from suppression, arguing: “The bright corn must not be burned because of the weeds that mingled with it.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Irish missionaries “revived the taste for letters” across Western Europe, as De Vinne notes: “Books of great beauty, admirably written by unknown Irish copyists, are still preserved in Germany, France and Switzerland, to which countries Irish missionaries were sent from Iona between the sixth and ninth centuries.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The Scriptorium as Workshop and Spiritual Discipline

The consecration of the scriptorium was itself a liturgical act. A surviving eighth-century benediction reads: “Vouchsafe, O Lord, to bless this scriptorium of Thy servants and all that work therein: that whatsoever sacred writings shall be here read or written by them, they may receive with understanding and may bring the same to good effect.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The monk in charge of the writing-room was the armarius (from armarium, the book-press). He provided writing materials, divided the work, preserved silence, and corrected careless scribes. He also managed the loan and exchange of books with other monasteries, often requiring pledges of other manuscripts or property as security. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] The scribes were absolved from field labour except at harvest, and had the privilege of visiting the kitchen to polish writing tablets, melt wax, and dry parchment. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Cistercian General Chapter of 1134 directed: “In all scriptoria, wherever monks write according to custom, silence shall be maintained as in the cloister.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] In the larger monasteries the scriptorium was placed adjoining the calefactory (warming-house), allowing hot air to be introduced through the stone floor. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Writing was frequently imposed as penance. In a codex from Lorch, one scribe wrote: “Jacob wrote… a certain portion of this book not of his own free will but under compulsion, bound by fetters, just as a runaway and fugitive has to be bound.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] But voluntary devotion was the norm. Othlonus, a monk of Tegernsee (born 1013), wrote nineteen missals illuminated with his own hand, “the production of which, he tells us, nearly cost him his eyesight.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] At Wedinghausen in Westphalia, the scribe Richard was so devoted that twenty years after his death, “when the rest of his body had crumbled into dust, the right hand, with which this holy work had been accomplished, was found intact, and has since been preserved under the altar as a holy relic.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

A monk of St. Gall left a plaintive note on one of his manuscripts: “He who does not know how to write imagines it to be no labour, but although these fingers only hold the pen, the whole body grows weary.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] Dietrich, Abbot of St. Evroul (1050-1057), told his monks the story of a sinful brother who had written an enormous folio volume. When he died, the devil claimed his soul, but the angels brought the book before the judgment throne: for each letter written, pardon was given for one sin. The count left one letter over — and it was a very big book. The soul was permitted to return to its body for a period of penance. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Carolingian Renaissance and Alcuin

Charlemagne’s educational reforms, carried out under the direction of the English scholar Alcuin of York (c. 735-804), gave the scriptorium system its most powerful institutional impulse. Alcuin had been trained at the cathedral school of York, founded by Egbert and nourished by the libraries that Benedict Biscop had collected on six arduous journeys to Rome. Alcuin’s teacher was Aelbert, who succeeded Egbert as Archbishop. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] The library at York was one of the most important in Christendom. Alcuin’s metrical catalogue of its contents includes Aristotle, Cicero, Pliny, Virgil, Statius, Lucan, and Trogus Pompeius, alongside the Church Fathers and Christian poets. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Called to the Continent in 782, Alcuin organized the palace school at Tours and imperial schools at Aachen and Milan. The script that became the standard for the imperial schools — the Caroline minuscule, which served seven centuries later as model for the first type-founders of Italy and France — can be traced directly to the school at York. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] Alcuin’s care for consistent punctuation and orthography impressed a special character on the manuscripts of the early ninth century. His treatise on orthography followed closely the principles Cassiodorus had laid down three centuries earlier. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Charlemagne’s Capitulary of 789 commanded: “Do not permit your pupils, either in reading or writing, to garble the text; and when you are preparing copies of the Gospel, the Psalter, or the Missal, see that the work is confided to men of mature age, who will write with due care.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] Alcuin’s verses for the scribes, inscribed over the door of the scriptorium at St. Martin’s in Tours, exhorted:

“Here let the scribes sit who copy out the words of the Divine Law, and likewise the hallowed sayings of the holy Fathers. Let them beware of interspersing their own frivolities in the words they copy, nor let a trifler’s hand make mistakes through haste. Let them earnestly seek out for themselves correctly written books to transcribe, that the flying pen may speed along the right path. Let them distinguish the proper sense by colons and commas, and let them set the points each one in its due place. It is a noble work to write out holy books, nor shall the scribe fail of his due reward. Writing books is better than planting vines, for he who plants a vine serves his belly, but he who writes a book serves his soul.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Alcuin died at Tours in 804. His work was carried forward by his pupils. Rabanus Maurus, abbot of Fulda and later Archbishop of Mainz, has been called “Alcuin’s greatest pupil” and was “intellectually a greater man than his master.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] John Scotus Erigena, appointed master of the palace school at Tours in 845 by Charles the Bold, widened the range of study to include works of doubtful orthodoxy like the Satyricon of Martianus Capella. The stream of learning, West summarizes, flowed “from York to Tours and from Tours (through Rabanus) to Fulda, thence to Auxerre, Ferrieres, Corbies (old and new), Reichenau, St. Gall, and Rheims, one branch of it finally reaching Paris.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Notable Monastery Scriptoria

Specific monasteries emerged as centres of exceptional literary production, whose manuscripts were sought throughout Europe:

Monte Cassino (founded 529 by St. Benedict) — the mother house of the Benedictine Order, near Naples, exercised centuries of influence. Abbot Didier (later Pope Victor III) had his monks make transcripts of Horace, Seneca, several treatises of Cicero, and the Fasti of Ovid. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] Here were produced the chronicles that form the basis of Muratori’s great collection. The first printing in Italy was done at Subiaco, the site of Benedict’s first retreat, nearly a thousand years after the saint’s death. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

St. Gall (Switzerland) — by the tenth century housed five hundred monks and a great body of students. Its manuscripts were noted as “among the most beautiful and accurate specimens of calligraphy known,” with exquisite miniatures and illuminations. The parchment was prepared by the monks themselves, who also did their own binding. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] The scribe Sintram was famous throughout the cisalpine world. The monastery maintained a society for Greek studies called the Hellenic Brothers. Lecturers taught Cicero, Quintilian, Horace, Terence, Juvenal, Persius, Ovid, and Sophocles. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] The great dictionary of Abbot Solomon (later Bishop of Constance) — a literary and scientific encyclopedia of 1,070 pages — was produced here. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Bobbio (Lombardy) — founded by Irish missionaries, became a great repository of classical texts. Its literary treasures were largely preserved in the Ambrosian Library in Milan. Gerbert, Abbot of Bobbio (later Pope Sylvester II), wrote letters revealing an active book trade: he paid transcribers in Rome, Italy, Germany, and Belgium, and bought texts of important authors at great expense. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Corbie (on the Somme) — one of the monasteries where five books of the Annals of Tacitus were found and preserved. Brother Nevelo records working off a grave sin by labour in the scriptorium. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] The monk Henri Bohic’s manuscript was written here in 1374; the cash record shows he received thirty-six solidi in addition to materials. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Fulda (Germany) — Rabanus Maurus made this the foremost intellectual centre of Germany. Its library at one time surpassed all others in Germany except perhaps St. Gall. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Tours (St. Martin’s) — Alcuin’s school produced the Carolingian minuscule that became the standard script of Europe. Odo, Abbot of St. Martin at Tournai (1093), wrote that “if you had gone into the cloister during the working hours, you would have seen a dozen scribes writing, in perfect silence, at tables constructed for the purpose.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] He caused all of Jerome’s Commentaries on the Prophets, all of Gregory’s works, and all available works of Augustine, Ambrose, Isidore, Bede, and Anselm to be transcribed.

Fleury (France) — the abbey schools contained as many as five thousand students during the tenth and eleventh centuries. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] Here were preserved the Aulularia of Plautus, the Commentaries of Servius on Virgil, and the Republic of Cicero. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Reichenau (Germany) — noted as early as 821 for its excellent manuscript collection. The librarian Reginbert prepared an exhaustive catalogue; many of the manuscripts were his own work. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Hirschau (Germany) — Abbot William (eleventh century) trained twelve monks as excellent writers and placed over them a monk “well versed in all kinds of knowledge.” During twenty-two years under William, his monks wrote a great many volumes, a large proportion of which were distributed to supply needier monasteries. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Wearmouth and Yarrow (England) — founded by Benedict Biscop, who made six journeys to Rome collecting books. These monasteries produced the Venerable Bede and trained Alcuin’s teachers. Biscop’s copies of the Bible according to Jerome’s version were placed open for use by monks and lay readers alike. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Nuns as Scribes

Women contributed significantly to manuscript production. The Rule of St. Caesarius of Arles (fifth century) required all sisters to be able to read and to devote two hours daily to study. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] The most remarkable of the nun-scribes was Diemude of Wessobrunn (eleventh century), who wrote with her own hand a library that included a two-volume Bible, a three-volume Bible, three volumes of Augustine on the Psalms, two volumes of Augustine on the Gospels, Gregory on Ezekiel, Origen on the Old Testament, Origen on the Canticles, Jerome’s Epistles (164 in number), the Tripartite History of Cassiodorus, Augustine’s Confessions, and many other works. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] “Some of the most beautiful specimens of calligraphy which have been preserved from the Middle Ages are the work of the nuns,” Putnam records. “They brought to the work a dexterity, an elegance of attainment, and an assiduity which the monks themselves could not attain.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Division of Labour and Materials

A clear division of labour developed in the larger scriptoria. Some monks wrote, others compared and corrected, still others rubricated (added red headings). The most skilled brethren — rubricatores, illuminatores, miniatores — painted initials, border decorations, and figurative scenes. Lorck describes this world: “Die Monche hatten in ihrem beschaulichen Leben Zeit nicht allein zu einem Abschreiben in einfacher Weise, sondern auch, dies zu einer Kunst auszubilden” (The monks had time in their contemplative life not only for simple copying, but also to raise it to an art). ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Parchment was costly throughout Europe until the late thirteenth century. The difficulty of obtaining it sometimes drove monastic scribes to scrape old manuscripts clean and write over them — producing palimpsests that have caused much scholarly lamentation. Robertson was particularly severe on the “ignorant clumsiness of the monks in thus destroying, for the sake of futile legends, so much of the great literature of the world.” Maitland countered that the destruction has been much overestimated: a large proportion of palimpsests represent monastery work replacing earlier monastery texts, not classic works destroyed by monks. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] As late as the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, notaries were forbidden to practise until they had sworn to use only new parchment — indicating the extent of the palimpsest problem. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The belief that transcribing good books was protection against Satan led scribes to add anathemas to their manuscripts. A St. Gall manuscript warns: “Let no one through all ages who wishes to have any part with Gallus remove or purloin this book.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] A donor of a Sacramentary to St. Benoit-sur-Loire invoked the fates of Judas, Ananias, and Caiaphas on any person who should remove the book from the monastery. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] A manuscript in the Bodleian reads: “This book belongs to S. Mary of Robert’s Bridge; whosoever shall steal it or sell it, or in any way alienate it from this house, or mutilate it, let him be anathema maranatha. Amen.” A later owner wrote beneath: “I, John, Bishop of Exeter, know not where the aforesaid house is, nor did I steal this book, but acquired it in a lawful way.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Cost of Books in the Manuscript Era

The laborious nature of hand-copying made books extraordinarily expensive — commodities beyond the reach of all but the wealthiest institutions and individuals. A complete Bible could cost as much as a farm. De Vinne reports that a “Bible fairly written” was sold in England in 1274 for 50 marks — roughly 33 pounds — at a time when a laborer’s daily wage was 1.5 pence and a sheep cost a shilling. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] Roger Bacon, the Franciscan scholar, claimed to have spent more than 2,000 pounds on books during his lifetime — at a time when the annual income of an English curate was just over 3 pounds.

The economics went beyond copying. Lorck emphasizes that illuminated manuscripts were true luxury objects: “Solche Werke hatten naturlich einen sehr hohen Preis und wurden mitunter mit einem Rittergut aufgewogen, konnten also selbstverstandlich nur von Fursten und reichen Leuten angeschafft werden” (Such works naturally had a very high price and were sometimes exchanged for a knight’s estate, so they could of course only be acquired by princes and wealthy people). ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

De Vinne provides a systematic breakdown. Daunou estimated the average price of each manuscript in a large fourteenth-century library at about 450 francs. Didot calculated that of 300 books in the library at Ratisbon in 1231, the average was 600 francs. By the fourteenth century, the price of copying a Bible at Bologna — exclusive of binding, parchment, and illumination — was 80 Bolognese livres. The real cost, however, lay not in the scribal labor but in the illumination and binding: “Books were expensive, not so much through the labor of the copyist, who did the simplest and cheapest part of the work, but through the extravagant ornamentation put on them by the illuminator and the binder.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Illuminated Manuscripts: Art and Investment

The medieval illuminated manuscript was as much an artwork and a status symbol as a vehicle for text. De Vinne describes the process in detail: “Each letter was carefully drawn, and filled in or painted with repeated touches of the pen.” The finest vellum was selected; the standard size was demy folio. Black ink was used for the body of the text, red for prominent words and rubrication. “When the copyist had finished his sheet, he passed it to the designer, who sketched the border, pictures and initials. The sheet was then given to the illuminator, who painted it.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The ornamentation was extravagant. “Every inch of space was used. Its broad margins were filled with quaint ornaments, sometimes of high merit, admirably painted in vivid colors.” Grotesque initials “often spanned the full height of the page” and were filled with “angels, butterflies, goblins, clowns, birds, snails and monkeys” — sometimes in highly inappropriate settings, even in copies of the Gospels. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Binding was equally sumptuous. Books were covered in silk, velvet, satin, or bright-colored leather, “embroidered with gold and pearls, studded with buttons of gold, banded on the corners with shields, and secured with clasps of precious metals.” Charlemagne presented a church with a copy of the Evangelists covered in plates of gold and silver, studded with gems. Books of this quality were less reading tools than treasured possessions — “the true office of the book was perverted,” De Vinne observes; “it was regarded, not as a medium of instruction, but as a means for the display of wealth and artistic tastes.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The Rise of the Stationer and the University Book Trade

As universities emerged in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries — Paris, Bologna, Oxford, Padua — the demand for books outgrew what monastic scriptoria could supply. A new figure appeared: the stationarius or librarius, a lay professional who produced and sold books in university towns.

The system that developed around the University of Paris was the most elaborate. De Vinne notes: “Through its clerical teachers, the church claimed the right to control the making, buying and selling of books. It extended its authority over parchment-makers, bookbinders, and every other class of mechanics that contributed in any way to their manufacture.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] The stationers were required to take an oath each year, to tell the truth about prices, and to sell only at rates fixed by four master booksellers appointed by the university. No book could be bought for resale until it had been exposed for five days in the Hall of the University and declined by all teachers and scholars. The profit on each sale was fixed at four deniers for a teacher or scholar, six deniers for the public.

Lorck adds that the stationers in Paris were officers of the university, subject to strict oversight: “Ohne Vorwissen des Rektors durften sie einem Studenten nichts abkaufen, mussten schworen, reell zu sein und dem Kaufer nur den 40. Pfennig als Gewinn abzunehmen” (Without the rector’s knowledge they could buy nothing from a student; they had to swear to be honest and to take only the 40th penny as profit from the buyer). ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] Under such constraints, the stationers barely survived. Many supplemented their income by selling furs, haberdashery, or by working as parchment-dressers and binders.

The pecia system emerged at Bologna and Paris to speed production. Authorized copies of university texts were divided into separate gatherings (peciae), which could be rented out to multiple copyists simultaneously, allowing a text to be reproduced in parallel. This was the closest the manuscript world came to an assembly line.

In Germany, the trade developed more freely: “Die Produktion der Manuskripte und der Handel mit denselben entwickelten sich deshalb auch in Deutschland viel freier, manchmal selbst an Orten, wo keine innere Veranlassung vorlag, so in dem Stadtchen Hagenau (um 1430)” (The production of manuscripts and their trade developed much more freely in Germany, sometimes even in places where there was no internal reason for it, such as the town of Hagenau around 1430). ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Burgundian Court Library and the Great Collections

The fourteenth and fifteenth centuries saw an explosion of secular book collecting among the European nobility. The most magnificent collection was that of the Dukes of Burgundy. John Man describes the context: the Burgundian court, along with the Flemish cities, was a “Pflanzschule” (seedbed) of culture in the North. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Charles V of France owned over 900 books by 1373 — almost all on fine vellum, superbly bound, adorned with precious stones and clasps of silver or gold. His brother Philip the Bold, Duke of Burgundy, “gathered around him artists, authors, copyists, and bookbinders, and established a great library.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] His successors, John the Fearless and especially Philip the Good, expanded the collection until it was the most magnificent in Western Europe.

These collections were overwhelmingly composed of romances, chronicles, and devotional works — not classical texts. De Vinne notes that in Charles V’s library, the catalogue shows “romances, legends, histories, and treatises on astrology, geometry and chiromancy.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The Decline of Monastic Copying

By the thirteenth century, the monastic monopoly on book production was eroding. The church had grown rich and worldly; the ascetic impulse that had driven the scriptoria faded. De Vinne describes a church that “had secured a position of supremacy in temporal as well as spiritual matters; it had grown rich, and showed disregard for the spiritual and educational means by which its successes had been made.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The rise of the Mendicant Friars, founded by Francis of Assisi, paradoxically accelerated the decline. Francis himself suffered none of his followers to have Bible, breviary, or psalter. The friars were initially popular for ministering where the beneficed clergy had grown neglectful, but “the leaders of the friars were men of piety… but the inferior clergy, with few exceptions, were extremely ignorant.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] The class of monks who had devoted their lives to copying imitated the example of the pleasure-loving friars. “The care of libraries was neglected for pleasures of a grosser nature. The duties of copyists and librarians passed, gradually and almost imperceptibly, into the hands of the laity.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The Black Death of 1347–1351 dealt a devastating blow. John Man describes how the plague killed roughly a third of Europe’s population — in some places over 60 percent. In Mainz, some 10,000 people died, half the city’s inhabitants. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] This catastrophic demographic collapse destroyed the supply of trained scribes, radically increased the cost of scribal labor, and created the economic vacuum that printing would fill.

The Rise of Lay Copyists and Commercial Production

As monastic copying declined, lay copyists filled the gap. The twelfth century saw the first signs: “During the twelfth century the ecclesiastical monopoly of book-making began to give way.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] Professional copyists, illuminators, and bookbinders formed guilds in the cities.

By the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, a sophisticated commercial manuscript trade had developed. Lorck records that manuscript dealers — “die noch lange nach der Erfindung der Buchdruckerkunst fortbestanden” (who continued long after the invention of printing) — visited fairs and markets, and the manuscript trade flourished alongside the early book trade even in Frankfurt. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The new secular literature of the troubadours and romances created a new kind of book. “To please their dainty tastes, copies were made with refinements of calligraphy never before attempted; the unwieldy sizes of folio and quarto were supplanted by small and handy duodecimos, and bindings of a more delicate character were introduced.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] The miniaturist — a courtly artist, not a monk — became the key figure in book illustration. “The artists were called miniaturists because their illustrations were miniature pictures, as artistically designed, and always more carefully painted than larger paintings made for the adornment of churches.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The Tension Between Manuscript and Print

When printing emerged in the 1450s, it did not immediately replace manuscript production. The two coexisted for decades — indeed, the manuscript trade continued in some forms into the seventeenth century. But the relationship was fraught.

The first printed books were deliberately designed to look like manuscripts — a phenomenon scholars call incunable consciousness. Early printers used Gothic typefaces that closely imitated scribal handwriting, left spaces for hand-rubricated initials, omitted title pages (beginning instead with the traditional incipit), and imitated scribal abbreviations and ligatures. As the incunabula page notes: “Incunabula are not fully modern books. They are ‘cradle-prints’ — objects caught between two worlds: the scribal and the mechanical.” Gutenberg understood that his market would only accept a printed Bible that matched or exceeded the beauty of manuscript copies.

But the economic logic of print was inexorable. John Man captures the scale: “In 1455 all Europe’s printed books could have been carried in a single wagon. Fifty years later, the titles ran to tens of thousands, the individual volumes to millions.” ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] A manuscript Bible that cost a farm could be replaced by a printed Bible costing a fraction — Fust sold copies of his folio Bible in Paris in 1462 for 50 crowns, against the 400–500 crowns typical for a manuscript of the same size. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Scribal Resistance to Printing

The scribes and illuminators did not yield without a struggle. De Vinne records two notable incidents of organized resistance:

First, in Venice: “Italy is indebted to the skill of German printers, whose early productions had been excluded from Venice at the petition of her querulous card-makers.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] The card-makers (playing-card illuminators) and manuscript illuminators saw their livelihoods threatened and petitioned the Signoria to exclude printed books.

Second, in Genoa: similar resistance occurred in 1472, though the details are sparse in the surviving record.

More broadly, De Vinne argues that the very refinement of manuscript culture was “one of the most formidable impediments in [printing’s] path.” The wealthy patrons who loved fine manuscripts had a “pitying contempt” for the “coarse tastes” that could be satisfied with printed work. “To the dainty tastes of book-makers printing was a barbarous trade; to the wealthy book-buyer, a printed book would have been the degradation of art and literature.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Yet the economic logic prevailed. As the number of readers grew, the demand for books outstripped what scribes could supply. The university system, the rise of vernacular literacy, the growth of a merchant class that could read — all these created a market that only print could serve.

The Replacement of Scribal Labor

By 1500, the transition was largely complete. Lorck notes that one sign of the changed landscape was that even printed books were sometimes copied by hand afterward — “ein Beweis, dass die gedruckten Bucher damals noch teuer waren und dass man in den Klostern immer noch Zeit ubrig hatte” (proof that printed books were still expensive at that time and that there was still time left over in the monasteries). ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

But the scribe’s world had ended. The great scriptoria of the Benedictine abbeys became museums of a lost craft. The illuminators who had once painted initials on vellum turned to woodcut illustration or found work decorating the luxury copies that still circulated among the very rich. The stationers who had rented peciae became booksellers. And the readers who had once waited months for a single hand-copied volume could now buy a printed book at the Frankfurt fair or from a traveling agent.

Manuscript culture did not vanish overnight — elaborate illuminated manuscripts continued to be produced for princely patrons well into the sixteenth century, and the art of fine calligraphy survived in chanceries and scriptoria. But after Gutenberg, the book was no longer a unique object. It was a multiple, a commodity, a technology. The world of the scribe had given way to the world of the printer.

The Commercial Manuscript Trade

As the monastic monopoly on book production declined and lay literacy grew, an organised commercial trade in manuscripts developed across Europe. George Haven Putnam’s detailed survey of this trade, drawing on Kirchhoff, Wattenbach, and other nineteenth-century scholars, documents a sophisticated network of scribes, dealers, patrons, and markets stretching from Sicily to Scotland. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Rise of Lay Scribes Outside Monasteries

The first signs of a lay scribal trade appear in Italy as early as the seventh century. Giesebrecht refers to a manuscript of Orosius written in the seventh century containing an inscription stating that it was prepared “in the Statione Magistri Viliaric Antiquarii” — one of the earliest recorded uses of the term statio, from which stationarius (bookseller with a fixed workshop) derives. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

By the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, professional scribes were working in cities across Europe, distinct from the monastic scriptoria. In Milan, records from the mid-fourteenth century show no fewer than forty professional scribes at work. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] Wattenbach finds organised manuscript businesses in Verona as early as 1338 and in Milan at the same period. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The shift from commissioned to speculative production is visible in the manuscripts themselves. Up to the close of the fourteenth century, most transcripts were prepared for individual buyers under special orders, as evidenced by the coats of arms or crests painted on title pages. After the beginning of the fifteenth century, many manuscripts appear with blank spaces left on the title pages for the subsequent insertion of arms — indicating that these copies had been prepared for general sale rather than by commission. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Not all manuscript dealers were dedicated booksellers. Kirchhoff speaks of usurers, dealers in old clothes, and pedlars carrying on the trade in books during the first half of the fifteenth century. In Milan, a perfumery dealer named Paolino Suordo included manuscripts in his stock in 1470, and later announced himself as a dealer in printed books. In both England and France at this time, manuscripts were dealt in by grocers and mercers. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Jewish financiers also acted as intermediaries. Monasteries and churches, when in financial distress, frequently pledged or sold their books to Jews, who took them for resale. In 1320, the monastery of St. Ulrich pawned a great collection of valuable books to mendicant monks; the successor Abbot, Conrad Winkler, recovered a portion in 1344 for 27 pounds heller and 15 pfennigs. In 1400, a scribe named Moses, specified as a Jew, sold a manuscript of Bartholomaeus Brixiensis in Bologna for eight florins — noteworthy given university regulations prohibiting book sales by Jews. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Burgundian Court Library and Flemish Illuminators

The most magnificent secular manuscript collections of the fifteenth century were assembled by the Dukes of Burgundy. Philip the Bold (1342–1404) and his successors John the Fearless and Philip the Good transformed their court into the premier centre of manuscript illumination in Northern Europe.

In 1386, Philip the Bold paid Martin L’Huillier, a manuscript dealer and bookbinder, sixteen francs for binding eight books, six of which were bound in grain leather. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] The Duke of Orleans was an equally avid collector: in 1394 he paid twenty francs in gold to Jehan de Marsan, master of arts and manuscript dealer, for the Letters of St. Pol, bound in figured silk and illuminated with the Duke’s arms; four years later he paid one hundred livres tournois for a Latin Concordance to the Bible, an illuminated manuscript bound in red leather. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Lombard banker Dyne Rapond became one of the most important suppliers to the Burgundian court, treating manuscripts as luxury goods alongside tapestries and shirts. In 1399, Rapond delivered to Philip of Burgundy a Livy illuminated with letters of gold for five hundred livres, and a work entitled La Propriete de Choses for six thousand francs. Charles VI of France bought from Rapond the Chronicles of France in four volumes, a manuscript of Seneca, and the Chronicles of Charlemagne, Pepin, and Godefroy de Bouillon. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] Jacques Rapond, probably Dyne’s brother, sold Philip a French Bible for 9,000 francs and a copy of The Golden Legend for 7,500 francs in 1400. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Flemish cities of Bruges, Ghent, and Antwerp became the premier centres for illuminated manuscript production. The Breviary of Grimani, produced in Bruges in 1478 by artists including John Memmling, was purchased in 1489 by Cardinal Grimani for five hundred ducats. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] A guild of publishers (librariers gild) was instituted in Ghent in 1424, and one in Brussels a year or two later, which came into relations with the St. Lucas Guild in Antwerp in 1450. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] The manuscripts produced in the Netherlands and Burgundy far surpassed those of the rest of the world in beauty and artistic finish. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Prices of Books

Manuscript prices varied enormously depending on materials, illumination, and market conditions. The most detailed surviving records come from Italy, France, and Germany between the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries.

Specific recorded prices include:

  • In 1260 in Bologna, a monk received two hundred florins gold (approximately one hundred dollars) for transcribing and illuminating a missal with original designs. For copying the text of the Bible without designs, another scribe received eighty lire (about sixteen dollars) in the same year. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

  • In 1345, Etienne de Conty paid 62 livres and 11 sous (equivalent to 825 francs in later currency) for a handsomely adorned copy of the Commentaries of Henry Bohic. The breakdown: scribal work cost 31 livres 5 sous, parchment 18 livres 18 sous, six gold initials 1 livre 10 sous, other illuminations 3 livres 6 sous, hire of the manuscript (paid to the university beadle) 4 livres, and binding 1 livre 12 sous. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

  • A good copy of the Corpus Juris was valued at 1,000 gold gulden in 1350. In 1427, a copy of Livy sold for 120 gold gulden. In 1470, a Plutarch fetched no less than 800 gold gulden — twenty years after Gutenberg’s press began working. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

  • In 1460, Jan Van Enkhuisen of Zwolle received 500 gold gulden for an illuminated Bible and 100 crowns for a plain-text Bible. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

  • The Countess of Anjou paid, in 1460, for a copy of the Homilies of Haimon, Bishop of Halberstadt, two hundred sheep, five measures of wheat, and five measures of barley. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Price declines accelerated as printing approached. At the end of the fourteenth century, the average price in Italy for a well-written copy of the Corpus Juris was 480 marks. In 1451, such a copy sold in Florence for 14.5 ducats (90 marks). In 1467, a copy of the comedies of Terence on 198 folio sheets (paper, not parchment) was bought in Heidelberg for three gulden. In 1499, a manuscript of the Hecuba of Euripides and the Idyls of Theocritus on 134 quarto sheets sold in Heidelberg for two gulden. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

At the humbler end of the market, the school ordinance of Bautzen (Saxony) in 1418 fixed the price of an ABC book containing a Paternoster at one groschen, a Doctrinal at half a mark, and a Donatus at ten groschens. At this time, a hen cost one pfennig, a pound of beef two pfennigs, and a loaf of bread three pfennigs. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Commercial Manuscript Dealers in Italy

The earliest Italian manuscript trade was centred in Rome, which was “practically the only book market in the world” from the seventh century. Abbots from England and Gaul sent agents to Rome to purchase books: St. Gertrude of Nivelle ordered sacred volumes from Rome in 658; the Abbot Benedict of Wearmouth secured books from Rome in 671, 678, and 685. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

By the fifteenth century, Florence had surpassed Venice as the centre of the manuscript trade for Italy and Europe. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] The wealth of the city, its cultivated citizens, its convenient location, and the accumulation of manuscripts in libraries like those of the Medici all contributed to Florentine pre-eminence. Scholars from France, Germany, and Hungary came to Florence to consult manuscripts that could be found only there, or to purchase transcripts produced with greater correctness and at smaller expense than anywhere else. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Vespasiano da Bisticci (Philippi Vespasianus) was the most important manuscript publisher of the period, “not only the most important publisher of the manuscript period, but one of the great scholars of his time.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] He called himself librarius florentinus and held the post of beadle of the University of Florence. For Cosimo de’ Medici, he prepared a collection of two hundred works, employing forty-five scribes for twenty-two months. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] His clients included the Duke of Burgundy and Matthias Corvinus of Hungary, who kept scribes resident in Florence to prepare transcripts. Codices produced by Vespasiano are preserved in the Bodleian Library in Oxford, bearing his inscription “Vespasianus librarius Florentinus hunc librum Florentiae transcribendum curavit.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] He died in 1496, one year after the establishment of the Aldine Press.

Johannes Aurispa (1369–1459) was “by far the most important of the manuscript-dealers of his time and possibly of the entire Middle Ages.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] Born in Sicily, he spent his early years in Constantinople, where he organised a staff of skilled scribes. In 1423, he came to Florence with 238 manuscripts. His shop produced works by Philo Judaeus, Strabo, Demosthenes, Homer, Sophocles, Aeschylus, Pindar, Aristotle, Plutarch, Cicero, Virgil, and others. Filelfo complained that “no book ever leaves your book-shop, except at a profit to you.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] In 1450, Aurispa gave up his business, took priestly orders, and lived as a scriba apostolicus.

Other notable Italian dealers include:

  • Johannes Aretinus (1375–1417) in Florence, a friend of Petrarch, who produced nine valued manuscripts including works by Aristotle, Cicero, and Livy. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

  • Johannes Galeotti of Genoa, who made journeys to Constantinople importing manuscripts to Venice. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

  • Agnolo da Sandro, a manuscript dealer in Florence as late as 1498. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

  • Niccolo di Giunta, active in the manuscript trade in Florence towards the end of the fifteenth century, founder of the Giunta family of printers and publishers. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

  • Bonaventura in Verona, a scriptor who occasionally employed a woman scribe. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

  • Melchoir in Milan (1452), described as a “dealer of note,” who had copies of Cicero’s Letters for sale at ten ducats each. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

  • Jacob the Jew in Padua (1455–1460), who carried on an active manuscript business despite university regulations against Jewish dealers. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

  • Carnerio, a bibliopola in Ferrara between 1440 and 1490. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Venice remained the headquarters for Greek manuscripts for many years, owing to its close relations with Constantinople. The manuscript dealers of Venice fixed their places of business at the Bridge of the Rialto and the Plaza of St. Mark. There was evidence of active trade between Venice and England for years before the introduction of the printing press. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Manuscript Trade in France

In France, the trade in manuscripts was dominated by the University of Paris, which retained control over the book trade even after the introduction of printing. Kirchhoff traces the beginning of this trade to the second half of the eleventh century. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Jean de Garland, compiling a directory of Parisian industries in 1060, notes: “Paravisus est locus ubi libri scholarium vendentur” (Parvis is the place where scholars’ books are sold), referring to the square near Notre Dame that later became the centre of the Parisian book trade. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] Peter of Blois, writing in the mid-twelfth century, speaks of the great collections of valuable books that Parisian dealers had for sale. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The earliest Paris bookseller whose name is recorded is Herneis le Romanceur, who had his shop at the entrance to Notre Dame in the early thirteenth century. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] By 1292, eight licensed booksellers had their workshops in the Rue Neuve Notre Dame. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] By 1448, the number of certified librarii in Paris had increased to twenty-four. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The university imposed strict regulations. Licensed dealers (libraires jures) were university members exempt from royal taxation. Unlicensed pedlars were forbidden to sell any volume for more than ten sous, limiting them to chapbooks and broadsides, and could not trade in covered shops, only open booths. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Notable French dealers include:

  • Nicholas L’Anglois (1313), bookseller and tavern-keeper in Rue St. Jacques. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

  • Dyne Rapond, a Lombard banker in Paris whose principal business was banking but who accepted orders for luxury goods including books. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

  • Nicholas Flamel, scribe and librarius juratus at the beginning of the thirteenth century, who used his bookselling profits to speculate in real estate. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

  • Jean Bonhomme (1486–1490), who called himself “bookseller to the university” and dealt in both manuscripts and printed books. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Outside Paris, provincial centres included Tours, Angers, Lille, Troyes, Rouen, Toulouse, and Montpellier. In Montpellier, a business of loaning manuscripts and peciae similar to that of Bologna developed early in the fourteenth century. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Manuscript Trade in Germany

The German manuscript trade was less developed than in Italy or France, due to the lesser wealth of German nobles and the narrower range of scholarship in German universities. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] As Richard de Bury’s account makes clear, English and French collectors looked to Italy and Paris, not to German cities, for manuscripts. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The oldest German university, Prague (founded 1347), placed scribes, illuminators, and book dealers under university control. Vienna (1365) had stationarii hiring out peciae. Heidelberg (1386) modelled its book trade regulations on those of Paris. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The most remarkable German figure was Diebold Lauber (or Lauber) of Hagenau, a schoolmaster turned scribe and publisher. He organised an active manuscript factory between 1443 and 1450, producing not only “good Latin books” but German epics, sagas, folk songs, chapbooks, fortune-telling books, and Bible stories in the vernacular. His advertisement read: “Any books that are desired, whether great or small, religious or profane, beautifully painted, all of these will one find by Diepold Lauber, scribe in the town of Hagenau.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] The Hagenau scribal schools became famous, and one of the first printing presses outside Mainz was later established there. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Other German dealers include:

  • Conrad Horn, a stadtschreiber in Nordlingen who carried on an extensive business in manuscript production and exchange. In 1427, he contracted to purchase a book for forty-three Rhenish gulden. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

  • Wolff von Prunow, a bibliopola in Heidelberg early in the fifteenth century. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

  • Joh. Minner, a scriptor in Nordlingen (1407), who sold a German translation of the Decretals to the Burgermeister. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

  • Peter von Haselo, a scribe who sold books on the steps of Strasbourg Cathedral in 1408. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The annual fairs at Salzburg, Ulm, Nordlingen, and especially Frankfurt, were crucial for the manuscript trade. It was from its trade in manuscripts that Frankfurt “by natural development, became and for many years remained the centre of the trade in printed books.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] The earliest record of printed books sold at the Nordlingen fair dates from 1470, and at the Frankfurt fair from 1480. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Brothers of Common Life played a distinctive role in Germany and the Low Countries, producing manuscripts in the vernacular for the common folk. “Their service as instructors and booksellers was probably one of the most important influences in helping to educate the lower classes of North Germany to read and to think for themselves.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] They later took up printing with equal promptness.

The Manuscript Trade in Spain

The manuscript trade in Spain was much less developed than in Italy or France, hampered by the long wars with the Moors. The University of Salamanca (founded c. 1220) was the principal market for Italian manuscript dealers. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Under the Moors, however, Cordova had been a great literary centre. The Khalif Al-hakem (tenth century) employed agents to collect books from distant countries, and his library is said to have contained 400,000 volumes, with a catalogue of forty-four volumes of twenty sheets each. He employed skilled scribes, binders, and illuminators in his palace. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] After the Spanish conquest of Cordova in 1236, this tradition was largely destroyed.

The only recorded Spanish manuscript dealer of the fifteenth century is Antonius Raymundi, a librarius of Barcelona whose inscription, dated 1413, appears in a manuscript of Cassiodorus. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Legacy

The commercial manuscript trade did not disappear overnight with the arrival of printing. Older scholars still preferred manuscripts, and there are instances of scribes preparing manuscript copies from printed books — even including the printer’s imprint. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] As late as 1498, manuscript dealers like Agnolo da Sandro were still active in Florence. But by 1500, the economic logic of print had decisively shifted the centre of the book trade from scribe to printer. The Frankfurt fair, which had begun as a market for manuscripts, became the great European book fair for printed works. The scribal workshops of Hagenau, the illuminators of Bruges, and the librarii of Paris and Florence gave way to a new world — but the trade routes, the commercial practices, and the readership they had built were the foundation on which that world was built.
