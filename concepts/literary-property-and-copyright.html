Literary Property and Copyright

This page covers the legal/commercial evolution of copyright as property from privilege to right, as documented in Putnam, Books and Their Makers in the Middle Ages, Vol. II, Part III (“The Beginnings of Property in Literature”).

I. The Venetian Privilege System

The term “privilegii” covered all copyrights, patents, monopolies, and special concessions for books and printing. Between 1469 and 1517 these were usually granted by the College (Cabinet) of Venice, recorded in its Minutes; occasionally the Senate conferred them, or chiefs of the Council. Privileges were freely granted but not obligatory. (Putnam II, 2473-2476)

1. John of Speyer’s Monopoly (1469)

The earliest Venetian (and probably Italian) privilege on record. A five-year monopoly to print books in Venice. John died shortly after securing it; it was not continued to his heirs, leaving Jenson, Nicolas of Frankfort, and associates free to operate. The privilege included a provision prohibiting importation of books printed elsewhere. (Putnam II, 2477, 2497)

2. Sabellico’s Author Copyright (1486)

The earliest record of an author’s copyright in Venice (and Italy). Dated September 1, 1486. Secured to Antonio Sabellico, historian of the Republic, the sole right to publish—or authorize publication of—his Decades rerum Venetarium, for apparently an indefinite term. Penalty for infringement: 500 ducats. The concession established a precedent of literary proprietorship for the author. (Putnam II, 2479-2481)

A more formal recognition followed in 1493: Daniele Barbaro received a ten-year copyright for his deceased brother Hermolao Barbaro’s Castigationes Plinii. In January 1492, Peter of Ravenna secured an indefinite-term copyright for his Phoenix (sometimes misidentified as the earliest author copyright; Sabellico’s predates it by six years). (Putnam II, 2483)

3. Aldus’s Privileges (1495/1501)

  • 1495 (or 1496): Aldus received a 20-year privilege for all books he might print in Greek text, plus a patent-right for his Greek type and printing method. The petition stated he had invested a large part of his resources in making the Greek fonts. (Putnam II, 2489)
  • 1501: Aldus obtained a ten-year copyright for all works printed in cursive/italic character, whose invention he claimed; the advantage was compactness. The Greek type was modeled on the script of Musurus; the cursive was a facsimile of Boccaccio’s writing. (Putnam II, 2491)

4. The Four Privilege Classes

First class — Simple monopoly: Government granted the beneficiary sole right to print or sell a whole class of books for a term of years. Example: John of Speyer, 1469. (Putnam II, 2477)

Second class — Author’s copyright: Secured to an author the copyright in his own production, recognizing literary property and producers’ rights. Earliest: Sabellico, 1486. (Putnam II, 2479)

Third class — Editor/publisher copyright: For works not of the holder’s own literary production. Earliest: Joannes Nigro, 1493, for his edition of Haliabas. Abuses soon arose—copyrights were secured for works never intended to be issued, used to extract payments from bona fide publishers. (Putnam II, 2485, 2487)

Fourth class — Patent privilege: Protection for improvements in the art of printing or specific classes of literature. Examples: Aldus’s Greek-type privilege (1495/6), Aldus’s italic-type privilege (1501), Terracina’s 25-year monopoly (1498) for all books in Arabic, Moorish, Syriac, Armenian, Indian, and Barbary. (Putnam II, 2489-2493)

Additional class — Protective privilege: Protected Venetian printing against foreign competition. Importation of foreign editions of copyrighted works was prohibited. After 1519, a condition appeared requiring the book to be printed in Venetian territory. (Putnam II, 2495-2499)

Terms and Enforcement

Average term of early privileges: ten years. Later 16th century saw longer terms (20 years in 1569, 24-25 in 1596). Terms varied from one to twenty-five years. After 1505, prolongations were granted for good cause (e.g., Leonardo Crasso’s edition of Polifilo received a second ten-year term in 1508 because wars had prevented him from recovering his investment). (Putnam II, 2515-2517)

No machinery existed to enforce privileges. Brown states there is no record of a suit by one printer against another for infringement. Complaints went to the College or Council of Ten. Penalties: confiscation and fines from 20 soldi to 1,000 ducats per copy (the latter for a pirated Ariosto in 1515), rarely imprisonment or suspension. Fines split three ways: court, complainant, city charity. (Putnam II, 2517-2523)

The College kept no register; no subject indexes; publishers had no safe means to check for prior copyrights. Duplicate privileges for the same work were sometimes granted to different applicants; earliest date prevailed if publication conditions were met. Saving clauses later became standard. (Putnam II, 2509-2513)

II. The German Imperial Privilege System

Sodalitas Rhenana (1501)

The earliest German privilege on trustworthy record was issued in 1501 by the Imperial Council, not to an individual but to the Sodalitas Rhenana Celtica (Rhenish Celtic Society) for an edition of the dramas of Hroswitha of Gandersheim (written c. 985), prepared by Conrad Celtes. This privilege is later than the early Venetian privileges but antedates the first French instance by two years and the first English by seventeen years. After 1501, a long series of imperial privileges issued by the Imperial Chancellor in the Emperor’s name followed. (Putnam II, 2913)

In 1512, an imperial privilege was issued to historiographer John Stadius for all that he should print—the first European privilege covering more than a single work or protecting books not yet published. No German privileges similar to Venice’s class-of-works or whole-language privileges have been found. (Putnam II, 2913)

Frankfurt Ordinances

The Frankfort ordinance of 1660 distinguished between Bücher (books) and Autores (authors). Local privileges were divided into two classes: “for works/books” and “for writers/authors.” (Putnam II, 2913)

The imperial commission appointed in 1569 to supervise the Frankfort Book-Fair (at the request of Maximilian II) exercised influence over the book trade centered there. In 1579, Rudolph II directed the clerk of the Imperial Court and the Dean of the Frankfort Convent to serve as the first members. The commission’s operations were largely controlled by Catholic interests; its oppressive censorship contributed to the shift of publishing from Frankfort to Protestant Leipzig. (Putnam II, 2953-2955)

Imperial privileges nominally protected books throughout the Holy Roman Empire, but authorities like Pütter and Kapp concluded they were largely disregarded by reprinters, except in the imperial cities (Reichsstädte). (Putnam II, 3291)

Luther’s Role

Luther was probably the first German author to draw attention to literary piracy and to argue for legislation recognizing property rights of literary producers. He held that the system of privileges was inadequate—its tendency was to narrow rather than widen the conception of literary property. He emphasized the author’s right to control editions to preserve textual accuracy (Text-Verfälschung). His interest in textual correctness was keener than in author remuneration; he even helped correct proofs for unauthorized editions. Writing in 1524: “Des falschen Druckens und Bücherverderbens fleissingen sich jetzt viele” (“Many now busy themselves with spoiling books through misprinting them”). (Putnam II, 2885-2889)

III. The French System

First Privilege (1503)

The first privilege in France was issued in 1503. The official censorship of publications began in 1515 with Francis I’s accession. Earlier “permits” under Louis XII were not the result of official examination by censors and involved no restrictions on publishers. (Putnam II, 3281)

Ordinance of 1537 (Legal Deposit)

The ordinance of March 7, 1537, provided:

  1. No work could be printed in the kingdom in any language (Latin, Greek, Hebrew, Arabic, Chaldee, Italian, Spanish, German, French, or others) without the King’s approval given through the royal librarian. (Putnam II, 3077)
  2. A copy of each printed book must be deposited in the library of the royal château of Blois, delivered to the Abbé Mellin de Saint-Gelais or deputies in each university and important city, with a certificate of delivery. (Putnam II, 3079)
  3. A copy of each book printed abroad and offered for sale in France must similarly be submitted for inspection; sale authorized only if found worthy of the Royal Library. Payment was made at the same price as charged to others. (Putnam II, 3081-3083)

The ground given for collection was preservation for future generations and protection against textual alteration. This edict was the first step toward a national library and the earliest French example of securing publication authorization in consideration of a tax of one copy for the nation. (Putnam II, 3083)

A second ordinance of March 20, 1537, gave further details; a 1538 ordinance appointed Conrad Néobar royal printer for Greek, requiring: (1) religious works approved by the Faculty of Theology, profane works by the Faculty of Belles-Lettres; (2) a copy of each work to the Royal Library; (3) Néobar’s books to announce him as “printer to the King.” He received 100 gold crowns yearly, tax exemption, an exclusive monopoly/copyright of five years for first-published books and two years for revised editions. (Putnam II, 3085-3095)

Terms

The term of privileges in France began at five years under Francis I and was gradually extended; by Henry IV’s reign the average through routine renewal was twenty years. (Putnam II, 3283)

The ordinance of Moulins (1566), issued by Charles IX, brought privileges and permits together and is often cited as the beginning of France’s copyright system: it required royal permission and letters of privilege under the great seal, and required the printer to insert his name, place, and the privilege in the book, on penalty of loss of goods and corporal punishment. (Putnam II, 3101)

IV. The English System

Pynson’s Privilege (1518)

The first privilege in England bears date 1518, issued to Richard Pynson, King’s Printer. The full wording: “Cum privilegio impressa a rege indulto, ne quis hanc orationem intra biennium in regno Angliae imprimat aut alibi impressam et importatam in eodem regno Angliae vendat” (a monopoly for two years for a certain Orationem). This is fifteen years after the first French privilege. The two-year term was notably short; most French and Italian privileges were for five to ten years. Pynson printed simply “cum privilegio a rege indulto” without term limit. (Putnam II, 3217-3219)

In 1530, a seven-year privilege was granted to an author in consideration of the value of his work—the first English copyright granted to an author. The first English copyright dispute on record arose c. 1530 when printer Trevers reprinted a work privileged to Wynkyn de Worde. (Putnam II, 3219-3221)

Stationers’ Company (1556)

The Stationers’ Company received its charter by royal decree in 1556 (two years after Mary’s marriage to Philip of Spain). It organized the printing and publishing trade of London, holding authority directly from the Crown—unlike the Paris libraires jurés, who were University members. The Company had no direct connection with Oxford or Cambridge. (Putnam II, 3213)

After 1556, entries in the Stationers’ registers became evidence of exclusive rights to print specified books. Royal privileges eventually ceased, superseded by the Company’s registers. The entries specified no term; property rights were thus indefinite and claimable in perpetuity. The registers served like land records for transfers and original ownership certification, with ownership understood to be based on Common Law and in perpetuity. (Putnam II, 3223)

The Stationers’ Company possessed the monopoly of printing throughout the United Kingdom; only its members could print. Its by-laws, where not conflicting with statutes, became law. The purpose: “The order and regulation of printing and of printing-presses in the interests of Church and State.” (Putnam II, 3225)

Star Chamber and the Licensing Act

The ordinance of 1643 renewed in substance the decrees of the Star Chamber (abolished in 1640). Milton defied it by publishing his Doctrine and Discipline of Divorce without license or printer’s name. In 1644, Parliament (the Long Parliament) considered an order that “No Book, Pamphlet, or Paper, shall be henceforth printed unless the same be first approved and licensed by such as shall be thereto appointed.” Milton’s Areopagitica was his protest against government licensing and the delegation of literary control to a commercial company created by government. In it he distinguished between “that part which preserves justly every man’s copy to himself” and “that other clause of Licensing Books.” (Putnam II, 3235-3239)

Copyright Act of 1709 (Act of Queen Anne)

The Act of April 1710 (Queen Anne’s Act) gave statutory protection for a term of fourteen years to the author of a literary production. This brought to a close the period of Common Law copyright in Great Britain, a result probably unintended by the legislators and unanticipated by the publishers who had sought it. They had applied for more specific protection during such term as Parliament saw fit to grant for property in their “copies,” whose existence had not yet been questioned. (Putnam II, 3227)

Millar v. Taylor (1769)

Not until 1769 was any serious contention raised against the continued validity of copyright at Common Law. In Millar v. Taylor, the Common Law right was maintained. The decision was noteworthy because Lord Mansfield, “the greatest authority on the subject of copyright whom Europe had thus far known,” concurred. (Putnam II, 3227)

Donaldson v. Becket (1774)

In 1774, the issue was raised a second time in Donaldson v. Becket, involving the same property (the copyright of Thomson’s Seasons). The House of Lords reversed its previous decision. The conclusions:

  1. An author had a Common Law right to his production before publication (10 affirmative, 1 dissenting).
  2. After publication, such Common Law right still rested in the author (8 affirmative, 3 negative).
  3. Under the statute of 1710, the author had lost his right of action at Common Law and retained protection only during the statutory term (6 affirmative, 5 negative — the critical vote).
  4. The right at Common Law prior to 1710 had been a right in perpetuity (7 affirmative, 4 negative).

Lord Mansfield voted on each issue in favor of the continued Common Law right and the perpetuity of copyright. (Putnam II, 3229)

This decision replaced Common Law copyright with protection limited to successive statutory terms—a development in England that had not yet been reached in any European state. France saw no such discussions or legislation based on literary property principles until the Convention of 1793. (Putnam II, 3231)

V. Summary of the Developmental Arc

As Putnam summarizes: the first kind of copyright in medieval Europe inhered in the Common Law control of property in a manuscript—a copyright having nothing to do with the rights of an original producer. While the first printing of books was done in Germany, the beginning of copyright protection for printed books is credited to Italy. Gutenberg, Fust, and their successors entered upon publishing without government protection. The Venetian privilege system (originating in 1469) was adopted by other Italian states and followed in substance by the rest of Europe. The privileges of the 15th and 16th centuries were the precursor and foundation of the later system of copyright. (Putnam II, 3261-3267)

By the close of the 17th century, the conception of literary property had not reached any very advanced stage. The decisive discussions came in England in 1769 and 1774, in France in 1793, and in Germany later (the Prussian Act of 1837 forming the model for other German states). (Putnam II, 3321, 3327-3329)

The fourth of the theories that emerged from these discussions—that property in an intellectual production is the creation of statute and subject to limitations based on community convenience—represents the survival that formed the basis of copyright legislation in both Europe and America. (Putnam II, 3337-3339)
