Printing Economics

Caption: Early print shop economics, showing capital, paper supply, type metal, skilled labor, patronage, buyers, fairs, binders, booksellers, and unsold inventory.

Early European printing was a high-fixed-cost business. A printer needed punches, matrices, type metal, presses, paper or vellum, ink, trained compositors, pressmen, correctors, and months or years of labor before a large book produced revenue. This is why johann-gutenberg needed johannes-fust’s capital, and why the 1455 lawsuit is as much an economic event as a biographical drama. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Cost Structure

The chief costs of a fifteenth-century printing shop were up-front and material: cutting punches, striking matrices, casting fonts, building or acquiring presses, buying paper or vellum, paying skilled labor, and financing stock until it could be sold. Large folios such as the gutenberg-bible required especially heavy investment because paper, vellum, type, rubrication, and long press time had to be paid for before buyers completed payment. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Capital Equipment

The press itself was surprisingly cheap and readily available. In 1474 Peter the Baker of Padua sold a wooden, fully equipped printing press for 100 lire — just over sixteen ducats. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:579-584] The real expense was type. A well-equipped workshop could be far more costly: Nicholas Jenson valued the contents of his workshop at 500 ducats in his will, specifically excluding the steel punches which he bequeathed separately to a partner. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:603-606] Aldus’s type designs — Greek, Latin cursive (italic), and Hebrew — were all largely new, and their cost must be reckoned in thousands rather than hundreds of ducats. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:4559-4563] For comparison, in 1541 Claude Garamond was paid 225 Livres Tournois (about one-third of Leonardo da Vinci’s annual pension) for cutting Robert Estienne’s Greek Royal types. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:4554-4558]

Subcontracting type-cutting began early. Gutenberg paid 100 gulden to the goldsmith Dunne; in 1475 Johan Rauchfass directed Francesco da Bologna to copy two of Jenson’s Gothic founts. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:626-630]

Paper

Paper-prices varied enormously. The smooth “carta reale” cost five times as much as the cheapest grade, which could be bought for only 2 soldi per quire. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:654-656] A modest press-run of 300 breviaries demanded forty ducats’ worth of paper, which the printer might have to raise on the security of future production — making it easy for the paper-supplier to become a creditor with effective control over a struggling press. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:666-671] By 1500, the market’s voracious demand had allowed paper manufacturers to afford a general reduction of charges. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:656-658]

Labour

Labour was readily available but surprisingly expensive. Only compositors needed any special training; inkers and press-operators could be recruited from among unemployed domestic servants and penniless students. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:674-682] A compositor earned 2.5 to 4.5 ducats per month in Padua during the 1470s — comparable to the 3 ducats per month offered by the Venetian government to a skilled hydraulic engineer in 1492. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:683-688] Press operators earned 2-3 ducats per month, inkers around 2 ducats. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:4891-4892] The master-printer normally also housed and fed his workmen, adding five to fifty ducats per year in rent and exposing him to fluctuating food prices. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:694-698]

Paper and labour together accounted for roughly equal portions of the fifteenth-century printer’s budget. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:652-654]

Paper was economically decisive. Vellum gave prestige but was expensive and limited; paper made larger editions and wider circulation possible. The expansion of printing therefore depended on paper supply as much as on presses or type. Cities such as venice benefited from access to northern Italian paper and commercial transport routes. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Revenue and Risk

Printing multiplied copies, but it also multiplied commercial risk. A manuscript producer could copy one book for one patron; a printer had to estimate demand for dozens or hundreds of copies. Unsold stock tied up capital, as seen in early Roman printing, where Sweynheym and Pannartz reportedly accumulated many unsold classical and patristic books. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The market was wildly unpredictable. Francesco da Madiis, a Venetian bookseller, disposed of more than 12,000 volumes between May 1484 and January 1488, but monthly sales fluctuated between just sixty (October 1485) and 535 (May 1487), with cash takings ranging from thirteen to 210 ducats. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:849-854] Bernardino Stagnino lamented in 1496: “Even when copies are complete, they cannot be sold quickly.” ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:855-857]

The first major market crash came in 1473, when over-production of Latin classics — driven by scholars’ enthusiasm rather than real demand — caused the Venetian industry’s output to decline by sixty-five per cent in a single year. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:743-748] In Rome, Sweynheym and Pannartz claimed to have printed no less than 20,475 copies of their various editions but appealed to the Pope through their editor, reporting that the workshop was “full of printed sheets, empty of necessities.” ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:749-758]

The most reliable early markets were institutions and professional readers: churches, monasteries, universities, lawyers, physicians, clergy, schools, and wealthy lay readers. Bibles, liturgical books, grammars, legal works, indulgences, calendars, and classical texts all served identifiable markets. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Economics of Quality Tiers

The quality spectrum described in incunabula had a direct economic logic. Each tier had different capital requirements, margins, and risk profiles:^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

OctavoModerateType design, smaller pressLower per copy, volumeLowSteady

EphemeralVery lowMinimalPer-unit trivialVery lowImmediate

A printer like Koberger operated across multiple tiers simultaneously: his Nuremberg Chronicle (1493) was a prestige project with 1,800 woodcuts and 600 folio pages, but he also printed Bibles, law books, and school texts. His 24 presses and 100+ workers could be shifted between projects as demand required. By contrast, a small-town printer with one press and a handful of type fonts would stick to jobbing work — indulgences, broadsides, the occasional school text — because he could not afford the months of tied-up capital that a large folio required.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The Economics of Illustration

Illustration added a major layer of cost and complexity. The choice between woodcut and copperplate was not merely technical — it was economic:

  • Woodcut had a fixed cost per block (the carver’s labor) but could be printed on the same press as type and inked in a single pass. The block could be reused indefinitely and across editions — the Nuremberg Chronicle famously reused the same city view for multiple towns. This made woodcut economical for large editions and repeat uses.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]
  • Copperplate required a separate intaglio rolling press, a separate inking process, and much longer to engrave. A single map plate might take months to engrave. The plate could produce only about 1,500-2,000 impressions before wearing out. This made copperplate economical only for premium-priced works like atlases, where fine detail justified the cost.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

The cost of woodcut illustration varied enormously. The Nuremberg Chronicle’s 1,800+ woodcuts required two years of work by Wohlgemut and Pleydenwurff for a fee of 1,000 gulden — roughly 100,000 dollars in modern terms. At the other extreme, a simple woodcut saint’s image could be cut in a day for a few groschen.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Scale and Concentration

Once type and presswork were in place, larger editions lowered the labor cost per copy. This made printing powerful but also pushed printers toward genres with repeatable demand. Small jobbing work such as indulgences and schoolbooks could generate cash flow; large prestige books built reputation but required more capital and exposed the printer to greater risk. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

By the late fifteenth century, the most successful firms operated less like solitary craft shops and more like publishing businesses. Anton Koberger in Nuremberg, Venetian printers, and later Aldine production combined presses, employees, agents, credit, distribution, and editorial choice. Printing became an industry before it became a modern profession. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Venetian Market and Industry Structure

Venice was the first city in Europe to feel the full impact of printing. By the end of the fifteenth century, roughly 150 Venetian presses had turned out over 4,000 editions, representing nearly twice the production of Paris and between a seventh and an eighth of the total output of all European presses. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:470-480] The expansion was explosive: 176 editions had been published in Venice by 1474; 593 by the end of the decade. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:468-470]

But the boom masked savage attrition. Rather more than 100 printing-companies have been identified in Venice up to 1490; only twenty-three were still active during the following decade; only ten survived the century. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:545-547] Survival required capital, contacts, and experience. Nicholas Jenson’s will disposed of approximately 4,000 ducats — a respectable fortune but a mere tenth of what a single spice-dealer could make from one year’s shortage, and nothing alongside the hundreds of thousands left by Venice’s merchant-noblemen. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:540-544] Erasmus credited Andrea Torresani, Aldus’s partner, with 1,000 ducats net profit each year and an overall fortune of 100,000 ducats (though this may be rhetorical exaggeration). ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:505-506]

The industry concentrated rapidly. According to Victor Scholderer’s calculations, fifty-odd presses produced some 600 editions up to the end of 1481; of those, 260 editions (forty-three per cent) came from just two companies — those of Nicholas Jenson and John of Cologne. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:954-958] Lucantonio Giunti, one of the most successful printer-publishers, more than doubled his capital base between 1491 and 1499, and almost doubled it again by 1509. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:948-950]

The most successful firms became capital-intensive, international operations. By the end of his life Jenson was concerned almost entirely with sales rather than production: he had 500 ducats worth of stock in Pavia alone, agents in most Italian centres, and an English contact for business north of the Alps. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:811-814] Gerolamo Strozzi commissioned editions from multiple Venetian printers and bought paper to the value of 731 ducats for the texts in which he had an interest, distributing finished books through agents in Rome, Siena, Pisa, Naples, Bruges, and London. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:864-868] ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:804-807]

The Aldine Press: Partnership and Capital Structure

The Aldine Press was founded around 1494 as a partnership between three men: Aldus Manutius (scholar-editor), Andrea Torresani (experienced printer-bookseller who had been active for more than a decade), and Pierfrancesco Barbarigo (nephew of the Doge of Venice). ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:4266-4268] The partnership was structured in two equal shares: one controlled entirely by the Barbarigo estate, the other held jointly by Torresani and Aldus. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:4272-4276] When Aldus and Torresani formally unified their property in 1506, four-fifths of the combined total was Torresani’s. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:4284-4286] Aldus’s overall share in the company was probably about one tenth — making it unlikely that he ever had the decisive word on business policy. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:4286-4287] This explains why Torresani was able to block further publication in Greek in 1505, and why Aldus could not offer reduced prices to friends since the editions were “a joint venture with certain others.” ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:4307-4308] ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:4301-4303]

A cross-check confirms the upper bound: in 1503 Aldus claimed to be producing 1,000 copies per month, and the prevailing price for octavo editions was 1.5 lire mocenighe per copy (approximately 0.234 ducats at 6.4 lire per ducat). ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:4934-4938] ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:5402-5403] This gives a monthly production value of about 1,500 lire, or 234 ducats. The landed income of a powerful Venetian nobleman, such as Procurator Nicolo Bernardo, amounted to 239 ducats per year from property — meaning it took a full year’s income from a substantial estate to keep the Aldine press active for a single month. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:4876-4877] ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:4938-4942]

Payments to Editors and Scholars

Scholarly editing was a significant cost. Erasmus earned 20 ducats for his services; Fra Giocondo received 10 for work on the Scriptores Rei Rusticae. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:4921-4922] A guarantee of 60 ducats had to be staked on the proper treatment of three volumes of St Catherine of Siena. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:4919-4921] Proof-readers commanded 4-6 ducats per month. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:719-720]

Aldine Pricing and Edition Economics

Contrary to his popular reputation as a publisher of cheap books for the masses, Aldus’s Greek editions were expensive — often more than double the price of comparable books from earlier printers. The catalogue of 1498 shows: ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:5724-5729]

By comparison, Francesco da Madiis had stocked “large Greek Dictionaries” at less than half a ducat, “Erotemata Graeca” at 1 marcello, and a selection of Hesiod and Theocritus at just over 2 marcelli — barely more than half the Aldine equivalents, and sometimes even lower. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:5731-5743] Codrus Urceus, a friend of Aldus, complained in 1498 that he could have bought ten fine Latin manuscripts for what he paid for his Aldine Aristotle. Alvise Capello walked into Francesco da Madiis’ shop, paid just over 2 ducats, and walked out with a dozen different books. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:5750-5755]

The Aldine octavo editions (c. 1501 onward), famous for their compact format, were priced at 1.5 lire mocenighe per copy (about 0.234 ducats or roughly 4 marcelli). ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:4936-4937] While this was a step down from the Greek folios, it was still not a budget price: the smaller works of Aldus’s predecessors cost as little as 1 marcello. The true cheap-book revolution had begun earlier, with the Milanese editions of Bonus Accursius and the general downward pressure on prices noted by Putnam from the 1490s onward. Aldus’s achievement was not cheapness but scholarly authority and the convenience of the octavo format.

Edition Sizes

Edition sizes varied by genre and risk appetite:

  • A 1478 contract for a Bible in Venice specified 930 copies. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:899] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]
  • The 1507 syndicate (de Tortis, Giunti, Scotto, Arrivabene) planned a five-year joint programme with prices and print-runs decided by majority vote. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:877-889]
  • Aldus in 1503 asserted a production rate of 1,000 copies per month. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:4934-4935]
  • Sweynheym and Pannartz in Rome claimed 12,000 to 20,475 copies printed across their various editions (as of the early 1470s). ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:749-756]

Distribution and Credit

Distribution was more sophisticated than commonly assumed. The pedlar with his donkey served the low end; substantial books were handled by merchants who knew their business. Salaried agents circulated in university towns with books for sale; professors recommended still-unpublished editions in their lectures. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:781-818] But retailers were often slow to pay, and unsold copies could be returned. In 1482 a Siena retailer simply returned his unsold copies to Strozzi, and a year later prices had to be reduced. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:830-832]

Printers spread risk through a variety of contracts: short-term partnerships to cover one or two editions, author-financed publications, and longer syndicates. The contract of 1507 between the de Tortis brothers, Arrivabene, Giunti, and Scotto set out a five-year joint plan; expenses and profits were divided into four equal shares. Printed sheets were stored in a collectively-rented warehouse to which each partner had a key; any decision affecting prices or edition sizes was taken by majority vote. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:877-889] At the other extreme, the 1478 Bible contract between Leonardus of Ratisbon and Nicholas of Frankfurt shows the craftsman bound hand and foot to the financier — Leonardus could draw money only in instalments as each batch of sheets was delivered and was barred from taking other work. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:897-906] Peter Maufer, a French typographer working in Padua, ended with debts of 225 ducats to his financier Bartolomeo Valdezocco, who took formal possession of the entire workshop; the new contract made him “the servant” who had to deliver every quire “day by day” to his master. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md:907-917]

Significance

The printing revolution was therefore not only a technical change from manuscript to type. It was a shift in the economics of knowledge: high initial investment, reproducible stock, wider markets, faster distribution, and increasing dependence on capital, credit, and reader demand. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The Gutenberg Bible Balance Sheet

The most detailed surviving estimate of the economics behind a single fifteenth-century printed book is the reconstructed balance sheet for the 42-line gutenberg-bible compiled by the German book historian Leonard Hoffmann and reproduced in John Man’s Appendix I. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md:1556-1619]

Costs

Hoffmann’s breakdown of Gutenberg’s expenditure on the Bible alone runs as follows (in gulden): ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md:1568-1583]

ItemCost (gulden)
Six presses240
Typecases and other fittings60
Rent for three years30
Heating20
Three hand moulds60
Typecasting metals100
Ink30
Paper400
Vellum300
Wages800
Bible (as exemplar for sales)80
Total (Bible alone)2,120

This sum almost exactly matched the loans Gutenberg had received from johannes-fust: the original 150 gulden plus the 1,600 handed over later, plus interest and compound interest — totalling about 2,200 gulden. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md:1562-1564]

Second Workshop

Gutenberg was not working only on the Bible. He ran a second workshop with other projects in hand: the Donatus grammars (some 5,000 copies), Sibylline Prophecies, indulgences, and other publications now lost. By analogy with the monastery of St Ulrich in Augsburg, which paid 702 gulden for ten presses and types in 1472 (when costs had already fallen), Man estimates this second operation cost another ~2,200 gulden — roughly the amount of the original loans. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md:1585-1586]

Total outgoings: around 4,400 gulden, minimum. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md:1587]

Projected Income

For the Bible specifically, Man derives income estimates from market benchmarks: ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md:1593-1607]

  • A handwritten Bible of equivalent quality cost 60–100 gulden, so Gutenberg needed to undercut scribal copies, especially after adding rubrication and binding costs. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md:1595-1597]
  • A book printed on paper cost about one-third of a vellum one. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md:1599]

Projected Bible sales: ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md:1601-1607]

Even on the most optimistic projection, combined Bible sales (4,200–5,250 gulden) only equalled or modestly exceeded total costs (~4,500 gulden). Income from minor projects contributed a further ~4,000 gulden over six years, but this was insufficient to cover ongoing expenses. At completion in 1455 the venture was technically insolvent, which explains why Fust moved to call in his loans and seize the workshop — an economic reality that underlies the famous 1455 legal dispute. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md:1609-1619]

Costs and Prices in Venice

While detailed balance-sheet data survive for Gutenberg’s Mainz operation, a different but equally valuable set of economic records exists for the Venetian book trade of the late fifteenth century. These come from the day-book of a Venetian bookseller (1484–1485, preserved in the Marcian Library), a surviving printing contract from 1478, and the record of Nicolas Jenson’s sale of his matrices — together with observations by the historian Horatio F. Brown on the cost structure of the early Venetian press. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md:3205-3225]

The Day-Book of a Venetian Bookseller (1484–1485)

Brown’s abstract of a Venetian bookseller’s day-book reveals both the range of stock and the prices paid by retail customers in the 1480s. The bookseller carried classics, Bibles, missals, breviaries, works on canon law, school-books, romances, and poetry. Purchases from the publisher were ordinarily made for cash, and most customers likewise paid in cash — but barter was not uncommon.

Recorded barter transactions include: ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md:3223]

  • A chronicle was exchanged for oil
  • Cicero’s Orations for wine
  • A general assortment of books for flour
  • Binders’ bills were settled with the Life and Miracles of the Madonna and with the series of the Hundred Novels
  • The proof-reader was paid in copies of a Mamotrictus, a Legendary, and a Bible
  • An illuminator’s account was adjusted with an Abacus (a multiplication table or condensed arithmetic)

Specific retail prices are recorded for several titles: ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md:3225]

TitlePriceUnit
Poggio’s Facetiae9 soldi—
Inamoramento d’Orlando1 lira—
Dante’s Inferno (with commentary)1 ducat—
Plutarch’s Lives2 ducats—
Martial (small volume)15 soldi—

The cheapest books were volumes of poetry and romance. Editions from certain printers commanded higher prices than the same titles from less prestigious imprints. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md:3225]

Manuscripts as the Largest Outlay

Brown observes that the heavier part of a printer-publisher’s expenditure was not manufacturing but the purchase and preparation of manuscripts: ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md:3211]

“The larger proportion of the outlay required for these early books was not the expense of the manufacturing, heavy as this was, but the payments required for the purchase of manuscripts, and for their revision, collation, correction, and preparation for the type-setters.”

This cost structure meant that scholarly competence — the ability to judge, edit, and prepare texts — was as important as technical skill with the press. A printer-publisher like Aldus Manutius, who could act as his own editor, held a significant business advantage. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md:3213]

The 1478 Bible Printing Contract

The most concrete surviving statement of manufacturing cost for a Venetian edition is a printing contract of 1478 between Leonardus (printer) and Nicolaus (publisher, who bore the commercial risk): ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md:3215]

  • Edition size: 930 copies of the complete Bible
  • Printing fee: 430 ducats (paid by Nicolaus to Leonardus)
  • Paper: furnished by the publisher (Nicolaus), at additional cost
  • Printer’s copies: 20 copies retained by Leonardus
  • Cost per copy (exclusive of paper): roughly 430 ÷ 910 ≈ 0.47 ducats per copy; Brown’s contemporary dollar equivalent is about $2.50 per copy
  • Cost per copy (with paper): roughly $3.00

By comparison, the selling price of Bibles in 1492 varied from 6 to 12 ducats (30–60 in Brown’s valuation), though these prices probably included various styles of binding. The mark-up from manufacturing cost to retail price was therefore substantial — roughly 12- to 25-fold over the printing fee alone, or 10- to 20-fold over the all-in cost with paper. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md:3215]

Jenson’s Matrix Sale (1479)

In 1479, the leading Venetian printer Nicolas Jenson sold a set of his matrices — the brass or copper plates struck from punches that serve as moulds for type-casting — to Andrea Torresano of Asola. Torresano later became the father-in-law of Aldus Manutius, and these matrices are believed to have formed the nucleus of the type-foundry equipment of the Aldine Press. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md:3205]

The sale is significant as an early instance of the transfer of physical capital (rather than finished books or publishing rights) between Venetian printing houses. It also underscores the value of well-cut type: Jenson’s Roman face was among the most admired in Europe, and its matrices were a tangible asset worth recording in the historical record. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md:3191-3205]

The Shift to Cheap Books (c. 1490)

Putnam notes a decisive market shift in the last decade of the fifteenth century. The earliest printed books had been planned to meet the tastes of wealthy collectors accustomed to beautiful manuscripts. By 1490, however, the revival of learning had created a new reading public “eager for knowledge and no longer fastidious as to the beauty of the form in which its literature was presented.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md:3219]

“By 1490, a demand had arisen for cheap books for popular reading, and in changing their methods to meet this demand, the printers permitted the standard of excellence of their work to suffer a material decline.”

The Gothic character had already secured a practical advantage over Roman type by offering “considerable economy of space,” which became increasingly important as price pressure mounted. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md:3207] This shift toward cheaper, smaller formats culminated in Aldus’s octavo classics (c. 1500), priced at about three marcelli (two francs, or roughly 0.40,equivalenttoperhaps2.00 in 1890s purchasing power), a revolutionary price point that brought classical texts within reach of impecunious students. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md:3337]

Significance for the Venetian Economy

Taken together, the day-book, the 1478 contract, and the record of Jenson’s matrix sale reveal a market that was already sophisticated by the 1470s: differentiated by format and price, operating partly on barter and partly on cash, with a clear distinction between manufacturing cost and retail mark-up, and with expensive editorial acquisition (manuscripts and scholarly correction) outweighing the physical cost of printing. The shift from luxury folios to cheap octavos after 1490 marks a genuine economic revolution in the accessibility of printed knowledge, repeating in miniature the pattern that Gutenberg’s own Bible had first exemplified in Mainz. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md:3211-3225] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md:3337]

The Liturgical Market

The largest and most dependable segment of the early printing business was the production of liturgical books — missals, breviaries, psalters, graduals, antiphoners, and other service books for the Church. These works had a captive institutional market: every monastery, cathedral, and parish needed them, and ecclesiastical authorities could mandate purchases through reform decrees. The economic logic was fundamentally different from speculative publishing for the learned market. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Origins: The Bursfeld Missal Project

The first major opportunity for liturgical printing emerged from the Council of Basel (1431-1449), which sought to standardize the diverse local liturgies of the German Church. The Benedictine abbot Johann Dederoth of Bursfeld proposed a new standardized missal — the Ordinarius — as part of a broader reform. The task was inherited by his successor Johann Hagen, who in 1446 formed a union of six monasteries to develop the text. When the papal legate Cardinal Nicholas of Cusa arrived in Germany in 1451 with the authority to approve the new missal, the conditions for large-scale liturgical printing were in place: an authorized text, a guaranteed ecclesiastical market, and papal backing. Cusa also wanted a standardized choirbook and breviary. John Man calls these “lines… with their guaranteed markets and perhaps backed by Church funds” that “offered potential bestsellers once they had been approved.” ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

This was Gutenberg’s original “big project.” He was preparing to print the Bursfeld missal when the project foundered on a dispute between Cusa’s text (supported by Rome) and the alternative liturgy backed by Mainz’s Archbishop Dietrich. Gutenberg found himself with a printing shop set up for a missal but no authorized text to print — a “publisher’s nightmare — two conflicting texts, one supported by Rome, the other by the archbishop.” This forced him to pivot to the Bible as his flagship work. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The Economics of Liturgical Printing

Liturgical books had a fundamentally different risk profile from other printed products:

  • Guaranteed demand. Every church needed missals and breviaries. Unlike classical editions, which depended on the unpredictable taste of humanist scholars, liturgical books had known buyers. Schoeffer supplied missals “across Central Europe — good business, no risk, high quality — for another forty years” after Gutenberg’s death. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

  • Standardization drove sales. The Council of Trent (1545-1563) mandated uniform liturgical texts across the Catholic world. This created waves of replacement demand: old missals became non-compliant and had to be replaced with authorized editions. The same dynamic applied to music: after Trent, printed graduals and antiphoners standardized Catholic liturgical music across Europe. ^[raw/articles/sixteenth-century-printing.md]

  • High fixed costs, reliable returns. Missals required multiple type sizes (for rubrics, chant, and text) and often two-colour printing (black and red). This made them expensive to produce, but the institutional market could absorb high prices. Price competition was less intense than in the classical or vernacular markets because the buyer was not an individual scholar but a church or monastery with a budget. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

  • Semi-clandestine cross-confessional trade. In the seventeenth century, Protestant Amsterdam became a centre for printing Catholic missals and breviaries for export to Catholic countries. The Blaeu firm specialized in this “semi-clandestine production of missals and other Roman Catholic literature” as a reliable revenue stream — strict Protestants called divine punishment when Blaeu’s printing house burned in 1672 because he printed Catholic liturgical works. ^[raw/articles/blaeu-review-nellen.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Durandus Publishing Phenomenon

John Man records one striking example of the liturgical market’s scale: the guide to liturgy by Guillaume Durand (Durandus), a thirteenth-century French canon law expert, had “over forty editions following Fust’s” initial printing. A work of practical church administration, not scholarly theology, it sold steadily because every cleric needed it — a textbook example of how the guaranteed institutional demand for practical religious works created a more predictable publishing business than speculative humanist editions. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Why Printers Overestimated the Church Market

Despite the solidity of liturgical publishing, De Vinne notes that many early printers made a strategic error by over-investing in religious works:

“The failure of many early printers to make their business profitable was largely caused by their injudicious selection for publication of bulky theological writings which cost a great deal of money to print, and were salable only to a small class. It was unwisely supposed that printing would receive its great support from the ecclesiastics. With this object in view, the first printers printed almost exclusively in Latin, and generally in the expensive shape of folio.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The distinction is between liturgical works (which had guaranteed institutional demand) and scholastic theology (which depended on a narrow academic market). Printers who conflated the two often overproduced and failed. Sweynheym and Pannartz in Rome printed twenty-eight patristic and classical editions but overproduction bankrupted them — even though they had a steady line of credit from the Church, the market for learned theology was far smaller than the market for service books. ^[raw/articles/printing-economics.md]
