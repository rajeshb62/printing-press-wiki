The Printing Press

Caption: Fifteenth-century wooden screw press, showing the screw, platen, pull bar, bed, tympan, frisket, forme, inking balls, paper, and press frame.

The printing press is the mechanical device used to transfer ink from a composed forme of movable-type to paper or vellum. While the principle of relief printing was known long before the 15th century, the press itself — capable of applying strong, even, and repeatable pressure over a large surface — was the crucial mechanical innovation that turned movable type into a practical system for mass-producing books. The press designed by johann-gutenberg around 1450, adapted from the screw presses used in wine-making and paper-making, remained the dominant form of printing technology for roughly 350 years.^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md]

Gutenberg’s Adaptation of the Screw Press

Gutenberg did not invent the press mechanism from scratch. The screw press had been used for centuries in Europe for pressing wine grapes, olives, and linen, as well as in paper-making (squeezing water from freshly made sheets).^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md] What Gutenberg did was recognize that this same mechanism — a heavy wooden screw threaded through a cross-beam, turned by a lever or bar to drive a flat surface downward — could be adapted to apply uniform pressure over a bed of type.

The key difference from earlier presses was the need for precise, even pressure over a broad rectangular area, rather than the concentrated pressure required for squeezing liquids. The printing press needed to descend perfectly parallel to the type bed and to release cleanly after each impression.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The Wooden Press Design

Gutenberg’s press, and the “common press” that descended from it for centuries, was built entirely of wood (with some metal parts in the screw mechanism) and consisted of the following components:^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md]

The framework: Two massive upright timbers (the cheeks) were connected by cross-beams at top and bottom, forming a sturdy, square frame. An intermediate cross-beam (the head) held the screw mechanism; another supported the bed.

The screw and spindle: A large wooden screw, threaded through a fixed nut in the upper cross-beam, extended downward. The thread pitch determined how much vertical travel the screw produced per revolution. The lower end of the spindle rested in a socket at the center of the platen.

The platen: A flat, rectangular block of wood (later faced with iron) suspended from the screw. When the screw was turned, the platen descended parallel to the bed and pressed the paper against the inked type. Because it was wooden, it was rarely perfectly flat, requiring compensating layers of woolen blanket to ensure even pressure.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The bar: A long wooden lever (the bar or rounce) was inserted through the spindle or attached to the screw. The pressman pulled the bar in a semicircular motion to turn the screw, bringing the platen down. After the impression, the bar was reversed to raise the platen. The labor involved was substantial — described as roughly equal to that of a plowman in the field.^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md]

The bed and coffin: The type forme (the locked-up arrangement of movable type, furniture, and quoins) was placed on a flat bed, sometimes of stone but usually of wood, encased in a frame called the coffin. The bed was mounted on rails so it could be rolled in and out from under the platen.^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md]

The Two-Pull System

The platen of Gutenberg’s press was smaller than the entire forme of type. A full-page forme could not be printed in a single impression. Instead, the pressman used the two-pull system: the forme was positioned so that only half of it sat under the platen; the bar was pulled to print that half; then the bed was rolled further in (or the carriage shifted) and the bar was pulled again to print the other half.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Lorck describes it precisely: “To print a forme, a double pull of the bar was necessary because the platen was not large enough to cover the full forme. The carriage was therefore first rolled only halfway in, and the first impression made, then rolled all the way in so that the other half could be printed.”^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

This meant that each sheet required two pulls for the first side, and two more for the reverse — four pulls in total to produce a single printed sheet. This halved the potential production rate but allowed the same press to handle large folio pages (used for the gutenberg-bible) without requiring an oversized platen.

The Frisket and Tympan (Paper Registration)

Two innovations were essential for producing clean, accurately registered prints on both sides of the sheet:

The tympan: A frame covered with parchment or cloth, hinged to the end of the bed. The sheet of paper was placed on the tympan, which was then folded down over the inked forme. The tympan included a soft blanket (the packing) that compensated for irregularities in the type height and the paper, ensuring an even impression.^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md]

The frisket: A light frame covered with a sheet of paper, hinged to the tympan. This paper mask had rectangular windows cut out precisely where the type would print; the rest of the frisket protected the margins from accidental ink marks. The frisket was folded down over the sheet, then the tympan and frisket together were folded onto the forme. After the impression, the whole assembly was lifted, and the printed sheet was removed.^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md]

Register points: To ensure that the reverse side of the sheet printed exactly in alignment with the first side, the pressman used pins or points fixed to the tympan. These pierced the paper at specific positions; on the second pass, the sheet was hung on the same points, guaranteeing that the printed area on both sides coincided precisely. De Vinne notes that four point-holes can be seen in early books from Caxton’s press.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Inking with Leather Balls

Before the introduction of rollers in the 19th century, ink was applied to the forme using ink balls (also called beating balls or Ballen). These were mushroom-shaped implements made of wood, stuffed with horsehair (or wool), and covered with tanned sheepskin or dog leather. The pressman (the Ballenmeister or “ball master”) dabbed the balls onto an ink slab to pick up ink, then worked it by rubbing the two balls together in a rhythmic motion to distribute the ink evenly. He then beat the forme by bouncing the balls across the type — first from top to bottom, then laterally — applying more pressure to areas requiring heavier color (such as large initials or woodcuts).^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

This was a skilled and physically demanding task. The balls had to be kept at the right consistency and humidity; over-inking caused smudging, under-inking produced gray, uneven sheets.

Pace of Work

The Gutenberg press was slow by any modern measure. The earliest documentary estimate comes from Philip de Lignamine, who wrote around 1472 that the printers of mainz produced about three hundred sheets per day.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] This was the output of a two-man crew working a single press:

  • The puller (pressman or Pressenmeister) operated the bar, sliding the carriage in and out, pulling the impression, and removing the printed sheets.
  • The beater (inkerman or Ballenmeister) handled the inking of the forme with the leather balls, rotating with the puller to share the labor.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md][raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The two-pull system meant that 300 sheets per day represented roughly 600 pulls of the bar (for one side) or 1,200 pulls for both sides. By the middle of the 18th century, improvements to the press (lighter construction, better screw design) allowed two pressmen to achieve 2,000—3,000 impressions per day on the common press — equivalent to 1,000—1,500 completed sheets, roughly five to ten times the output of Gutenberg’s original press.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] De Vinne notes that by 1840, a single hand-pressman in New York with an inking machine could produce 1,500 impressions, with each impression covering a surface area roughly eight times larger than those of the 15th century.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Evolution of the Press

The Blaeu Press (c. 1620)

The first significant recorded improvement to the wooden press was made by Willem Janszoon Blaeu, an Amsterdam printer, astronomer, and instrument-maker. Around 1620, Blaeu introduced several refinements:^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md]^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

  • The spindle of the screw passed through a square block guided in the wooden frame, which prevented the platen from twisting and ensured a truer vertical motion.
  • The platen was suspended from the block by wires or cords, allowing it to settle flat against the forme regardless of minor variations in the screw alignment.
  • A mechanical device for rolling the bed in and out replaced the manual sliding of the heavy carriage.
  • An iron hand-lever replaced the wooden bar, giving the pressman better leverage.

Blaeu’s press became the standard across Europe and England. A press of this type was used by Benjamin Franklin as a journeyman printer in London in the early 18th century.^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md] Blaeu is said to have built nine presses, each named after one of the Muses.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Other improvements to the wooden press were made by Danner in Nuremberg, but the Blaeu design remained substantially unchanged for the next 180 years.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Common Press (c. 1500—1800)

The printing press in general use from Gutenberg’s time through the end of the 18th century is referred to as the common press. Lorck observes that “the press of 1750 was in principle and in all essential components the same as that from the year 1500.”^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] It was built from wood, though over time the spindle, the nut, and the platen were gradually made of brass or iron, and the foundation and rails were made of iron. But the fundamental mechanism — a screw-driven platen, a sliding bed, a two-pull operation — remained essentially unchanged for 350 years.^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md]

The Stanhope (Iron) Press (1800)

The first major redesign of the printing press in three and a half centuries was the work of Charles Stanhope, 3rd Earl Stanhope, in England, around 1798—1800. The Stanhope press replaced the wooden frame with a single casting of iron, providing far greater rigidity and allowing much larger forms to be printed.^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md]

Stanhope retained the screw but added a combination of levers that multiplied the pressman’s force, making it possible to apply the necessary pressure with less exertion. The press was extremely heavy and cumbersome, but it was the first iron printing press ever constructed. According to Hoe, the printers of the day were so eager to adopt the lever system that they retrofitted levers onto their wooden presses — which generally destroyed them, as the wooden frames could not withstand the strain.^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md]

The Cylinder Press (Koenig, 1814)

The true revolution in press design came from Friedrich Koenig, a Saxon printer and engineer who moved to England. In 1812—1813, Koenig (assisted by mechanic Andrew Bauer) devised a press that replaced the flat platen with a revolving cylinder passing over a flat bed of type. This was the first practical cylinder press.^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md]

Koenig’s press used a reciprocating bed: the forme moved back and forth under the cylinder; the cylinder made a partial revolution to print as the bed passed under it, then continued turning (with a reduced-diameter segment, so the returning bed cleared without touching). The press also incorporated automatic inking rollers, replacing the manual ink balls.

In 1814, Koenig installed two of his double-cylinder presses in the office of The Times in London, where they printed at the rate of 800 sheets per hour — roughly ten times faster than a hand press. The printers were kept in the dark about the new machinery until the moment of publication; the November 29, 1814 issue of The Times was the first newspaper ever printed on a steam-powered cylinder press.^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md]

This marked the beginning of the end for the hand-press era. Within decades, cylinder presses — improved by Applegath, Cowper, Napier, and by Robert Hoe & Co. in America — became the standard for high-speed newspaper and book printing, while the hand press survived only for fine-press work and proofing.

Significance

The printing press as a physical machine is sometimes overshadowed by the intellectual achievement of movable-type, but without the press, the type would have been useless. The press provided the mechanical force that made rapid, consistent, large-scale printing possible. It was the first machine in history designed for the mass production of identical copies of text, and its basic principles — a reciprocating bed passing under an impression surface — remained the foundation of all printing technology until the late 20th century.

The press’s 350-year stability (c. 1450—1800) is itself remarkable: it demonstrates that Gutenberg’s design was so well-suited to its purpose that it took the combination of iron casting, steam power, and the demand for cheap newspapers to finally displace it.^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md]
