Map Making and the Printing Press

Map making and printing developed in a tight symbiotic relationship in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. The printing press made possible the mass reproduction of maps, while the technical demands of map publishing drove innovations in printing technique — especially in copperplate engraving, colour printing, and large-format presswork. At the same time, the rediscovery and printing of Ptolemy’s Geography transformed European understanding of the world by making ancient geographical knowledge available to every scholar with access to a printed book. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Maps Before Printing

Before the press, maps were rare, expensive, and non-standardised. Manuscript maps in the European tradition included the mappa mundi tradition (world maps oriented to the east, combining geography with biblical history), portolan charts (practical navigational maps of Mediterranean coastlines, used by sailors since the thirteenth century), and a small number of regional maps based on Ptolemy’s coordinates. Fewer than a thousand manuscript maps survive from the entire Middle Ages. A single manuscript of Ptolemy’s Geography might cost as much as a small farm. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Manuscript maps were typically unique: each copy was drawn by hand, and copies of the same source could differ substantially. There was no mechanism for standardisation, no way to correct errors systematically, and no means of reproducing a map in quantity. The printing press changed all of this. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The Printed Ptolemy: Cartography’s Foundational Text

The Geography of Claudius Ptolemy (second century AD) was the most important geographical text of antiquity. It provided a systematic method for locating places by latitude and longitude and included instructions for map projection. The text was rediscovered in Byzantium in the late fourteenth century, translated into Latin by Jacopo Angeli da Scarperia around 1406, and circulated in manuscript through the fifteenth century. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Printing transformed Ptolemy’s work from a rare scholarly treasure into a widely available reference. The key printed editions were:

Woodcut vs. Copperplate for Maps

The choice between woodcut and copperplate engraving was the central technical decision in early map printing. Each method had distinct advantages and limitations that shaped the evolution of cartographic publishing.

Woodcut Maps

Woodcut (xylographic) maps were cut from a single block of wood, with the map and any text carved together. The block was a relief surface — raised lines took ink, recessed areas were white — and could be printed on the same press as movable type. This meant woodcut maps could be integrated into books and printed in a single pass with the text. The Ulm Ptolemy of 1482, with its thirty-two woodcut maps by Johann Schnitzer, was the first printed Ptolemy to use woodcut. Leonhard Holl, the printer, “was the first to print a work with woodcut maps into which type could in part be inserted” (der erste, der ein Werk mit in Holz geschnittenen Landkarten, worin zumteil Typen eingesetzt werden konnten, druckte). ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The hybrid technique of inserting type into woodcut maps — cutting recesses into the woodblock for individual letterforms or lines of type — allowed place-names to be set in movable type and locked into position before printing. This was a significant innovation: it meant that text changes (corrections, additions, translations) required only recomposition of type, not recarving of the woodblock. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Copperplate Maps

Copperplate engraving (intaglio) produced much finer lines and more detail. The map was incised into a copper plate with a burin, then the plate was inked and wiped — ink remained only in the incised lines — and printed under heavy roller pressure. Because copperplate was an intaglio process, it required a separate rolling press rather than the screw press used for type. This meant copperplate maps could not be printed alongside text and had to be bound in as separate sheets. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md]

Copperplate was superior for maps because it allowed:

  • Much finer lines for coastlines, rivers, and mountains
  • More lettering per area
  • Delicate hachuring and stippling for terrain
  • Clearer differentiation between land and water

Conrad Sweynheym made “the first attempts to produce maps for the printing press in high-cut copper” (die ersten Versuche, Landkarten für die Buchdruckerpresse in Kupfer hoch geschnitten herzustellen) for the 1478 Rome Ptolemy. His letter-punch technique — individual capital letters punched into the copper plate rather than cut with a graver — was a unique technical innovation that linked typefounding and map engraving. The same punches appeared on the Eichstatt map of Nicholas of Cusa and the Rome Ptolemy, suggesting Sweynheym may have engraved both. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

The Shift to Copperplate

By the mid-sixteenth century, copperplate had decisively won for high-quality map work. Giacomo Gastaldi in Venice, Abraham Ortelius in Antwerp, and Gerard Mercator in Duisburg all used copperplate exclusively. The shift was driven by reader demand for detail and by the growing market for atlases, where the higher cost of copperplate was offset by premium pricing and larger edition sizes. Woodcut maps persisted in cheaper productions (school texts, cosmographies like Munster’s) but disappeared from serious cartography by 1600. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

The Atlas as a New Book Genre

Before Ortelius’s Theatrum Orbis Terrarum (1570), maps were sold as single sheets, as loose collections, or as illustrations bound into books. There was no standardised format, no uniform size, and no systematic organisation. The atlas — a collection of maps designed to uniform specifications, bound as a book, and sold as a coherent work — was a new category of printed artefact. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

The atlas required:

  • Standardised plate sizes so that all maps in the book matched
  • A consistent design language (borders, typefaces, scales, compass roses)
  • Scholarly apparatus (descriptive text on the verso, a catalogue of sources, indexes)
  • Commercial distribution through the same book-trade networks that sold other printed books

Ortelius’s Theatrum succeeded because it was not only a cartographic achievement but a publishing achievement: Plantin’s printing, the Antwerp distribution network, and the Frankfurt Book Fair made it a European bestseller. The atlas genre — with Ortelius, Mercator, and then the Blaeu family — became one of the most prestigious and profitable branches of the printing trade. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

The Business of Map Publishing

Map publishing shared the economic structure of book publishing but added the costs of copperplate engraving. A single engraved plate might take months to produce and cost the equivalent of a year’s wages for a skilled worker. The capital tied up in plates was substantial and had to be recovered over multiple editions. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

This high fixed cost created several dynamics:

  1. Plate inheritance: Map plates were bought, sold, inherited, and re-used across generations. The Blaeu firm’s atlases incorporated plates originally engraved for Ortelius and Mercator. The Eichstatt map’s plate, originally owned by Conrad Peutinger, was still being used for printings as late as the 1560s or 1570s. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]
  2. Privilege protection: Map publishers sought exclusive privileges (early forms of copyright) to protect their investment. The Venetian Senate granted Gastaldi the title of cosmographer and protected his rights. Ortelius obtained privileges from the King of Spain and the Emperor. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]
  3. Commercial risk: Leonhard Holl’s Ulm Ptolemy “had no financial success; first he had to pawn the work, later sell it” (Pekuniären Erfolg hatte Holl davon nicht; erst musste er das Werk versetzen, später verkaufen). It took a Venetian buyer, Justus de Albano, to revive the edition through his factor Johannes Reger. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]
  4. Piracy: Successful maps were immediately copied. Ortelius’s Asia map (1567) was copied in Venice by Donato Bertelli. The Antwerp printer-publisher Gerard de Jode tried to compete with Ortelius’s Theatrum with his Speculum Orbis Terrarum (1578), but failed commercially, partly because Ortelius’s privilege protection gave him a head start.

Maps in the Broader Printing Revolution

The printing of maps contributed to the broader transformation of European knowledge in several ways:

  • Standardisation of geographical knowledge: For the first time, readers in different cities could consult the same map. Errors could be identified, reported to the publisher, and corrected in the next edition. The cumulative improvement of map accuracy over successive editions was a direct consequence of print. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]
  • Integration of text and image: Maps required the combination of image (coastlines, rivers, mountains) with text (place-names, legends, titles) on the same sheet. This pushed the technical boundaries of both woodcut and copperplate printing. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]
  • Scientific collaboration across distance: Munster’s network of 120+ collaborators, Ortelius’s Catalogus auctorum, and Mercator’s correspondence all used print to build a geographically distributed community of knowledge. Cartography was one of the first sciences to rely systematically on the printed book for its development. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]
  • Popularisation of geography: Munster’s Cosmographia (written in German as well as Latin) and the many vernacular editions of Ortelius’s Theatrum brought geographical knowledge to readers beyond the scholarly elite. This had practical consequences for navigation, trade, and colonial expansion. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]
  • The first reference-work genre: The atlas, as the first standardised, systematically organised printed reference work, established a model that would be followed by dictionaries, encyclopaedias, and other reference genres. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]
