Spread of Printing

Caption: Spread of printing across Europe from Mainz to major fifteenth-century print centers, 1450-1500.

Caption: Mainz printer diaspora after the sack of 1462, with routes toward early print centers across the Rhine region, France, Italy, and England.

The spread of printing from mainz across Europe between 1450 and 1500 is one of the most rapid and consequential diffusions of a technology in history. Within fifty years of Gutenberg’s first experiments with movable-type, printing presses had been established in roughly 280 cities across Europe, producing an estimated 15—20 million individual volumes. The engine of this diffusion was the Printer’s Dispersal of 1462 — the sack of Mainz by Archbishop Adolph of Nassau — which scattered the journeymen who had learned the art in Gutenberg’s, Fust’s, and Schoeffer’s workshops to every corner of the continent. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The Printer’s Dispersal of 1462

The single most important event in the spread of printing was not a commercial development but a political catastrophe. On the night of October 27—28, 1462, troops of Archbishop Adolph II of Nassau stormed Mainz, murdering hundreds of citizens and plundering the city. Mainz had been the sole center of European printing for over a decade; its master printers — Gutenberg, Fust, and Schoeffer — had maintained the technology as a closely guarded trade secret, and journeymen had sworn oaths of secrecy. The sack shattered this monopoly.

The journeymen printers, suddenly thrown out of employment, “fled panic-stricken to other countries; and considering themselves freed from their oath, the great secret of thirty years was spread abroad.” ^[raw/articles/pearson-gutenberg-and-art-of-printing.md] These wandering printers carried not only their practical knowledge of typefounding, punchcutting, presswork, and the punch-matrix system, but also actual type, matrices, and tools. Lorck describes them as “furchtlose Apostel und vorherbestimmte Verbreiter eines neuen Glaubens” — fearless apostles and predestined disseminators of a new faith. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The dispersal followed the major trade and pilgrimage routes of late-medieval Europe — down the Rhine to Cologne and the Netherlands, up the Rhine to Basel and Switzerland, across the Alps to Italy, westward along the French rivers to Paris and Lyon, and south to Spain. In nearly every city, the first printers were German immigrants. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Spread Within Germany

Printing spread rapidly within the German states after 1462:

  • Strasbourg: Although Gutenberg himself had worked there in the 1430s—1440s during his years of experimentation, the first established press was that of Johann Mentelin (c. 1458). Mentelin was producing a Bible by 1466 and issued what is thought to be the first printer’s catalogue in 1471. Heinrich Eggesteyn also printed in Strasbourg from the mid-1460s. By 1500, Strasbourg had some fifty printers, making it one of the most productive printing centers in Germany. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

  • Cologne: Ulrich Zell, a priest and printer who had probably learned his craft in Mainz, established the first press in Cologne around 1464. Cologne became a center of typographical innovation — the introduction of printed signatures (for binder’s collation), pagination, proper book-titles, and column titles are all associated with Cologne printers. Zell later provided crucial testimony in the Cologne Chronicle of 1499, affirming that “the art of printing was first invented in Mainz, by Johann Gutenberg.” ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

  • Basel: Berthold Ruppel (Berthold of Hanau), a workman from Gutenberg’s own shop, brought printing to Basel around 1468. The city’s university, founded in 1460, provided a ready market for scholarly texts. By 1473, Michael Wenssler and Bernhard Richel were producing substantial editions. Basel later became the home of Johann Froben (from 1491), whose press printed the works of Erasmus and set new standards for humanist scholarship. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

  • Augsburg: Gunther Zainer, probably a pupil of Fust or Schoeffer, began printing in 1468. Augsburg, a wealthy imperial city and major commercial hub, quickly developed a robust book trade. Zainer’s illustrated editions helped establish the German tradition of the illustrated printed book. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

  • Nuremberg: Anton Koberger established his press in 1470 and built it into the largest publishing operation in Europe during the incunable period. Koberger operated twenty-four presses simultaneously, employed over 100 workers, and maintained a network of branches and agents stretching from Paris to Vienna and from Krakow to Venice. His most famous production was the Nuremberg Chronicle (1493), a world history of 600 pages with over 1,800 woodcut illustrations, printed in editions of 1,500 Latin and 1,000 German copies. Koberger’s scale of operation was unmatched: he published more than 230 works in his career. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Other German cities that received presses before 1500 include: Bamberg (Albert Pfister, c. 1461, who printed the first illustrated book Der Edelstein and the first German Bible), Ulm (Johann Zainer, 1473), Lubeck (Lucas Brandis, 1475), Leipzig (c. 1481), and many others. By 1500, over sixty German cities had printing presses, with some 300 individual printing establishments. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Italy

Printing reached Italy remarkably early, and Italy — particularly Venice — surpassed Germany in total output before the end of the century.

  • Subiaco (1464): Two German printers, Konrad Sweynheym (from Schwanheim, near Mainz) and Johann Pannartz, established the first press in Italy at the Benedictine monastery of Santa Scholastica in Subiaco, some seventy kilometers east of Rome. They may have been encouraged by Nicholas of Cusa, who had close ties to the monastery and to the Benedictine order. In 1465 they printed Italy’s first printed book: the works of Firmianus Lactantius, an early Christian author. They used a semi-Gothic type that blended German and Italian scribal traditions. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

  • Rome (1467): Sweynheym and Pannartz moved their press to Rome in 1467, encouraged by Giovanni Andrea de Bussi (Vatican librarian and former secretary to Nicholas of Cusa). They produced twenty-eight titles in Rome, including editions of classical authors. Overproduction eventually ruined them; by 1472 their house was filled with unsold books, and the press failed. Pannartz died soon after, and Sweynheym turned to engraving maps. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

  • Venice (1469): Johannes de Spira (John of Speyer), a German printer who had learned his craft in Mainz, received a five-year monopoly on printing in Venice from the Signoria in 1469. His first book was Cicero’s Epistolae ad familiares, printed in a fine roman type. After his death later that year, his brother Wendelin continued the press. Nicolas Jenson, a Frenchman who had been sent to Mainz by Charles VII to learn the art, arrived in Venice around 1470 and perfected the roman typeface that became the standard for the next four centuries. Erhard Ratdolt, a German from Augsburg, introduced the first printed title pages and decorated initials from 1476.

Venice became the printing capital of Europe by 1500, producing over 4,000 editions — more than any other city. Its commercial networks, paper supply from the Alpine valleys, and concentration of humanist scholars created ideal conditions for the industry. The Venetian press of Aldus Manutius (from 1494) revolutionized book production in the 1490s with his octavo-format Libri Portatiles (portable books), printed in the cursive italic type he commissioned. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

  • Florence: Printing reached Florence in 1471, brought by Bernardo Cennini, though the city’s most famous printer-publisher was the Giunta family (from 1477). Florence’s humanist culture made it a natural market for classical texts, but it never rivaled Venice in scale.

  • Milan: The first press in Milan was established by Antonio Zarotto (a native of Parma, not Germany) in 1470. Milan became an important center for liturgical printing under the patronage of the Sforza dynasty.

Other Italian cities with presses by 1500 include Bologna, Padua, Ferrara, Naples, Brescia, and Parma. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

France

  • Paris (1470): The first press in France was established not by a commercial entrepreneur but by the University of the Sorbonne. Three German printers — Ulrich Gering (from Constance), Michael Friburger (from Colmar), and Martin Crantz (from Basel) — were invited by the professors Guillaume Fichet and Johann Heynlin (also called Jean de Lapierre) to set up a press within the Sorbonne. Their first book was the Letters of Gasparino of Bergamo (1470), printed in a roman type inspired by Venetian models. The Sorbonne press was primarily a scholarly enterprise, issuing classical and humanist texts for the university community. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

    Chevillier’s L’Origine de l’Imprimerie de Paris (1694), drawing directly on the Sorbonne’s registers, records that Jean de Lapierre (Heynlin) was the one who wrote to Germany to summon the printers, being himself German and having the necessary connections. He was Prior of the Sorbonne in 1470, the year the press was established. Lapierre not only procured the printers but prepared copies for them, read proofs, and helped with their editions. Fichet’s letter prefacing the first book thanks Lapierre for “the great advantage he has procured for the study of the Sciences, by means of the Germans he has brought.” ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

    The first Paris editions — eleven known titles printed 1470-1471, preserved in the Sorbonne library — were all printed in the same round Roman type (“gros Romain”) from the same matrices. Chevillier describes them: some letters are half-formed and half-printed, finished by hand; there are no capital letters (spaces left for illumination); no titles, foliation, or signatures; the ink is a beautiful black; some lines are printed in red on vellum. The paper is not very white but strong and well-sized. The books include Gasparino’s letters and orthography, Florus’ epitome of Livy, Sallust, Fichet’s own Rhetoric (on vellum), the Cynic Epistles, Bessarion’s letters, Laurentius Valla’s Elegantiae, and Jacobus Magnus’ Sophologium. ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

    Around 1473, after Fichet had left for Rome (called by Pope Sixtus IV in 1471) and Lapierre was preparing to enter the Carthusian order, Gering moved his press from the Sorbonne to the rue Saint-Jacques at the sign of the Soleil d’or (Sun of Gold). New types were cast in new matrices — Chevillier identifies three or four distinct characters in these second-press editions, some resembling current handwriting, others a large clear Roman type that he judges as beautiful as the best Venetian work. Gering’s Bible (c. 1475) was the first Bible printed in France. ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

    In 1473, the second press in Paris was established by Pierre Caesaris (Keyser) and Jean Stol, two Germans studying at the University who had been instructed by Gering. Caesaris, a Master of Arts, lodged on the rue Saint-Jacques at the sign of the Swan, in a house belonging to the Sorbonne. Their books — in Roman letters but less beautiful than Gering’s first editions — included the Manipulus Curatorum (1473), Seneca’s Epistles (1473), Valerius Maximus (1475), and the Dialogue of Ockham (1474), which Chelvillier attributes to them on typographic grounds. ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

    Gering continued printing in Paris after his partners departed (around 1477). He died on 23 August 1510 in the Soleil d’or, rue de Sorbonne, after forty years of printing in Paris. The Sorbonne kept an annual anniversary for him. By 1500, Paris had become one of Europe’s largest book-trade centers, with numerous presses in the Latin Quarter. The Estienne (Stephanus) family would later make Paris the capital of classical and biblical scholarship in the 16th century. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/chevillier-origine-imprimerie-paris.md]

  • Lyon (1472): Lyon received its first press in 1472, brought by three German printers. The city’s position at the confluence of the Saone and Rhone, its location on the trade routes connecting Italy, Germany, and France, and its regular fairs made it a natural center for the book trade. Lyon specialized in legal, medical, and humanistic works and had more than fifty printers by 1500. In the 16th century, Lyon would rival Paris in the scale of its publishing industry. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Low Countries (Netherlands)

  • Utrecht (1470): The first press in the Northern Netherlands was established by Ketelaer and De Leempt in Utrecht around 1470, though some scholars believe there may have been earlier activity in the region.

  • Deventer: Richard Pafraet established a press in Deventer around 1477. The city, home to the Brethren of the Common Life (whose schools educated Erasmus), became a center for the printing of schoolbooks and theological works.

  • Louvain (Leuven, c. 1473): Flanders’ university city received its first press from John of Westphalia (Johannes de Westfalia) around 1473. The university, founded in 1425, provided a steady market for scholarly texts. Dirk Martens, another influential printer, began work in the 1470s and later became one of Erasmus’s publishers.

  • Antwerp: Though Antwerp would become the printing capital of the Low Countries in the 16th century (the Plantin-Moretus house), printing only arrived there in the late 1470s. The city’s commercial importance as a port and financial center made it a natural location for the book trade.

Printing in the Low Countries had a distinctive character: the region’s prosperous towns, high literacy rates (by medieval standards), and strong tradition of manuscript illumination created a receptive market. The so-called “Coster question” — the claim by some Dutch scholars that Laurens Janszoon Coster of Haarlem had invented movable type before Gutenberg — arose from this region. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Spain

Printing reached Spain relatively late compared to Italy and France, but spread quickly once established.

  • Valencia (1474): The first press in Spain was established by Lambert Palmart, a German from Cologne, who printed the first dated Spanish book, the Obres e trobes en lahors de la Verge Maria, in 1474. The exact date is March 25, 1474.

  • Barcelona (c. 1475): The first press in Barcelona was set up by two German printers, Johann Gherlinc and Johann of Salzburg (also known as Johannes de San Ciuto). Barcelona quickly developed a significant printing industry, producing Catalan-language works alongside Latin.

  • Seville (c. 1477): The first press in Seville was established by Meinard Ungut (a German) and Stanislaus Polonus (a Pole). As the gateway to the New World, Seville would later become the center for books shipped to the Americas.

By 1500, printing presses were active in at least a dozen Spanish cities, including Salamanca, Zamora, Burgos, and Zaragoza. The Complutensian Polyglot Bible, printed at Alcala de Henares under the patronage of Cardinal Cisneros (1514—1517), would become Spain’s most famous early printing achievement. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

England

Printing reached England later than any other major European region, introduced not by a German immigrant but by an Englishman who had learned the trade abroad.

  • Westminster (1476): William Caxton (c. 1422—1492), an English merchant, diplomat, and translator, learned printing in Cologne and practiced it in Bruges (where he printed the Recuyell of the Historyes of Troye, the first book printed in English, in 1473). In 1476, he returned to England and established his press at the sign of the Red Pale in the precincts of Westminster Abbey. His first dated English-printed book was The Dictes or Sayengis of the Philosophres (1477), a translation by Earl Rivers.

Caxton’s press was not a commercial operation on the scale of Venice or Nuremberg, but it was culturally decisive: Caxton printed over one hundred works, many of them in English translation (Chaucer’s Canterbury Tales, Malory’s Le Morte d’Arthur, Aesop’s Fables), and his linguistic choices helped standardize the emerging English literary language. After his death in 1492, his foreman Wynkyn de Worde took over the press and moved it to Fleet Street, establishing the pattern of London’s book trade for centuries. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Oxford received its first press in 1478 (printer: Theodoric Rood of Cologne), and St. Albans in 1479 (a schoolmaster-printer), but English printing remained overwhelmingly concentrated in London until well into the 16th century.

The German Immigrant Printer

A pattern recurs across every region to which printing spread: the first printer was almost always a German immigrant. Sweynheym and Pannartz in Subiaco, Johannes de Spira in Venice, Ulrich Gering in Paris, Lambert Palmart in Valencia, Meinard Ungut in Seville, Theodoric Rood in Oxford — all were Germans or had learned their craft in the German-speaking lands.

This was no accident. The practical knowledge of typefounding, punchcutting, and presswork could not be learned from books; it was transmitted through apprenticeship and hands-on experience. The Mainz workshops, established by Gutenberg in the 1440s and expanded by Fust and Schoeffer in the 1450s, were the only places in Europe where a printer could acquire this training before 1462. After the sack of Mainz, these trained printers carried the technology with them, and their apprenticeships became the seed from which national printing traditions grew. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The German printers did not merely transplant a technique; they adapted it. In Italy, Sweynheym and Pannartz moved from Gothic Textura to a semi-Gothic and then to roman type. In Paris, Gering began with roman type at the Sorbonne, influenced by Venetian models. In Spain, Palmart and Ungut developed hybrid types that blended Gothic and roman characteristics. The technology was standard, but its typographic expression diversified according to local scribal traditions and reader expectations. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Significance

The spread of printing was the first large-scale diffusion of an information technology in European history. It transformed a secret art concentrated in one mid-sized Rhenish city into a pan-European industry within a single generation. The Printer’s Dispersal of 1462 — a political catastrophe that destroyed Mainz’s printing monopoly — paradoxically ensured the technology’s triumph.

By 1500, the infrastructure of the modern book trade was in place: typefoundries, presses, publishing houses, book fairs (notably Frankfurt and Lyon), distribution networks, and a literate reading public. The spread of printing laid the material foundation for the Renaissance, the Reformation, and the Scientific Revolution. Every subsequent revolution in communication — from the steam press to the internet — follows a pattern first established by the German journeymen who carried Gutenberg’s invention out of Mainz in 1462. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

George Haven Putnam, whose second volume of Books and their Makers during the Middle Ages (1896) provides the most detailed English-language account of the early book trade, emphasizes that the spread of printing was driven not merely by technical capability but by “the requirement” of a Europe hungry for books: “With the requirement came, as always, the men. The press that Gutenberg had given to the world was not allowed to rust for want of plucky and public-spirited printers to develop its full scope and usefulness.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

This specialisation meant that a scholar seeking a Greek edition went to Venice, a lawyer seeking a law commentary went to Lyon, a bishop ordering a missal went to Mainz or Paris, and a merchant wanting a practical almanac went to the nearest local printer. The printing press was not a uniform technology — it adapted to each city’s economic and intellectual ecology.

Sixteenth-Century Expansion: Beyond Europe

The first generation of printing (1450-1500) had been a European phenomenon confined to the continent. The sixteenth century saw printing cross the Atlantic, penetrate Eastern Europe and Russia, and reach Asia through Jesuit missionary presses. The German printer diaspora that had driven the first wave was gradually supplanted by local printing traditions, but the pattern of itinerant printers carrying the technology to new territories continued. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Scandinavia

Printing reached Scandinavia in the incunable period but took hold only slowly. Denmark received its first book from the traveling German printer Johann Snell, who printed a Latin account of the Siege of Rhodes in Odense (1482). The first Danish New Testament was printed not in Denmark but in Leipzig (1524), reflecting the absence of a mature domestic printing industry. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

In Sweden, Snell also printed the first book, the Dialogus creaturum moralizatus (1483). He was followed by Johann Fabri, whose widow completed the Breviarium Upsalense in 1496. Then came “an interruption of over fifty years, during which no trace of the art is found in Sweden.” Printing revived with the Reformation: the first Swedish Bible was printed in 1521, and the New Testament in Finnish appeared in 1548. King Charles IX established a royal printing press in 1594. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Norway received printing even later. The first book printed in Norway was produced in Christiania (Oslo) in 1643 by a traveling printer, Tyge Nielsen from Copenhagen. A permanently established press arrived only with a German printer, Valentin Kuhn. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Poland and Eastern Europe

Krakow, the seat of Polish learning, had a press from 1491 (Swaybold Frank), who also printed works in Cyrillic script for East Slavic markets. Hebrew printing began in Krakow in 1517 and flourished under Isaac ben Aaron Prostitz (see hebrew-and-oriental-printing). The first Bible in the local language was printed in Brzesc (the Radziwill Bible, 1563), and the Bohemian Brethren printed a six-volume quarto Bible at Kralitz in Moravia (1579-1593). ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Russia

The first press in Russia was established in Moscow in 1553 under Grand Prince Ivan Vasilievich (Ivan the Terrible). The press was operated by the deacon Ivan Feodorov and Timofey Mstislavzoff, under the supervision of the Dane Hans Hannsen. Their most famous production was the Apostol (Acts of the Apostles), printed in Church Slavonic in 1564 — the first dated printed book in Russia. Only a single copy survives, in the library of the Academy of Sciences in St. Petersburg. The Russian press produced primarily liturgical works in Cyrillic type, which required its own typefounding tradition independent of the Latin and Gothic types of Western Europe. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Americas

The Spanish were the first to bring printing to the New World, “a full hundred years before North America had a press.” The first press in the Americas was established in Mexico City around 1539 by Juan Pablos (Giovanni Paoli), an Italian printer working under the sponsorship of the Cromberger firm of Seville. Juan Cromberger, a German-descended printer of Seville, sent Pablos with a press and types to Mexico to supply the colonial market. The earliest surviving Mexican-printed book is the Manual de Adultos (1540), a liturgical work. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The output of the American presses was “mainly religious in content,” but also included historical works and many linguistic books in indigenous languages for missionary use. Printing followed the Spanish colonial administration: presses were established in Lima (Peru) and Santo Domingo later in the century, always under Church and Crown control. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Asia: Jesuit Missionary Presses

The Jesuits brought European-style printing to Asia before any colonial administration did. In Japan, a book on the lives of the Apostles was printed in European type at Tacaco (on the island of Nippon) in 1591, with a vocabulary appended. The Jesuits also printed at Amacusa, and Nagasaki had “a fairly active press” by the end of the sixteenth century. These presses produced Christian texts in Japanese using both Latin characters and Japanese script, for the use of converts and missionaries. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

In China, the Jesuits operated concealed presses in Peking (Beijing) from the early seventeenth century. The oldest known Peking-printed European-style book is a Chinese-language catechism titled Coelestis doctrinae vera ratio (1603). The Jesuit Nicolas Trigault published a Chinese dictionary in three volumes in Nanjing around 1620. These presses were clandestine — the Chinese imperial authorities did not authorize European printing — and their output was limited to missionary materials. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Africa

The only recorded African press from the early period was a brief appearance on the island of Sao Tome (1494), where a printer named Valentin of Moravia produced liturgical works for the Portuguese colonial and missionary presence. There is no evidence of sustained printing on the African mainland until much later. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Significance of the Second Wave

The sixteenth-century expansion of printing meant that by 1600, the technology had reached every continent except Australia and Antarctica. The pattern established by the Mainz diaspora — itinerant printers carrying type and presses to new territories, adapting their output to local languages and authorities — repeated itself in Mexico City, Moscow, and Nagasaki. But the second wave was qualitatively different: printing was now carried not by independent craftsmen but by organized institutions — the Spanish colonial administration, the Russian Orthodox Church, and the Society of Jesus. The age of the wandering German printer was giving way to the age of the corporate press. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Seventeenth-Century Expansion: The Global Press

The seventeenth century extended printing to North America, deepened its presence in Asia, and saw the technology become a truly global medium. The Dutch Republic, the English colonial administration, and the missionary societies replaced the Spanish and Portuguese as the primary carriers of the press to new territories. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

North America

The first press in North America was established in Cambridge, Massachusetts in 1638. The equipment was brought from England by the Puritan minister Joseph Glover, who died during the voyage; his widow carried out the plan and set up the press. The first printer was Stephen Daye (or Day), and the first known product was The Freemans Oath (1639). The press was later moved to Boston and operated by Samuel Green. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

From the start, the North American press was different from its European counterparts. Lorck notes that the press in North America “could not draw its principal nourishment from science and literature as in Europe” because the colonies were “struggling for political and material existence.” Its main task was “the promotion of civil liberty and political education, as well as the strengthening of faith among the adherents of the many religious sects.” ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The colonial authorities were ambivalent about the press. In 1662, Massachusetts appointed official censors and passed a law that “no press other than the one in Cambridge should be set up within their jurisdiction.” Full press freedom did not arrive until around 1755. For most of the seventeenth century, “many books were still printed in England” for the colonial market. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Boston received its first press under John Forster; the second was operated by Samuel Green and continued by his brother Bartholomew Green, who in 1704 started the first American newspaper, The Boston News Letter. In 1709, Green printed a psalter in an indigenous American language. James Franklin (older brother of Benjamin) arrived in Boston in 1717 with a press and types learned in England, and started the Boston Gazette (1719) and later the New England Courant. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Benjamin Franklin (1706-1790) began his career as a printer’s apprentice to his brother James, later establishing his own press in Philadelphia with Hugh Meredith. He published Poor Richard’s Almanack for 25 years and printed the earliest known German-language book in America (1730). Franklin’s press also served as a vehicle for his civic and scientific work; his later political career as a statesman and diplomat made him the most famous printer in American history. His self-written epitaph reads: “The body of Benjamin Franklin, Printer, (like the cover of an old book, its contents worn out, and stript of its lettering and gilding) lies here, food for worms!” ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

German-American printing began with Christoph Sauer (Christopher Sower), a German immigrant who established a press in Pennsylvania. Sauer’s most important undertaking was the German Lutheran Bible (1743), 1,284 pages in royal quarto, printed in 1,200 copies and bound in leather. For this work, he established the first type foundry in America (1740). This was the first Bible in a European language printed in North America; the first English-language American Bible appeared only in 1782, under Oxford University’s monopoly. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

South America

Printing continued to expand in Spanish America. Presses were established in Lima (Peru) and other colonial centres, always under Church and Crown control. A secret Jesuit press operated in Cordoba (in present-day Argentina) in the seventeenth century, producing clandestine religious works. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Asia: India and Southeast Asia

European printing in Asia expanded beyond the Jesuit missions to include Protestant missionary and colonial presses.

In India, the first European-style press was established in Goa (1563), the earliest Portuguese settlement on the west coast. In Tranquebar on the Coromandel Coast, the London Society for the Propagation of the Gospel established a press in 1569, printing a New Testament in quarto, prayer books, and catechisms in Portuguese, English, Danish, and several Asian dialects. When Tranquebar came under Danish control, the Danish mission became active: Bartholomaeus Ziegenbalg and Heinrich Plutschau brought a press and types from Germany. Tamil type was cast at the orphanage press in Halle and brought to India by J.G. Adler, who published the four Gospels and Acts (1714) and the complete Tamil Bible (1723, three parts in quarto). ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

In the East Indies, the Dutch East India Company (VOC) established presses in Batavia (modern Jakarta, Java) at the end of the seventeenth century. On Ceylon (Sri Lanka), Governor G.W. von Imhof ordered a press set up in Colombo in 1737 for printing Gospels in local languages, which later also produced scholarly works. In Manila (Philippines), printing was active from around 1600, and in the eighteenth century Catholic missionaries there competed with the Protestants in Tranquebar for evangelical printing. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Lorck’s Conclusion

Lorck’s handbook closes its survey of the spread of printing with a summary:

“Thus the art of Gutenberg, which in its first hundred years had already made its way across all of Europe and achieved a high level of development… was spread across the entire world. Already by the time which forms our provisional boundary, the words spoken a hundred years later held true:

‘Across the expanse of the Ocean into the farthest regions of the Earth, Carries, Celebrated one, your Fame, never resting in flight; You need no stone, nor cast bronze, For every line of the book proclaims your praise.‘” ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]
