Early Printers’ Tools and Techniques

The first printing offices of the 1450s were experimental workshops where johann-gutenberg and his contemporaries assembled a new technology from the tools and materials of several existing trades — the goldsmith’s graver, the vintner’s screw press, the papermaker’s vat, and the scribe’s ink pot. Every element of the typographic process — from the steel punch to the printed sheet — had to be invented, refined, and brought into coordination. This entry surveys the complete toolkit and workflow of the early printer.

Punchcutting

The punch (German: Stempel) was the starting point of all type production. A punch was a steel bar, roughly the size of a chisel shank, with a single letter engraved in reverse and in relief on its end.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Materials and technique. The punch-maker first learned to temper steel — heating and quenching it until it reached a strength that stopped short of brittleness. Using a score of different graving tools (files, gravers, scorpers) with minute scooped and angled tips, the punch-cutter scraped away tiny flakes of steel, typically 0.01 mm thick. Fred Smeijers described the sensation of cutting well-tempered steel as “much like cold butter.”^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] For inaccessible interior spaces — the counters of letters like ‘e’, ‘a’, ‘o’ — punch-cutters often used counter-punches (small steel stamps struck into the punch face to create the recessed area).

Time and skill. Cutting a single punch took a day or more. A full font of type for a book like the Gutenberg Bible required between 200 and 300 distinct characters (accounting for ligatures, abbreviations, and variant sorts), meaning the punchcutting phase alone could occupy a skilled goldsmith for a year or more.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md][raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] Only a few fifteenth-century printers — Jenson, Cennini, Sweinheym, Veldener — cut their own punches. Most bought matrices or type from established printers.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The Matrix

The matrix (German: Mater) was a small bar of soft copper or brass into which the hardened steel punch was driven, creating a sunken (intaglio) impression.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md][raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Striking the matrix. The punch was positioned on the copper bar and struck with a hammer. For the large types of Gutenberg’s Bibles and the 1457 Psalter, copper softened by heat was likely used to prevent the punch from shattering.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] After striking, the matrix was filed and justified — its sides squared and its depth adjusted — so every type cast from it would stand at precisely the correct height (approximately 0.918 inches, or 24 mm, the French standard type-high).^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md][raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Trade in matrices. Matrices were valuable trade goods. The same typeface appears in books printed by Caxton, Van der Goes, Leeu, Bellaert, Machlinia, and Veldener, proving that matrices struck from the same punches circulated among multiple printing shops.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] The Ripoli Press bought a full font of Roman matrices from a punch-cutter named John Peter in 1477 for 10 florins in gold.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The Adjustable Hand Mold

The hand mold (German: Giessinstrument) was Gutenberg’s central mechanical invention — the device that made practical movable-type economically viable.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md][raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Construction. The hand mold consisted of two interlocking halves, made of brass or another hard metal. The matrix was fitted into one half at the bottom of the mold cavity. The two halves could be slid apart or together, adjusting the width of the cavity to accommodate letters of different widths (a wide ‘m’ vs. a narrow ‘i’) while keeping the body height — the kegel or point size — perfectly constant.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md][raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Gutenberg’s key insight. Before the adjustable mold, anyone who attempted movable type had to carve each letter individually — too slow and too imprecise. The hand mold allowed rapid, accurate mass production: an experienced typefounder could cast four sorts (individual types) per minute, several hundred per day.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] The mold was held together by screws; when disassembled, its pieces looked like meaningless fragments of a three-dimensional jigsaw — a built-in security measure that kept Gutenberg’s secret safe.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Irregular bodies. Because early printers used a single adjustable mold for multiple body sizes, types of the fifteenth century were made without systematic graduation. There were wide gaps between larger bodies and confusing proximities between smaller ones. No printer of the incunable period had any idea of a regularly graduated series of sizes.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md][raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] This primitive adjustable mold was later discarded in favor of dedicated molds for each body.

Type Metal Alloy

The type metal had to meet contradictory requirements: melt at a moderate temperature, flow into the fine details of the matrix, shrink minimally on cooling, and be hard enough to withstand thousands of impressions without deforming.

Composition. The early typefounders, drawing on the metalworking knowledge of goldsmiths and coin-makers, used an alloy of:

  • Lead — the base metal, low melting point (327 degrees C)
  • Tin — improved fluidity and added some hardness
  • Antimony — the critical addition; reduced shrinkage and greatly increased hardness

The Ripoli Press cost book of 1481 records “lead, tin, and obscurely mentions another” metal — almost certainly antimony.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] This composition is essentially the same as modern type metal.

Casting the type. The type caster sat before a low furnace with a melting pot set into it. The hand mold, with matrix fitted, was held in one hand. With the other hand, a ladle of molten metal was poured into the mold’s mouth. The caster gave a sharp jerk to force the metal into every fine line of the matrix. The metal cooled almost instantly. The mold was opened, and the finished type — still with the jet (or Anguss), the excess metal from the pouring channel, attached — was ejected.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md][raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Dressing the type. The jet was broken or cut off. The type was rubbed smooth on its sides and feet — a process called dressing — to ensure it stood perfectly upright and square. Unlike modern types, early types had no nick (groove on the side to guide the compositor’s fingers); instead, some had a small circular depression to indicate orientation. A surviving accidental impression from Conrad Winters’ press in Cologne (1476) shows a type with “a circular mark, about one-tenth of an inch diameter, on the side of the type” — a guide for the compositor.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md][raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] Early types also had sloping shoulders (the “beard”) rather than the later square shoulder, a design that prevented the paper from blackening.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

No surviving types. Not one of the millions of types founded in the fifteenth century has survived. The only evidence of their form comes from accidental impressions like the Winters press incident.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md][raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Compositor’s Tools

The type case. Types were stored in a divided wooden case. Capitals (majuscules) were kept in the upper compartment; small letters (minuscules) in the lower — the origin of the terms upper case and lower case.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] The number of compartments varied; early fonts included many ligatures (ff, fi, ffi, ffl) and abbreviations that increased the character set to 200 or more distinct sorts.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The composing stick. The compositor worked with a composing stick (German: Winkelhaken) — a simple wooden strip of deal with a fixed side and an end-piece tacked on. Unlike the modern iron stick with an adjustable screw and knee, the early stick was made for a single measure; a new stick or a retacking of the movable piece was required for each different line length.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md][raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] Some very early printers (the “unknown printer” and Albert Pfister) may have had no stick at all, taking types directly from the case and wedging them into mortised wooden blocks that served as chases.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Composing rule. Composing rules (thin strips of metal to separate lines) were not used by early printers. Lines were placed directly on top of one another, making justification difficult because types could not slide easily across the rough surface.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md][raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Dictation method. Conrad Zeltner, a learned printer of the seventeenth century, recorded that early compositors often set type by dictation rather than reading copy. A reader would dictate aloud to three or four compositors at once, from different pages or different manuscripts simultaneously.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] Madden’s analysis of a Weidenbach office demonstrates that three distinct compositors working on the same book produced characteristic errors only explainable by dictation.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] This method was abandoned as compositors became less educated.

The Printing Press

The early printing press was an adaptation of the screw press used for wine-making and paper-drying. Hoe describes Gutenberg’s press as “two upright timbers, with cross pieces of wood to stay them together at the top and bottom,” with a wooden screw passing through the upper cross piece to lower a wooden platen onto the type.^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md]

Parts of the press. De Vinne (from the Badius illustrations) describes:

  • Cheeks: two upright beams forming the framework
  • Cap: a thick cross-piece at the top holding the screw and spindle
  • Till: a large square collar steadying the spindle’s descent
  • Spindle and screw: the wooden screw that drove the platen downward
  • Platen: the flat pressing surface (only 9 by 14 inches — less than half the bed)
  • Bed (of stone): the flat surface on which the forme rested, set in a wooden “coffin”
  • Bar: the handle used to turn the screw
  • Tympan: a hinged frame covered with cloth or parchment, onto which the paper was placed
  • Frisket: a paper-covered frame hinged to the tympan, with the printing area cut away to prevent the sheet from touching non-printing areas
  • Points: pins on the tympan that perforated the paper for registration when printing the reverse side (the two-pull system)^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md][raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The two-pull system. Because the platen was smaller than the bed, the press could print only one half of one side of a sheet at a time. The pressman pulled the bar to bring the platen down on half the forme, then pushed the bed forward and pulled again to print the other half. This meant each sheet required four pulls: two for the front, two for the back.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Blankets and damp paper. A woolen blanket (in two or more thicknesses) was placed between the platen and the paper to diffuse the impression and compensate for irregularities in the type height, paper thickness, and platen flatness. Paper was dampened before printing — each sheet received just enough moisture to soften the fibers without dissolving them, allowing the type to make a clean, crisp impression.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md][raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Inking: Balls and Ink

Inking balls. Types were inked using balls (German: Ballen) — pads made of untanned sheepskin, stuffed hard with wool or horsehair, and mounted with wooden handles.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md][raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md] The pressman (or his assistant the beater) worked the two balls against each other to distribute the gluey ink evenly across their curved surfaces, then beat them on the type. This was a slow, messy process — and one that could accidently pull out ill-justified type, as happened at Conrad Winters’ press.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md][raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Printing ink composition. Water-based inks (used for block printing and manuscript writing) would not adhere properly to metal type. Gutenberg developed a revolutionary oil-based formula:

  • Linseed oil — boiled to the consistency of a thick varnish, forming the vehicle
  • Lampblack (soot from burned oil or resin) — the black pigment, carefully roasted to degrease it
  • Amber, rosin, varnish — to adjust body and drying properties^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The Ripoli Press cost book of 1481 lists the ingredients as linseed oil, turpentine, Greek pitch, black pitch, marcassite, vermilion, rosin, hard and liquid varnish, nutgalls, vitriol, and shellac.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md][raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] Nutgalls and vitriol were carryovers from writing-ink recipes; shellac and varnish gave the ink gloss.

Quality and appearance. Early printing ink varied enormously in quality. Jenson’s ink in Venice appears as “an intense, velvety, glossy black”; the Gutenberg Bible’s ink is “a strong, permanent black, without gloss”; in other books the ink is “dingy and smearing” or “a paste color which can be rubbed off with a sponge.”^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] The microscopic examination of early inks shows the black pigment is not fine and not thoroughly mixed with proper drying oil.

Proofreading and Correction

Early methods. The proofreading system of the first printing offices is not fully clear. Madden identified curious errors in three copies of a book printed at Weidenbach about 1464, suggesting each compositor read his own proof — and read it badly.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] In other offices, in-house correctors or hired scholars read proofs.

Learned correctors. Many printers enlisted eminent scholars to serve as correctors or press editors. These men did double duty, correcting both compositors’ errors and the faults of the manuscript copy — which, as copyists were notoriously underpaid and careless, were often extensive.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] The first table of errata, two folio pages, was issued by Gabriel Peter of Venice in 1478.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Accuracy of incunabula. Prosper Marchand wrote forcefully against the common prejudice that early printed books were more accurate than modern ones: “They are deceived who think that books are accurate in proportion to their age. For the most part, the older they are, the more inaccurate they are.”^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] Yet even inaccurate early editions were superior to scribal copies: printing prevented the progressive debasement of texts, and faulty editions could be corrected and replaced.

Division of Labor

The early printing shop organized work into distinct roles, a division that persisted for centuries:

  • Punchcutter (Stempelschneider): a specialized goldsmith who engraved the steel punches
  • Typefounder/Type-caster (Giesser): cast type from matrices and the hand mold
  • Compositor (Setzer): assembled type into pages. Compositors were generally more educated and higher-status than pressmen; in some shops (Italy, Paris), women worked as compositors
  • Pressman (Drucker): operated the press, including pulling the bar
  • Beater (Farbreiber or Ballenmeister): applied ink to the forme using the ink balls (on early presses, two pressmen were required — one to ink and one to pull)
  • Corrector (Korrektor): a scholar-editor who read proofs
  • Publisher (Verleger): the investor or master printer who financed, coordinated, and marketed the books

Apprentices who wished to become master printers paid a premium and learned primarily composition and the theoretical knowledge of type-casting and presswork — sufficient to direct workmen.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md][raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Pace of Work

Philip de Lignamine stated that the printers of Mainz “printed three hundred sheets a day.”^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] Considering the two-pull system and the manual inking, this was a small but steady output. By comparison, a hand-pressman of 1840 — with an inking machine and a single-pull platen covering the full bed — could produce about 1,500 impressions per day. The early pressman’s output was thus roughly one-eighth of his nineteenth-century counterpart’s.

The Gutenberg Bible, with some 3 million characters across 1,286 pages (about 180 copies), required an estimated two years of continuous presswork with multiple presses running simultaneously.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] Six compositors and twelve printers (two to a press) likely worked on the Bible at peak production.

Permanence of the Method

Despite the crudeness of the early tools — wooden presses, saw-cut types, rough paper, handmade ink — the fundamental method invented by Gutenberg proved remarkably stable. As De Vinne noted: “The punch, matrix and mould, the tympan, frisket and points, the use of damp paper and oily ink, of curved surfaces for applying the ink, and of blankets for diffusing the impression, are still in fashion.”^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] The system endured for more than 350 years, until the industrial cylinder press and machine casting began to transform the trade in the early nineteenth century.
