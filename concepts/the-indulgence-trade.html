The Indulgence Trade

The indulgence was the first great commercial market for European printing. Before Bibles, before psalters, before any bound book, movable-type proved its commercial viability by mass-producing single-sheet documents that the Church sold to remit sin. This trade—the sale of indulgences—financed Gutenberg’s early presses, provided the earliest dated specimens of typography, and later erupted into the controversy that became the reformation.

What an Indulgence Was

An indulgence was not forgiveness of sin itself (that required confession and contrition) but the remission of the temporal punishment still due after sin had been forgiven. The theological underpinning was the Treasury of Merit—the infinite reservoir of grace accumulated by Christ and the saints, which the Pope could dispense to the faithful. Pope Clement VI formalized this doctrine in the bull Unigenitus (1343), declaring that the Pope held the keys to this treasury and could grant indulgences to the living (and, by 1476, to souls in purgatory). ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

An indulgence, therefore, was a certificate stating that the bearer had performed a specified good work—paid alms, prayed, fasted—and thereby qualified to receive remission of temporal punishment for a defined period (e.g., three months, a year) or, in the case of a plenary indulgence, all punishment whatsoever. The document itself—called a confessionalia in Latin or Ablassbrief (‘reduction-letter’) in German—was a contract between the penitent and the Church, with blank spaces left for the name of the buyer, the date, and the place of sale. Once filled in by hand, it became a personalized ticket to shortened time in purgatory—or, for a plenary indulgence, a straight pass to Heaven. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The First Print Run: Cyprus and the Turkish Crusade

In August 1451, Pope Nicholas V granted King John II of Cyprus the right to issue plenary indulgences for three years (1 May 1452 to 1 May 1455) to raise mercenary funds against the advancing Ottoman Turks. Paulinus Zapp (Chappe), the Cypriot ambassador, appointed John de Castro as chief commissioner for Germany. The Archbishop of Mainz, Theodoric, granted permission to sell within his diocese. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Sales were initially slow. Then Constantinople fell on 29 May 1453. The shock across Europe was immediate. John de Castro, panicking that Cyprus had fallen too, embezzled the collected money and was arrested and imprisoned—a scandal that further injured the market. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

With the indulgence’s expiry date of 1 May 1455 fast approaching, Zappe needed to distribute thousands of documents urgently. Hand-copying was too slow, too error-prone, and too vulnerable to forgery. He turned to johann-gutenberg’s new printing press in mainz. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Gutenberg’s Indulgence Editions

Three distinct editions of the indulgence letter were printed, known today by their line-counts: approximately 30, 31, and 32 lines (De Vinne uses 30, 31; modern scholarship typically refers to the 31-line and 28-line editions). Each was a single sheet of parchment, about 9 x 13 inches, printed on one side only, with movable type, black oil-based ink, and blank spaces for the buyer’s name, the date, and the seller’s signature. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The three editions used different typefaces, cast from different matrices and set by different compositors:

  • The 31-line edition used a display line in the large Double-pica body type (the same type used for the 36-line Bible and the Donatus), with the text body in a smaller type on English body. The large display types are identifiable as the lettres de forme (Pointed Gothic), the text as lettres de somme (Round Gothic). ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]
  • The other editions used types on Paragon body and a Pica body, some of which match the type of the 42-line Bible. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]
  • The small text type used in one edition is unique—it appears in no other known work. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The presswork is good, done on a properly constructed screw press with damp vellum and oil-based ink, but the typography has blemishes: abbreviations are frequent, lines are unevenly spaced, the capital letters are rudely cut. One surviving copy shows a white gap below the sixteenth line where the types were not securely fastened in the chase. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Surviving Copies and Their Significance

Twenty-three copies of these indulgence letters survive (De Vinne in 1876 counted eighteen; Lorck by 1882 counted twenty-three; modern scholarship counts at least twenty-three, with new fragments occasionally discovered in book bindings). They date from 25 November 1454 to 30 April 1455—the period of the last desperate sales push. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

These surviving copies are historically invaluable because they are the earliest printed documents with a firm date. The 31-line edition carries the printed year 1454 (MCCCCLIIII); the others carry 1455. This makes them the cornerstone of Gutenberg chronology: they prove that by late 1454 Gutenberg not only had a working press but was operating at least two workshops (the Gutenberghof and the Humbrechthof), with two distinct typefaces being run simultaneously. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The indulgences also provide the first solid evidence of a demand for printing—a customer (the Church) needing thousands of identical copies of the same text faster than scribes could produce them. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

After 1 May 1455, the indulgences became worthless. Their parchment sheets were recycled by bookbinders as waste leaves for binding pastedowns, which is how most copies survived to be discovered centuries later. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Financial Engine

The indulgence trade was not a one-off job for Gutenberg; it was a recurring revenue stream that sustained his press between larger projects. John Man notes that by late 1454 indulgences were “flowing from his presses,” churning out by the thousand for sale across the Frankfurt-Mainz-Cologne region. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] The income from indulgences, Donatus grammars, and the Sibylline Prophecies just about covered his costs and paid the interest on the loans from johannes-fust, even as the monumental 42-line Bible was being typeset in the second workshop. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The business model was elegant:

  • The Church provided the text and the authority (the papal bull).
  • The commissioner (Zapp/de Castro) handled distribution and collection.
  • The printer provided the press, the type, the paper or vellum, and the labor.
  • The product was a blank form—the text was fixed, but names, dates, and places were filled in by hand at the point of sale. This made the document both standardized and individualized, and it meant thousands of copies could be run from a single setting of type.

This “blank form” model—printing identical documents with variable fields—was printing’s first killer app. It was the fifteenth-century equivalent of printing tax forms, banknotes, or contracts. It required no bookbinding, no rubrication, no complex page layout—just speed, accuracy, and volume. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The Business of Selling Salvation

Indulgence selling was a deeply corrupt business even by the standards of the medieval Church. The De Vinne source describes the scandal of John de Castro’s embezzlement and notes that the 1454 indulgences “give us a glimmer of the corruption of some of the men who sold the indulgences—a corruption which, in the next century, brought down upon the sellers and the system the scorn of Holbein and the wrath of Luther.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The illustration reproduced in De Vinne—a Holbein satire—shows a canon absolving a kneeling young man while pointing at a money-chest, three Dominicans greedily counting coins at a table, a penniless cripple being turned away, and the Pope enthroned granting commissions. The apparatus of the indulgence trade—commissioners, sub-agents, preachers, collectors—created a sprawling commercial network that reached every parish in Germany. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The Road to Reformation

The indulgence trade that Gutenberg helped launch with his press in 1454 came back to haunt the Church seventy years later. In 1515, Pope Leo X authorized a new plenary indulgence to finance the completion of St. Peter’s Basilica. The marketing was entrusted to Johann Tetzel, a Dominican monk whose sales pitch was legendary: “As soon as the coin in the coffer rings, the soul from purgatory springs.” Tetzel’s campaign was backed by printed indulgences—by then a well-established genre of print. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

In Wittenberg, Martin Luther was appalled. On 31 October 1517, he sent his Ninety-Five Theses (strictly, a disputation on the power and efficacy of indulgences) to Archbishop Albrecht of Mainz. Within weeks, the theses were printed and circulating across Germany—in Leipzig, Basel, Nuremberg, and Wittenberg itself. As John Man puts it: “The noises that accompanied Luther’s message of doom were probably not hammer-blows; they were the squeaks and bangs of busy printing presses.” ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The same technology that Gutenberg had used to mass-produce indulgences was now being used to mass-produce arguments against them. The reformation became the first great European movement to exploit print at scale: Luther’s writings, vernacular Bibles, pamphlets, and broadsides circulated faster and farther than manuscript culture ever allowed. The indulgence trade, which had proved printing’s commercial viability in the 1450s, proved its revolutionary power in the 1510s.

Summary

The indulgence trade sits at the crossroads of church finance, commercial printing, and religious upheaval:

  • It was the first large-scale commercial application of movable type.
  • It produced the earliest dated printed documents (1454-1455), anchoring Gutenberg’s chronology.
  • It demonstrated the blank-form business model that would later underpin official printing (tax forms, legal documents, papal bulls).
  • It created the financial bridge that kept Gutenberg’s press running while the Bible was being produced.
  • Its abuse triggered the Reformation, which in turn proved print’s power to spread ideas faster than any institution could suppress them.
