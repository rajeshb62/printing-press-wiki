Block Printing (Xylography)

Block printing (also called xylography, from Greek xylon “wood” + graphein “to write”) is a printing technique in which an entire page — text and images alike — is carved as a single raised surface on a block of wood. The relief surface is inked and pressed against paper; the carved-away areas remain blank. Unlike movable-type, where individual letters can be rearranged and reused, each xylographic block is a fixed, permanent unit: the image and text on that block can never be separated or rearranged.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

De Vinne captures the essence of the distinction: “The fixedness of the design on the block is the great feature which separates xylography from typography. The printing surfaces of the two methods are alike. Types and xylographic engravings are printed together, by the same process, and on the same press.”^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] Block printing was the dominant form of mechanical text reproduction in East Asia for centuries and the precursor to Gutenberg’s invention in Europe.

History in China

Block printing was invented in China during the Tang dynasty (618—907 CE). The fundamental technique — carving characters in relief on a wooden block, inking the raised surface, and transferring the image to paper by rubbing — was a natural extension of earlier Chinese practices of seal-carving and stone-rubbing.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Chinese tradition credits the invention of block printing to Foong-Taou, a minister of state, who dampened a written sheet of paper, pressed it onto a smooth wood surface to transfer the ink, and then cut away the field to leave the characters in relief.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] The method was refined over the following centuries.

The Chinese method used a fluid ink (lampblack mixed with water or size), a brush for inking, and a second dry brush for rubbing the paper against the block. No press was required. Paper was printed on one side only; the printed sheets were folded at the uncut edge and stitched at the cut edge, producing the characteristic Chinese book format.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The Technique

The process of block printing followed these steps:

  1. Preparation of the block: A board of hardwood (pear, cherry, or plum wood in Europe; typically boxwood or pear in China), about ½—¾ inch thick, was planed smooth and sized with a rice-paste solution to close the pores.
  2. Transfer of the design: Text and images were written or drawn on transparent paper, then transferred face-down to the block by rubbing. The design appeared in reverse on the wood surface.
  3. Carving: The engraver cut away the field (the non-printing areas) with knives and gouges, leaving the design standing in relief. Every line of text had to be carved meticulously, letter by letter — there was no way to reuse a letter for a different page.
  4. Inking: The raised surface was brushed or dabbed with ink.
  5. Impression: Paper was laid on the inked block and rubbed with a hard leather pad (the Reiber or burnisher), or in later European practice, pressed by a screw press adapted from the wine/linen press.

The earliest European prints were rub-prints (German: Reiberdrucke). The Reiber pressure was so forceful that it left a deep indentation in the paper, making it impossible to print the reverse side. These early prints are anopistographic (printed on one side only). To create a book, the printed leaves were folded and pasted together at the inner edges — exactly as Chinese books were made.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Early European Use

Block printing appears in Europe around the end of the 14th century, though its precise origins are undocumented. De Vinne notes: “There is no record, nor even any tradition, concerning an invention of xylography.”^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Playing Cards

Playing cards were among the earliest products of European block printing. Cards reached Italy from the Islamic world around 1300 and Germany by the last quarter of the 14th century. The demand was enormous, and by about 1410 the cities of Augsburg, Nuremberg, and Ulm had guilds of card-makers (Kartenmacher) and card-painters (Kartenmaler). The earliest cards were printed from carved wood blocks and then hand-colored. De Vinne observes that card-makers also made religious images — the two trades were closely linked.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Textile Printing (Zeugdruck)

Before paper printing became common, block printing was used on textiles. In Italy, as early as the 12th century, silk and linen fabrics were decorated with printed patterns. By the 13th century, printed leather was used for wall-hangings and even bookbindings. Textile printing used similar carved wooden blocks but with colored pigment or mordant pastes rather than black ink.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Religious Woodcuts

From about 1400, block-printed religious images — known in German as Heiligen or Halglein (“little saints”) — became enormously popular. The earliest dated European woodcut is St. Christopher (1423), bearing the image of the saint carrying the Christ child across a river, with a prayer in Latin. Other early subjects included the Virgin Mary, the Crucifixion, the Passion, saints, and the Last Judgment. These cheap prints were sold at churches during pilgrimages and feast days, serving as devotional aids for the common people.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Lorck notes that “the pictorial representations had mainly religious subjects, and the need showed itself especially in times of great religious excitement.”^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] The demand was amplified by religious brotherhoods and pilgrimages.

Block-Books

A block-book (German: Blockbuch or Tafeldruck) is a complete book printed from carved wooden blocks — text and illustration together on each block. About thirty xylographic works survive from the 15th century, the most extensive containing up to 50 leaves. They are of three types: images without text, images with text, and text without images (rare).^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] The artistic quality is generally modest, though some show considerable skill.

The most important block-books are:

Ars Moriendi (“The Art of Dying Well”)

A guide to preparing for a holy death, depicting the five temptations (unbelief, despair, impatience, vainglory, avarice) that assail the dying man, countered by his guardian angel. The xylographic edition in the British Museum (formerly owned by T. O. Weigel of Leipzig) is considered the earliest and most accomplished — 12 leaves in small folio, printed in brown ink, with a conception and execution that Lorck praises as surpassing all other known 15th-century woodcuts in spiritual expression and technical mastery. The work was enormously popular and was translated into every major European language.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Biblia Pauperum (“The Bible of the Poor”)

A typological work pairing Old Testament scenes with their New Testament antitypes. For example, the sacrifice of Isaac is shown alongside the Crucifixion; Jonah emerging from the whale alongside the Resurrection. Each page presents three biblical scenes with explanatory text. The book is probably of Lower Rhenish origin. The name “Bible of the Poor” may refer either to the lesser clergy (who called themselves Pauperes Christi, “Christ’s Poor”) or to the book’s intended readership of those too poor to afford a complete Bible.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Speculum Humanae Salvationis (“The Mirror of Human Salvation”)

The most complex and widely debated of the block-books. Like the Biblia Pauperum, it presents New Testament events in parallel with Old Testament prefigurations. Of the many editions of this popular work, only one has xylographic text (cut into the block alongside the images); the others use movable type for the text while retaining xylographic illustrations.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Speculum Salutis Controversy

The Speculum editions sit at the heart of one of the most contentious debates in early printing history: the Coster controversy. Dutch bibliographers have long claimed that the Speculum editions with typographic text were printed by Lourens Janszoon Coster of Haarlem using movable type — possibly before Gutenberg. Lorck summarizes the Dutch position: “The book is of Lower Rhenish origin and the Dutch declare this late product to be a work printed by Coster with movable types.”^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The controversy arises because the Speculum editions mix technologies: xylographic illustrations with typographic text. Some editions have text cut in the block; others use movable type. This hybrid character makes it difficult to date or attribute them with certainty. Modern scholarship generally rejects Coster’s priority, but the Speculum editions remain valuable evidence of the transitional phase between xylography and typography — a period when printers were experimenting with combining both methods.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Other Block-Books

  • Apocalypse (Historia Sancti Johannis): three editions with 50 illustrations, three with 48.
  • Ars Memorandi: a mnemonic guide to the four Gospels, 15 crude woodcut plates.
  • Der Entkrist (The Antichrist): the legend of the false Messiah, 26 leaves.
  • The Legend of St. Meinrad: 48 xylographic leaves.
  • Ciromantia (Dr. Johann Hartlieb’s Chiromancy): a treatise on palm-reading, 24 leaves printed on both sides.
  • The Calendar of Johannes de Gamundia: the oldest known ephemeris (c. 1470).
  • Donatus (Aelius Donatus, Ars Minor): a Latin grammar in catechism form, one of the most frequently printed schoolbooks of the 15th century — both xylographic and typographic editions survive.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Limitations of the Technique

Block printing had fundamental constraints that made it unsuitable for large-scale book production:

  1. Each page required a separate block: A 100-page book needed 100 individually carved wood blocks, each representing weeks of labor. Every page was a unique, non-reusable artifact.

  2. No correction or revision: If a carving error was made, the entire block or the defective section had to be cut out, plugged with new wood, and re-carved. Comprehensive proofreading before cutting was essential.

  3. Blocks wore and cracked: Wood is not durable under repeated pressure. Blocks split, warped, and the fine lines of small text wore down after a few hundred impressions, requiring recarving.

  4. Storage: A library of books required warehousing hundreds or thousands of blocks. The blocks had to be kept clean and dry to prevent warping and insect damage.

  5. Limited format: Early rubbing methods allowed only one-sided printing. The paste-up format (two leaves glued at the inner edge) was clumsy and structurally weak.

  6. No economy of scale: The same labor was required to carve a block containing the letter “e” ten times as to carve any other letter — there was no reuse of common characters across pages.

As one of Gutenberg’s fictionalized partners in Pearson’s narrative objects: “Block-printing is by no means to be despised in books of a few pages; but in a large book of many pages, we waste time in cutting letters, as they are only of use for that book, and cannot be taken apart and used for another.”^[raw/articles/pearson-gutenberg-and-art-of-printing.md]

Relationship to Gutenberg’s Movable Type

johann-gutenberg began his printing career in Strasbourg in the 1430s with block printing. The Speculum editions (if the traditional account is accepted) were the last block-book projects Gutenberg worked on before turning fully to movable type. Pearson’s narrative dramatizes the transition: Gutenberg’s partners Riffe and Dritzhn argued to keep printing block-books; Gutenberg insisted that for large books, block printing was a dead end.^[raw/articles/pearson-gutenberg-and-art-of-printing.md]

The decisive insight was that block printing’s fundamental limitation — carving each page as a fixed unit — could be overcome by breaking the page into individual, reusable letters. As movable-type replaced xylography for text, block printing did not disappear. It survived as woodcut illustration, combined with movable type in a hybrid process that became standard in early printing. The Gutenberg Bible itself is a pure typographic work, but within a decade, illustrated books like the Nuremberg Chronicle (1493) combined movable type for text with xylographic illustrations — sometimes as many as 2,000 woodcuts in a single volume.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

De Vinne summarizes the relationship: “Xylography, the rudest method, was the first in use; typography, a more useful method, soon followed; copper-plate printing, the artistic method, was the proper culmination. The order of invention was that of progressive development from an imperfect to a perfect method.”^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Even after the screw press was adapted for typography, xylographic blocks continued to be printed on the same press — the printing surfaces of both methods are identical in form (relief). Block printing thus paved the way for Gutenberg’s revolution in two respects: it created a market for printed images and short texts, and it demonstrated the fundamental principle of relief printing that typography would perfect.
