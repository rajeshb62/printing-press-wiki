Hebrew and Oriental Printing

Hebrew printing was a distinct and technically demanding branch of early typography, pursued both by Jewish printers for Jewish communities and by Christian scholars and missionary presses for humanist and evangelical purposes. It required specialized typefaces with vowel points (nikud) and cantillation marks, skilled correctors fluent in the language, and a readership that was simultaneously diasporic (scattered across Europe and the Mediterranean) and concentrated (in urban Jewish quarters and yeshivot). The history of Hebrew printing intersects with every major theme of the early printing revolution: the economics of typefounding, the confessional competition between Catholics and Protestants, the humanist revival of ancient languages, and the spread of printing beyond Latin Christendom. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The First Hebrew Printers: The Soncino Family

The first Hebrew books printed from movable type were produced by the Soncino family, a dynasty of Jewish printers who operated in the Italian town of Soncino (near Cremona) from 1483. John Man’s appendix lists their first press at Soncino in 1483-1484, with subsequent presses in Naples (1490), Barco (1496), Pesaro (1507), and Constantinople (1530). ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Lorck notes that before the sixteenth century, Hebrew printing “was practised only by Jewish printers at Soncino, Naples, Pesaro, and Constantinople.” The technical challenge was considerable: Hebrew script requires elaborate flourishes, vowel points (nikud), and cantillation marks that did not fit easily into the punch-matrix-mold system designed for the simpler Latin alphabet. The Soncino family invested heavily in type-cutting and produced some of the earliest Hebrew Bibles, Talmudic tractates, and liturgical works. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Daniel Bomberg: The Aldus of Hebrew Printing

The single most important figure in the history of Hebrew typography was Daniel Bomberg (c. 1483-1549), a Christian printer from Antwerp who established a press in Venice dedicated exclusively to Hebrew and rabbinical literature. The Lorck source draws the direct parallel: “What the Aldi and the Giunta were for Greek, Latin, and Italian printing, Daniel Bomberg was for Hebrew.” ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Bomberg’s achievements included:

  • Three editions of the Hebrew Bible with the best rabbinical commentaries, in four folio volumes each
  • Five portable editions of the Bible, in smaller formats
  • The Babylonian Talmud in twelve folio volumes — the first complete printed edition, a landmark of Hebrew typography
  • Numerous rabbinical works by medieval and contemporary authors

He employed the scholar Chaja Maier Ben David as chief corrector, working “together with many capable correctors.” His Hebrew types were cut to exceptional standards — Lorck states they “are considered the most beautiful down to the present day” — and a vellum impression of his 1525 Bible at the Wolfenbüttel library is “considered a typographical jewel.” ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Bomberg press was also a commercial venture. Lorck records that Bomberg “is said to have spent between 3-4 million crowns on Hebrew printing, and not to have looked sufficiently to his own profit” — a characterisation that echoes the financial difficulties of Aldus Manutius and suggests that Hebrew printing, like Greek printing, was driven as much by scholarly commitment as by commercial calculation. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

For a detailed biography, see daniel-bomberg.

Hebrew Printing in Eastern Europe

Hebrew printing spread eastward in the sixteenth century. In Krakow, Jews “began Hebrew printing in 1517, which flourished greatly.” The most notable Krakow Hebrew printer was Isaac ben Aaron Prostitz (active c. 1550), who printed both the Babylonian and Jerusalem Talmuds along with many other works. The Krakow Hebrew presses served the large Jewish populations of Poland-Lithuania and produced editions that circulated across Eastern Europe. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

In Prague, Lorck records that the city “has some significant Hebrew prints to show.” The Prague Hebrew press was active from the early sixteenth century and produced important editions of biblical and rabbinical texts. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Hebrew printing also appeared in the Ottoman Empire. The Soncino family eventually moved to Constantinople, where they continued printing Hebrew texts. Jewish refugees from the Spanish expulsion of 1492 brought knowledge of printing to the eastern Mediterranean, establishing presses in Salonika, Constantinople, and elsewhere. John Man notes that “knowledge of printing came with Jewish refugees fleeing from persecution in Spain to Constantinople.” ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Christian Hebrew Scholarship and Printing

Hebrew printing was not only a Jewish enterprise. The humanist revival of ancient languages created a Christian market for Hebrew texts, especially the Hebrew Bible and grammars.

Johannes Reuchlin (1455-1522) was the greatest Christian Hebraist of the age. His defence of Hebrew literature against the Dominican Johann Hochstraten’s proposal to destroy all Jewish books except the Bible became a cause celebre for humanist scholars. Erasmus and other humanists supported Reuchlin, and the controversy highlighted the importance of Hebrew printing for Christian scholarship. Reuchlin’s Hebrew grammar and dictionary were among the earliest printed works to teach Hebrew to Christian readers. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Francois Tissard published a Hebrew grammar and alphabet in Paris in 1508, printed by Gilles Gourmont, who thereby earned the title “first printer of Hebrew in Paris.” The works were the Grammatica Hebraica et Graeca (quarto) and the Alphabetum Hebraicum et Graecum. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Robert Estienne, the great Parisian scholar-printer, was appointed “Imprimeur Royal for works in Hebrew and Latin” by King Francis I in 1539. In 1545 he completed a quarto edition of the Hebrew Scriptures in twenty-four parts, which Maittaire described as “a magnificent work.” Estienne’s Hebrew types were among the finest in Europe, and he used them for scholarly editions of the Bible that presented Hebrew, Greek, and Latin texts in parallel columns. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Complutensian Polyglot (1514-1517), printed at Alcala under the patronage of Cardinal Ximenes, was the first polyglot Bible, presenting the Old Testament in Hebrew, Greek, and Latin. It required the cutting of entirely new Hebrew, Greek, and Latin typefaces and represented the most ambitious scholarly printing project before Plantin’s Polyglot of 1568-1572. A sample page in Aldus’s workshop (a three-column page in Hebrew, Greek, and Latin) may have inspired the project. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Oriental Presses in Rome

Beyond Hebrew, the Catholic Church also sponsored printing in other oriental languages. The Congregation de Propaganda Fide (established 1622) combined a missionary seminary with a printing press equipped with oriental types cut by the punchcutter Stefano Paoli. The press printed New Testaments and devotional books “in many languages” for missionary use, though Lorck notes it “never developed an effectiveness commensurate with its considerable resources.” ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Earlier oriental printing included:

  • The first printed Arabic book in Europe: Septem Horae Canonicae (Fano, 1514), printed by Gregorio Gregorio on the press of Pope Julius II
  • The Genoa Polyglot Psalter (1516), by the Dominican Agostino Giustiniani, containing Hebrew, Greek, Arabic, and Chaldaic (Aramaic) texts with Latin translations — the first polyglot psalter, though Guistiniani’s planned complete polyglot Bible failed for lack of support
  • The Paris Polyglot (1645, ten volumes, adding Arabic and Samaritan) and the London Polyglot (1654-1657, six volumes, adding Ethiopian and Persian) — each requiring new typefaces

John Man notes that these polyglot projects “needed their own typefaces, each one adding a new dimension to oriental studies. And the whole mighty project was held together by its appendices and index.” ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Type Technology

Hebrew type presented unique technical challenges. The script requires:

  • Vowel points (nikud) — small marks placed above, below, or inside consonants to indicate vowels
  • Cantillation marks — signs indicating how the biblical text should be chanted
  • Final letter forms — five Hebrew letters have different shapes when they appear at the end of a word
  • Ligatures and flourishes — elaborate calligraphic connections between letters

Early Hebrew typefounders approached these challenges in two ways: either cutting separate type pieces for every possible consonant-vowel combination (a vast character set), or using a system of combined type where vowel marks could be positioned above or below consonants through careful spacing and kerning. The Soncino family and later Bomberg both invested heavily in solving these problems, and Bomberg’s types especially were regarded as the high-water mark of Hebrew type design. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]
