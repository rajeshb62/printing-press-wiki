Major Publishing Houses Compared

The printing press produced a series of distinct publishing models across the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Each major house answered the same fundamental challenge — how to finance, produce, and distribute printed books at a profit — but their solutions differed dramatically depending on their city, capital, connections, and ambitions.

This page compares the six most significant publishing operations of the period by the dimensions that defined their business models.

Key Comparative Dimensions

Capital and Financing

Koberger was self-financed through his prior career as a goldsmith and jeweller. His operation was the largest of the incunable period because he could reinvest profits from one edition into the next, and he supplemented his own capacity by contracting work to external printers (Amerbach, Gruninger). When his capital was impaired after 1513 by withdrawals to heirs, the firm collapsed. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Aldus operated on partnership capital: Barbarigo (nephew of the Doge) and Torresani (experienced printer) each held shares, with Aldus himself controlling perhaps one-tenth. Monthly expenses ran 200-234 ducats, and he “died a poor man, reinvesting all receipts into fresh scholarly ventures.” He needed a partner with capital because he had none himself. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Plantin used royal patronage (Philip II’s promised 21,000 florins for the Polyglot Bible) and international credit. The Polyglot nearly bankrupted him when the subsidy went unpaid, but his diversified output — liturgical books, scientific works, vernacular literature — kept the firm afloat. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Elzevir built a low-capital model: they did not even print their own books at first, contracting the actual presswork while focusing on distribution. Their small-format duodecimos cost far less to produce than folios, and their family-based agency system required minimal cash outlay. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Distribution Model

Koberger built the first pan-European agent network: agents or representatives in Frankfurt, Leipzig, Vienna, Basel, Strasbourg, Cologne, Paris, Budapest, Warsaw, Venice, Florence, Rome, Antwerp, Bruges, and Leyden. He was “the first bookseller in Germany, and possibly in Europe, to issue a classified catalogue of current publications.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Aldus depended on “direct correspondence with scholars” because Italy had no organised book-trade before 1550. He had stock worth 500 ducats in Pavia alone and agents in major Italian cities, but could not establish satisfactory distribution north of the Alps. The Frankfurt Fair was useless to him when wars blocked the Alpine passes. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Plantin exploited the Frankfurt Fair heavily: consignments of 1,200 to 5,212 volumes per fair. He maintained a permanent agent in Paris (Sonnius, at 40% commission), an agent in Salamanca for Spain (annual sales 5,000-15,000 florins), and planned a London branch that war prevented. He also used the fair as a buying market to acquire stock for his Antwerp shop. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Elzevir created a “federation of book-selling agencies” run by family members. Within fifteen years of the founder’s death, the firm had representation in nearly every European book-selling centre. Branches existed in The Hague, Utrecht, Amsterdam, Copenhagen, and a permanent depot in Frankfurt. Family members received their business training through foreign postings, creating a loyal and self-perpetuating distribution network. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Market Position

Koberger aimed at institutions: libraries, monasteries, universities, and wealthy collectors. His Nuremberg Chronicle (1,800 woodcuts, 600 pages) epitomised the high-cost, high-prestige model. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Aldus created a new market: the travelling scholar and the gentleman who wanted a personal library. His octavo format was a deliberate market intervention — portable, affordable, and authoritative. But his Greek editions remained expensive: 11 ducats for a complete Aristotle set. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Plantin served every market simultaneously: the Polyglot Bible for royalty, liturgical books for the Church, scientific works for scholars, and vernacular literature for the growing middle class. He was “the first medieval publisher to deliberately plan books for what would now be called a popular circulation.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Elzevir perfected the cheap-book market. A Virgil of 1636 or a Pliny of 1640 sold for one florin (43 cents). Their duodecimo classics — small, portable, well-edited — reached students and professionals who could not afford folios. The scholar Puteanus complained that great authors deserved “dignified” formats, but the market disagreed. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Blaeu occupied the premium niche: the Atlas Maior was one of the most expensive printed works of the seventeenth century, aimed at princes, merchants, and institutions. The firm’s seaman’s guides and navigation manuals provided steady lower-margin income to balance the atlas business. ^[raw/articles/blaeu-review-nellen.md]

Longevity and Succession

Only two houses survived their founder. Plantin lasted 312 years under the Moretus family, ending only when the city of Antwerp bought the premises as a museum. Elzevir lasted 120 years across three generations, declining only under the incapable Abraham. Koberger collapsed within 27 years of the founder’s death because capital was withdrawn by heirs who returned to the family goldsmith trade. Aldus died with his founder, though the press continued under the Torresani family. Estienne fled Paris for Geneva and continued across three generations, declining as the family’s scholarly energy dissipated. Blaeu ended abruptly in the 1672 fire, though the business staggered on for a few years. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/blaeu-review-nellen.md]

The Succession of Publishing Centres

The centre of gravity in European publishing moved westward over three centuries:

  1. Mainz (1450-1470s): The birthplace — Gutenberg, Fust, Schoeffer. Technology secret, output small but foundational.
  2. Venice (1470-1550): The first capital — 4,000+ editions by 1500. Aldus, Jenson, Giunti, Bomberg. Combination of paper supply, maritime trade, and humanist scholarship.
  3. Basel & Lyon (1500-1580): Froben in Basel, multiple printers in Lyon. The Reformation-driven market for pamphlets and scholarly editions.
  4. Antwerp (1550-1600): Plantin. Largest 16th-century output, the Polyglot Bible, international distribution.
  5. Amsterdam & Leyden (1600-1700): Elzevir, Blaeu, Janssonius. Dutch press freedom, typographic excellence, atlas publishing, the newspaper.

The shift from south to north was driven by religious conflict (the Spanish Fury destroyed Antwerp’s book trade), the relative press freedom of the Dutch Republic, and the decline of Italian economic dominance. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Patterns and Lessons

Across these six houses, several patterns recur:

  • The founder’s personality determined the firm’s character. Koberger the industrialist, Aldus the scholar, Plantin the international businessman, Estienne the fugitive scholar, Elzevir the network-builder, Blaeu the atlas-maker. The firm was an extension of the man.

  • Distribution was the hardest problem. Every house struggled to get books to distant buyers. Koberger solved it with agents, Plantin with fairs, Elzevir with family, Aldus never solved it properly.

  • Capital was always the constraint. Even Koberger’s immense operation collapsed when heirs withdrew capital. Plantin was nearly bankrupted by a royal patron who did not pay. Aldus died poor.

  • The pure publisher emerged slowly. The Giunti model — financing without printing — was the exception in the 16th century. Most publishers were still printers. The separation of roles became normal only in the 17th century with the Elzevirs, who outsourced printing while focusing on distribution.

  • Press freedom was a competitive advantage. The Dutch Republic’s relative tolerance made Leyden and Amsterdam the publishing capitals of the 17th century, as scholars and authors fled censorship in Catholic territories.
