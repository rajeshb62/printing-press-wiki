Maritime Publications

From the 1530s onward, the printing press produced a distinct and commercially significant category of works specifically for mariners and navigators: pilot books, navigation manuals, island books, and sea atlases. These maritime publications were practical tools — often written in the vernacular, illustrated with coastal profiles, and priced for working seamen — and formed a genre quite different from the scholarly geographies and luxury atlases produced for the same presses. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md] ^[raw/articles/blaeu-review-nellen.md]

Pilot Books and Rutters: The Dutch Leeskaarten

The earliest printed maritime publications in Northern Europe were the Dutch leeskaarten (literally “reading-maps”) or pilot books — practical guides for coastal navigation that gave compass bearings, depths, and descriptions of harbours, accompanied by coastal profiles showing what the land looked like from the sea.

The first known printed Dutch pilot book was De Kaert vader zee, published in 1532 by Jan Severszoon. An enlarged and revised edition by Jan Jacobszoon followed in 1541. Neither survives in any known copy. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Cornelis Anthoniszoon (c. 1507-1553), a Dutch painter and cartographer, produced the next landmark. In 1543 he published the Caerte van Oostlant, a large wall map of the eastern navigation (the Baltic and North Sea). In the note to readers on this map, he promised “a little book” that would have “pictures of the land as seen from the sea and other things useful and pleasurable to mariners.” That book was his Onderwijsinge vander zee (Instruction concerning the Sea), a treatise on navigation — the first in the Dutch language — probably first published in 1544. The book consisted of two parts: the Onderwijsinge proper (a theoretical navigation treatise) and the Caerte van die Oosterse See (Chart of the Eastern Sea), which included dozens of woodcut coastal profiles by Anthoniszoon himself. Unlike the earlier pilot books, which treated both the western (Atlantic) and eastern (North Sea) navigations, Anthoniszoon covered only the eastern navigation, as a companion to his map. The earliest surviving edition is the third (1558), printed in Amsterdam by Jan Ewoutzoon. A second edition (c. 1551) and the first (c. 1544) are lost. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

A later anonymous pilot book, the Leeskaartboek van Wisbuy (1566), and its English translation The Safegard of Sailers (1584), borrowed heavily from Anthoniszoon but included additions by another hand. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

The Scottish Rutter

The Scottish pilot Alexander Lindesay compiled a manuscript rutter (a sailing guide) describing the coasts and harbours of Scotland, including the Hebrides and Orkney Islands. This manuscript fell into the hands of the French cosmographer Nicolas de Nicolay (1517-1583), who took it back to France in 1547 after a spying mission to England and Scotland. The rutter was put to immediate practical use: in June 1547 a French fleet under Admiral Leone Strozzi, with Nicolay as pilot, used it to besiege St. Andrews. Lindesay’s original manuscript is lost, but Nicolay had it translated from Scots into French by Johannes Ferrerius, added his own section on navigation derived from his translation of Pedro de Medina (see below), and eventually published it in 1583 as La navigation du roy d’Escosse Jacques cinquiesme (Paris: Gilles Beys), with a fine copper-engraved chart. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Navigation Manuals: The Spanish and Mediterranean Tradition

The most influential printed navigation manual of the sixteenth century was the Arte de Navegar (Art of Navigation) by Pedro de Medina (1493-1567), a Spanish mathematician, cosmographer, and instrument maker. Medina arrived in Seville and discovered that many of the instruments and charts produced by the Gutiérrez family (who had a near-monopoly on navigational tools under the Casa de Contratación) were faulty. He complained to the Council of the Indies and was granted a licence in 1538. The Arte de Navegar was published in Valladolid in 1545 (with a second, expanded edition in 1546), and Sebastian Cabot, the aged pilot major of Spain, publicly impugned its accuracy in a dispute that required the adjudication of a special court of the Casa. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

The work was an international success. It was translated into French by Nicolas de Nicolay as L’Art de naviguer (Lyons: Guillaume Rouille, 1554, with a title page reading 1553), including a copper-engraved map centred on the Atlantic. The French edition was republished repeatedly: Lyons (1561, 1569, 1576), Rouen (1573, 1577, 1602, 1607, 1628), and La Rochelle (1618). The Rouen editions used woodcut maps; the Lyons editions used copperplate. An Italian translation also appeared. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

In addition to his translation of Medina, Nicolay himself produced a manuscript on comparative naval organizations (1582), titled Extraict des observations… touchant la diversité des navires, galleres, et autres vaisseaux de mer, describing the different types of ships used in the Ocean, Baltic, and Mediterranean seas. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Isolarii: Printed Island Books

A distinctly Italian (and especially Venetian) genre, the isolario or island book was a printed collection of maps and descriptions of the world’s islands — part geographical reference, part travel narrative, part illustrated book. The genre existed in manuscript before printing, but print turned it into a commercial success. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

The most famous printed isolario was that of Benedetto Bordone (c. 1450-1539), a Paduan miniaturist, woodcut artist, and cartographer active in Venice. Bordone had applied to the Venetian Senate for permission to publish maps as early as 1508, but his great work, the Libro di Benedetto Bordone nel qual si ragiona de tutte l’isole del mondo (Book of All the Islands of the World), appeared only in 1528, printed in Venice by Nicolo d’Aristotile, detto Zoppino. He had been granted permission to print in 1526, and there is a preparatory manuscript in Florence dating from before 1524. The work consisted of 108 woodcut maps of islands and views of port cities, each with a double-line border and lines indicating the eight winds radiating from a central point. It began with three large maps (Western Europe and the Mediterranean, Southeastern Europe, and an oval world map — the earliest known printed oval world map, though Bordone probably borrowed the projection from Francesco Rosselli). ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

The Libro was a great success. A second edition, retitled Isolario di Benedetto Bordone, appeared in 1534 (also from Zoppino), adding only the text of Pizarro’s letter describing his conquest of Peru. A third edition followed from Francesco di Leno (c. 1537), and a fourth from Frederico Toresano (1547). All editions used the same woodblocks. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Bordone’s Isolario “firmly established the genre as an Italian and particularly Venetian specialty.” Tommaso Porcacchi’s L’Isole piu famose del Mondo followed in 1572, and as late as 1697 Vincenzo Maria Coronelli published an Isolario in the form of a world atlas. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Bordone was also a miniaturist who may have done the woodcut illustrations for the Aldine Hypnerotomachia Poliphili (1499), and his interest in the press is documented by applications for privileges on translations of Lucian (1494), illustrations of the “Triumph of Caesar” (1504), and a world map (1508), though none of these survive. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Sea Atlases and Mariner’s Guides

The late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries saw the rise of the printed sea atlas — a collection of navigational charts designed for use at sea, distinct from the geographical atlases of Ortelius and Mercator.

Lucas Jansz Waghenaer (c. 1533-1606), a Dutch navigator and cartographer from Enkhuizen, published the Spieghel der Zeevaerdt (Mariner’s Mirror) in 1584-1585, the first printed sea atlas. It contained navigational charts, coastal profiles, and sailing directions for European waters, and was quickly translated into English, Latin, French, and German. The term “waggoner” entered the English language as a generic name for a book of sea charts. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

In Amsterdam, Willem Jansz Blaeu (1571-1638) built a substantial business around maritime publications alongside his better-known atlases. The Blaeu review documents that he “onderscheidde hij zich ook met de uitgave van veel boeken op het gebied van de wiskunde, navigatie en astronomie” (distinguished himself also with the publication of many books on mathematics, navigation, and astronomy), and that a chapter of Van Netten’s study is devoted to “zijn zeemansgidsen” (his seaman’s guides) and his collaboration with the mathematician Adriaan Metius. Blaeu disguised his Copernican convictions in these mariners’ guides, presenting a conventional Ptolemaic world picture to avoid frightening practical sailors. ^[raw/articles/blaeu-review-nellen.md]

His competitors Willem Janssonius and Jacob Aertsz Colom also produced pilot books and sea charts. All three firms were located on the Damrak in Amsterdam, in direct proximity to one another, and their competition extended to the maritime publishing market: Janssonius and Colom regularly reprinted Blaeu’s successful navigation titles in cheaper formats. ^[raw/articles/blaeu-review-nellen.md]

The Business of Maritime Publishing

Maritime publications shared several economic characteristics that distinguished them from other printed products:

Vernacular audience. Unlike most scholarly publishing, which remained in Latin, pilot books and navigation manuals were written in the vernacular (Dutch, Spanish, French, English, Italian) because their users were working mariners and ship captains, not university-educated scholars. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Illustration costs. The signature feature of these works — coastal profiles showing the appearance of the land from the sea — required woodcut or copperplate illustration. Anthoniszoon’s Caerte van die Oosterse See was built around dozens of such profiles. The cost of cutting these blocks or plates was substantial but could be amortized across multiple editions. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Low survival rates. Pilot books and rutters were carried to sea, used in salt spray, and literally read to pieces — a fate they share with cheap school texts and ephemera. Of Anthoniszoon’s Onderwijsinge vander zee, the first and second editions are entirely lost; the only surviving copy is of the third (1558) edition, preserved in a library. The same fate befell the early Dutch pilot books of Severszoon and Jacobszoon. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

International market. Successful navigation manuals crossed linguistic borders rapidly. Medina’s Arte de Navegar was translated into French (1554) and Italian within a decade of its Spanish publication, with editions in five French cities across the next seventy years. Waghenaer’s Spieghel der Zeevaerdt appeared in four languages within a decade of its Dutch publication. The Leeskaartboek van Wisbuy was translated as The Safegard of Sailers in English. This multilingual diffusion was driven by the practical needs of international shipping. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

State involvement. Navigation manuals were entangled with state-sponsored monopolies and colonial administration. In Spain, the Casa de Contratación in Seville controlled the production and licensing of charts and navigation texts. Medina’s Arte de Navegar emerged from a dispute with the officially sanctioned Gutiérrez family of instrument makers and chart producers. The Venetian Senate granted privileges for map and island-book publication. In the Dutch Republic, the States-General granted seven-year privileges to competing publishers. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Relation to Other Printed Genres

Maritime publications fit into the spectrum-of-printed-output as a middle-tier product — more expensive than ephemera (because of illustration and navigational expertise) but considerably cheaper than the prestige atlases of Ortelius or Mercator. They belong alongside school texts, practical manuals, and professional reference works as a category of applied printing: books made not for contemplation or display, but for use.

They also connect directly to the maritime trade routes that distributed printed books across Europe. Venice, the leading fifteenth-century printing capital, was also a maritime republic whose commercial fleet carried books along with other goods. The Blaeu firm’s location on the Damrak in Amsterdam — the city’s main harbourfront — was no coincidence: sea captains and ship owners were customers as well as subjects. ^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md] ^[raw/articles/blaeu-review-nellen.md]
