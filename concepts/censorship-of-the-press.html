Censorship of the Press

From the earliest days of the printing press, both Church and state authorities recognized that the new technology posed a fundamental threat to established structures of control. The censorship apparatus that developed in the decades after Gutenberg was designed for a world of manuscripts, in which heretical ideas could be tracked, confiscated, and destroyed one copy at a time. The printing press rendered that model obsolete at a stroke.

The First Censorship: Berthold of Mainz (1486)

The earliest known edict specifically targeting the printing press was issued on 4 January 1486 by Berthold von Henneberg, Archbishop of mainz. The mandate forbade all persons from printing, publishing, buying, or selling any book translated from Greek, Latin, or any other language before the written translation had been approved by a committee appointed from the faculty of the University of Mainz. The penalties were severe: excommunication, confiscation of the books, and a fine of 100 florins of gold. [source: De Vinne, pp. 513-521]

Berthold’s preamble acknowledged that printing had been given “for the instruction of human life,” but complained that certain men “led by the desire of vainglory or money, do abuse this art.” He specifically condemned vernacular translations of the divine offices, which he claimed had been rendered “unintelligible” by “rash unlearned simpletons.” The edict was the first attempt to impose pre-publication approval — a template that would be replicated across Europe. [source: De Vinne, pp. 521]

University Control: Cologne (1479) and the Sorbonne

Before Berthold’s edict, universities had already begun exerting control. The University of Cologne, one of the earliest centers of printing outside mainz, asserted authority over printers within its jurisdiction as early as 1479, requiring that all books be inspected before distribution. The Sorbonne in Paris exercised similar authority — the first printing office in Paris had been established in 1469 within the Sorbonne itself, at the invitation of the learned Fichet and Heynlin, who saw the press as a tool for scholarship but also kept it under the University’s watchful eye. [source: Putnam, pp. 235-236]

As the Paris book trade grew, the Sorbonne’s Theological Faculty became the most aggressive censorship body in France. By 1512, the King was writing to the University requesting the Faculty to examine books the Court found suspicious. Under Francis I, the Sorbonne’s “bigoted and ignorant censorship” (as Putnam describes it) drove some of the most talented publishers out of the country — most notably Robert Estienne, who fled to Geneva in 1552 after years of harassment over his Greek and Latin Bibles. [source: Putnam, pp. 239, 249, 396-434]

The University of Paris and the Book Trade

Andre Chevillier’s L’Origine de l’Imprimerie de Paris (1694), Part IV (“Des Droits de l’Universite sur la Librairie”), provides the most detailed archive-based account of the University of Paris’s authority over the book trade — a system that long predated the printing press and shaped French censorship well into the age of print. Chevillier, a librarian of the College of Navarre, drew directly on the University’s registers, the Livre du Recteur, and the Recueil d’Actes preserved in the College of Navarre archives. [source: Chevillier, Preface and Part IV, pp. 301-417]

The University’s Jurisdiction

The Paris book trade — booksellers (librarii), writers (stationarii), binders (relieurs), illuminators (enlumineurs), and parchment-makers (parcheminiers) — was entirely under the jurisdiction of the University. Chevillier states this as a fact “so clear from a great number of ancient titles in its Archives, that one would have to be blind not to see it.” In 1615-1616, under the Rectorate of Jean Aubert, the University’s titles were organized into an inventory divided into four parts by the letters A through D, with the fourth part (letter D) containing the records on the book trade. [source: Chevillier, Part IV, pp. 301-302; lines 22767-22804 of the raw text]

The 21 Regulations

Chevillier enumerates 21 regulations that the University imposed on booksellers, drawn from statutes of 1275, 1313, 1341, and 1403. Among them:

  • Booksellers must obtain permission from the University to practice (Rule 10)
  • They must post bond (caution) of 100 Parisian livres (Rule 6)
  • They must have attestation of life, morals, and sufficient capacity (Rule 7)
  • They are subject to correction by the University, which could fine them or even depose them for cause (Rule 8)
  • They must attend University assemblies and processions when summoned (Rule 9)
  • They cannot set their own prices — this was the University’s prerogative, exercised through four elected booksellers (Rule 11)
  • They cannot sell above the taxe or buy below the assessed price (Rule 12)
  • No book could be offered for sale without prior communication to the University for approval and, if necessary, correction (Rule 13)
  • They must rent their books to those who desired them, at the taxed price (Rule 14)
  • They must lend exemplaria to those who wished to copy them, at the taxed price (Rule 15)
  • Their profit was limited to 4 deniers per livre for masters and scholars, and 6 deniers for other customers (Rule 18)
  • They could take no “pot-de-vin” or engage in simulated contracts in the sale or purchase of books (Rule 19)
  • Each bookseller must post in his shop the catalogue of his books with the taxed price (Rule 21)
  • Those who had not taken the oath could sell books only to a maximum value of 10 sous (Rule 22)

[source: Chevillier, Part IV, Chap. I, pp. 303-304; lines 22891-22950]

The Taxe (Price-Fixing) System

The University’s authority to set book prices — the taxe — was a defining feature of the Paris book trade. The University either set prices directly through deputies chosen from its faculties, or (more commonly) through four sworn booksellers elected annually. A well-authenticated proof of this practice exists in the Livre du Recteur (Rector’s Book), where many folios are filled with the taxe imposed on a great number of books by both types of deputies. The list contains nearly 300 works, each with its taxed price. Examples include Gregory’s Super Job priced at 100 sous 8 folios, the Liber Homiliarum of St. Gregory at 18 deniers, and Hugh of St. Victor’s De Sacramentis at 140 sous 5 folios. [source: Chevillier, Part IV, Chap. I, pp. 315-316; lines 23700-23720, 24070-24075]

The Statute of 1341, issued by the University on 6 October at St. Mathurin, formalized the system: four principal booksellers were elected annually as taxatores librorum. Named in the statute were Jean de Fonte, Jean Vachet, and Alain Brito. Only these four — or at least two of them acting together — could set prices. Their authority extended to investigating whether any unsworn person was practicing as a bookseller. [source: Chevillier, Part IV, Chap. I, pp. 306-307; lines 23015-23160, 23144-23155]

The Statute of 1313 contained similar provisions: no stationer could rent an exemplar before it had been corrected and taxed by the University; no stationer could charge more than the taxed price; no stationer could deny an exemplar to anyone wishing to copy it. [source: Chevillier, Part IV, Chap. I, pp. 308-309; lines 23188-23192]

The Sworn Booksellers (Libraires Jurez)

The University alone had the right to create and institute the booksellers of Paris, a privilege confirmed by the French Crown. Charles VI’s letters of 20 June 1411 explicitly reaffirmed this right: “it belongs and appertains to our said Daughter [the University], and to none other, to establish and institute all the booksellers selling and buying books, whether in French or in Latin, in our said City of Paris.” The letters required that booksellers swear an oath, be examined and approved, and could be punished if they abused their office. [source: Chevillier, Part IV, Chap. I, pp. 309-310; lines 23217-23295]

From these sworn booksellers, four were chosen as the quatuor majores librarii or Grands Jurez. They governed the book trade under the University’s supervision for at least 300 years. The Parlement arrêt of 1 July 1541 refers to them: “Booksellers, or other Merchants who wish to expose for sale any Books that have come to them newly, before opening their bales, shall be required to call the four sworn Booksellers to be present at said opening.” Alongside them were twenty lesser booksellers (parvi librarii), but all were considered Suppôts et Officiers de l’Université. [source: Chevillier, Part IV, Chap. III, pp. 343-344; lines 25815-25836]

Many of the most famous Parisian printers and booksellers styled themselves with this University affiliation: “Libraire de l’Universite de Paris,” “Libraire Jure en l’Universite de Paris,” “Librarius Universitatis Parisiensis.” Among them were Guillaume Le Caron, Jean Belin, Jean Dupre, Simon Vostre, Jean Petit, Josse Bade, Henri Pacquot, Thielman Kerver, and many others. [source: Chevillier, Part IV, Chap. II, pp. 327-331; lines 24262-24295]

The Four Censors

The most direct precursor to the French royal censorship apparatus was the creation of four official censors (censeurs et examinateurs) under Louis XIV. Chevillier reproduces the Letters Patent in full: “Louis, by the grace of God, King of France and Navarre… we have created and erected, and by these Presents create and erect, four Censors and Examiners, who shall be taken from the Body and Faculty of Theology of our University of Paris, to henceforth see, read, and examine all manner of new Books concerning Theology, devotion, and good morals, which shall henceforth be printed in this Kingdom.” [source: Chevillier, Part IV, Chap. VI, pp. 399-405; lines 29836-29970]

The edict, drawing on earlier regulations of Charles IX (1563), required that:

  • Approved books bear the attestation and approbation of the censors
  • Printing privileges be expedited only through the Great Chancellery
  • Two copies of each book be deposited: one to remain with the examiners, signed and paraphased by the author on every leaf
  • The two senior examiners distribute new books among themselves
  • The approbation be signed by the examiner and one of the two seniors

The censors received 2,000 livres annually in wages: 600 livres each for the two seniors, 400 livres each for the two juniors, payable quarterly from the King’s general receipts of Paris. The edict stipulated that the fourth position, on each vacancy, would be filled by election among the Doctors of the Society of Sorbonne, with two Doctors from the College of Navarre called to participate — a mechanism that kept the censors firmly within the University’s control. [source: Chevillier, Part IV, Chap. VI, pp. 402-405; lines 29925-30042]

The Approval System (Approbation)

The approval of theological books was a two-stage process. First, doctors examined the book and signed their approbation, which was placed at the beginning or end of the volume. These doctors “remained guarantors to the Public and to the Faculty of the doctrine they had approved.” Only then would the King or the Parlement grant a privilege for printing. Chevillier provides examples: the approbation of George Venetus’s Harmonia Mundi (1578) signed by G. Genebrard and F. Huart; the Cathechism of Peter Canisius (1579) approved by Doctors Dadre and Perier; and the third edition of the Bibliotheque des Peres (1609) approved by four doctors: A. Duval, M. Aubert, C. Henriot, and Girard. [source: Chevillier, Part IV, Chap. VI, pp. 391-393; lines 29385-29413]

After the Faculty gave its reprobation (rejection) or approbation, the King or Parlement granted the privilege for printing. The Parlement arrêt of 12 January 1508 granted Berthold de Rembolt a privilege for St. Bruno on the Epistles of St. Paul “after he had produced the Approbation of the Doctors.” Similarly, the arrêt of 4 July 1511 granted Poncet le Preux permission to print Dionysius Carthusianus on the Sentences based on the attestation of Jean Maziere, a Religious and Regent Doctor in Theology. [source: Chevillier, Part IV, Chap. VI, pp. 394-395; lines 29644-29680]

Chevillier is careful to note that this approval system long predated Luther. He cites the Statute of 1313, which forbade stationers from renting any exemplar “before it has been corrected and taxed by the University,” and the Statute of 1341, which required that “if it happen that they have new exemplaria, they shall not communicate them, neither for themselves nor for others, until they have been approved, corrected, and taxed by the University.” He concludes: “Those who shall have read these Articles of the Statutes will be sufficiently persuaded that the University enjoyed this right long before Luther was born.” [source: Chevillier, Part IV, Chap. VI, pp. 393-394; lines 29582-29641]

Import Control

The University’s authority extended to books imported from abroad. The Regulation of 11 December 1403 required that if a sworn bookseller “has brought foreign books for sale to Paris, he shall immediately, or as soon as he can, declare them to the Rector, so that for the common utility of the Masters and Scholars, order may be given concerning them according to the regulation of the University.” [source: Chevillier, Part IV, Chap. VI, p. 394; lines 29610-29620]

The Parlement arrêt of 1 July 1541 elaborated the inspection procedure for imported books: “Booksellers, or other Merchants who wish to expose for sale any Books that have come to them newly, before opening their bales, shall be required to call the four sworn Booksellers to be present at said opening, and see the Books which are there.” If the books were suspect, the four sworn booksellers were to alert the Rector and Deans of the three higher Faculties, who would then commission: two Masters of Arts for grammar, logic, rhetoric, and philosophy books; two Doctors of Theology for books on religion; two Doctors of Canon Law for legal books; and two Doctors of Medicine for medical texts. These deputies were to “put some mark or paraph at the end of one of the said Books, which they will retain by them” as proof of approval. [source: Chevillier, Part IV, Chap. IV, pp. 358-359; lines 26900-26976]

University-Crown Relations

The relationship between the University and the French Crown on matters of the book trade was one of mutual reinforcement. The Crown repeatedly confirmed the University’s authority, and the University served as the Crown’s instrument of intellectual control.

Charles VI’s letters of 20 June 1411 threatened with royal authority those who would trouble the University in its possession of the right to create booksellers. Louis XII, in his Declaration of 9 April 1513, recognized the twenty-four sworn booksellers as Suppôts et Officiers of the University, exempt from certain taxes. Francis I confirmed this in his letters of 10 October 1516. [source: Chevillier, Part IV, Chap. III, pp. 337-338; lines 25319-25367]

After the printing press arrived in Paris in 1469-1470, the University continued to exercise its jurisdiction over the book trade. Louis XII ordered the University to assist at the funeral convoy of Charles VIII in 1498. The University prosecuted the sale of heretical books — in 1517, the Syndic Procureur complained in Assembly about a seditious book against the Catholic religion being distributed, and the matter was sent to the Faculty of Theology for examination. [source: Chevillier, Part IV, Chap. IV, pp. 351-353; lines 26448-26604]

The high point of Crown-University collaboration came under Louis XIV. The Edict of September 1651 (registered in Parlement 19 December 1651) reasserted “the first Ordinances, and particularly those of Henry II,” specifically the Edict of Fontainebleau (11 December 1547) and the Edict of Chateau-Briant (17 June 1551). It made “very express inhibitions and prohibitions to all Printers and Booksellers to print, sell, or publish any Book concerning Religion or morals, nor even to expose for sale, publish, or sell those which have been brought from foreign countries, until they have first been seen, visited, and examined by the Faculty of Theology.” [source: Chevillier, Part IV, Chap. VII, p. 407; lines 30466-30484]

The Parlement arrêt of 13 February 1663, which suppressed the book of Amadeus Guimenius, further specified that:

  1. No book concerning theology, the Church, or the State could be printed without approval by Doctors
  2. At least one of these Doctors must be from the body of the Faculty of Theology of Paris
  3. The approved doctor must be a secular (not regular) Doctor
  4. The approbation must identify where the approving doctor resides, so that he could be held accountable

[source: Chevillier, Part IV, Chap. VII, pp. 407-408; lines 30488-30530]

Chevillier’s account makes clear that the University of Paris censorship system was not a Reformation-era innovation but a continuation of manuscript-era controls that had been in place since at least the 13th century. The printing press did not create the censorship apparatus; it forced it to become more systematic. The University’s rights over the book trade, grounded in its archives and confirmed by successive monarchs from Charles VI to Louis XIV, provided the institutional framework within which French press control operated for over four centuries.

The Venetian Card-Makers’ Petition (1472)

The first known protest against the printing press from a threatened craft guild came from Venice’s card-makers (carte da gioco). Venice had a flourishing industry in hand-painted playing cards and devotional woodcuts. When printers began producing such items by the hundreds with woodblocks and movable type, the card-makers petitioned the Signoria for protection. The Venetian Senate refused to grant a monopoly or suppress the new technology, but the petition marks the earliest recorded instance of economic — as opposed to theological — pressure against printers. Within a few decades, Venice would have over 150 presses at work and would become the publishing capital of Europe. [source: general historical consensus, confirmed by the structure of Venetian privilege system in Putnam, pp. 2471-2477]

The Papal Bull of 1501 and the Roman Index

In 1501, Pope Alexander VI issued a papal bull (known as Inter multiplices) against the unauthorized printing of books. The bull required that all printed works be examined by ecclesiastical authorities before publication, under pain of excommunication. It was the first time the papacy had addressed itself specifically to the regulation of the new art. [source: Putnam’s discussion of Church censorship framework, pp. 111, 289-291]

The Church’s campaign against heretical print reached its fullest expression in the Index of Prohibited Books. The first Roman Index was published in 1559 by Pope Paul IV, under the title Index auctorum et librorum qui ab officio sanctae Romanae et universalis Inquisitionis caveri ab omnibus et singulis in universa Christiana Republica Mandantur. It divided prohibited works into three classes: (1) authors whose entire output was banned; (2) authors whose specific works were banned; and (3) anonymous prohibited books. Notably, it also listed 61 printers whose entire output was prohibited. [source: Putnam, pp. 2665]

The Pauline (Tridentine) Index of 1564, issued after the Council of Trent, refined the system. It introduced ten rules for the guidance of censors and the famous formula donec corrigatur — a work could be conditionally permitted if condemned passages were expunged. It provided that all booksellers must maintain a signed list of their stock for inspection by the Inquisitor. The Tridentine Index was printed in Venice by the Aldine Press and was reprinted at least ten times between 1564 and 1593. [source: Putnam, pp. 2667-2669]

State Censorship: England and France

Secular authorities were no less enthusiastic than the Church about controlling the press. In England, Henry VIII took personal control of the licensing regime. The first printing privilege in England was granted in 1508 to Richard Pynson. In 1538, Henry issued a proclamation requiring all books to be licensed by the Privy Council or its appointees before publication. This system culminated in 1556, when Queen Mary chartered the Stationers’ Company, giving it a monopoly over all printing in England: only members of the Company could lawfully operate a press. The Stationers’ registers became the de facto copyright registry of the realm. [source: Putnam, pp. 3219-3227]

In France, Francis I’s attitude was ambivalent. Initially a patron of learning who founded the Imprimerie Royale and appointed Robert Estienne as Royal Printer, he increasingly bent to pressure from the Sorbonne and Parliament. His reign saw the execution of the printer-scholar Etienne Dolet, burned as a heretic in 1546. The Sorbonne compiled its own blacklists of condemned books, and in 1544 the University of Paris published a formal catalogue of censured works. [source: Putnam, pp. 239, 301, 422-423]

The Venetian Privilege System (1469-1517)

Venice developed the most sophisticated system of printing privileges in Europe, beginning in 1469 when John of Speyer was granted a five-year monopoly on printing in the city. The system served both as a form of copyright protection and as a mechanism of state control. The Council of Ten and the College of Venice granted privileges for specific works or classes of books, typically for terms of five to ten years. [source: Putnam, pp. 2475]

In 1505, the Venetian Senate formalized the privilege system, requiring applicants to submit their manuscripts for review. By 1544, a full censorship apparatus was in place: no book could be printed without approval from three authorities — the Inquisitor, a public reader in philosophy or theology, and a ducal secretary. By 1562, all licences had to be registered with the Esecutori contro la Bestemmia. [source: Putnam, pp. 2621-2629]

In 1548-49, Venice’s printers, publishers, and booksellers were organized into a guild — the first compulsory trade association of book-men in Europe, predating the Stationers’ Company by six years. The Guild was created not only to protect trade interests but “for the purpose of facilitating the work of the State and of the Church in keeping a close supervision upon all the productions of the Press.” [source: Putnam, pp. 2603-2609]

The Paradox of Print: The Reformation

The reformation exposed the fundamental paradox of censorship in the age of print: the apparatus designed to control the press was itself a manuscript-era technology, incapable of keeping pace with the printing press.

When Martin Luther posted (or circulated) his Ninety-Five Theses in October 1517, they were printed and distributed across Germany within weeks. Editions appeared in Leipzig, Basel, Nuremberg, and Wittenberg before Christmas. As John Man writes, “the dam broke with astonishing speed.” The 95 Theses were “the first mass medium that couldn’t be controlled.” [source: Man, pp. 1466-1468]

Putnam notes the same paradox: “The contention that the productions of the printers must be subjected to the approval of the authorities of the State was made promptly after the printing-press began its work. It was, however, only when the Press came to be utilised as the most effective ally of the heretical reformers that this censorship became a matter of serious and prolonged contention.” [source: Putnam, pp. 111]

Luther himself recognized the value of the new technology. He wrote in German rather than Latin specifically to reach “the masses of the people.” His pamphlets, sermons, and tracts flooded Germany at a volume that exceeded the enforcement capacity of any censor. The spread-of-printing made it impossible to intercept the flow: a work condemned in one city could be printed in the next, and smuggled back in bales of cloth or casks of wine. [source: Putnam, pp. 1448-1452]

The Church’s attempt to suppress Luther’s works through the Imperial Edict of Worms (1521) failed utterly. As Putnam notes, “the enormous impetus given to the education and active-mindedness of the people through the distribution of literature … can hardly be overestimated.” Printing technology had made censorship structurally impossible for the first time in human history. [source: Putnam, pp. 1452]

The Inquisition and the Book Trade

The Roman Inquisition prosecuted print-related offenses with increasing vigour after 1547. In Venice alone, there were 132 cases in the sixteenth century, 55 in the seventeenth, and only 4 in the eighteenth — not because censorship relaxed, but because it had succeeded in suppressing the industry. By 1596, the number of presses in Venice had fallen from 125 to 40 within months of the Clementine Index. [source: Putnam, pp. 2647, 2721]

The Congregation of the Index was instituted by Pope Pius V in 1571 to deal exclusively with questions of book prohibition and expurgation. Sixtus V enlarged the Tridentine Index in 1588 and expanded its ten rules to twenty-two. The Clementine Index of 1596, under Clement VIII, was the most comprehensive yet, but provoked fierce resistance from Venice, whose ambassador Paruta argued that it would destroy the most important book trade in Europe. [source: Putnam, pp. 2677-2693]

Case Study: Plantin in Antwerp

Christophe Plantin, the most important printer-publisher of the late sixteenth century, navigated the treacherous waters of Catholic censorship in Antwerp with exceptional skill. Operating under the rule of Philip II of Spain, Plantin maintained Catholic orthodoxy in his officially published works while employing Protestant scholars and secretly printing for both sides of the religious divide. His regulations forbade “the discussion of religious or political questions” among his 150 employees — a necessary precaution in a city torn between Spanish authority and Dutch rebellion. [source: Putnam, pp. 1698-1826]

In 1571, Plantin printed the Index Expurgatorius for Philip II, prepared by a commission of ecclesiastics chaired by Arias Montanus. This Index specified which passages of condemned books could be cut out, allowing booksellers to sell expurgated copies rather than destroy entire volumes. Plantin also printed the official list of prohibited books in 1586, comprising some 600 titles over 119 pages. [source: Putnam, pp. 1698-1700, 1818]

Legacy

The censorship of the press in the first century of printing reveals a profound structural tension. The printing press was the first mass medium, capable of producing identical copies in quantities that made wholesale suppression impossible. Yet authorities — Church and state alike — continued to apply an enforcement model designed for manuscripts: track the source, seize the copies, punish the producer. This model worked well enough to slow the spread of ideas in Italy and Spain, where the Index and Inquisition succeeded in strangling once-flourishing publishing industries. In Germany, England, and the Netherlands, however, the sheer volume and speed of print overwhelmed every attempt at control.

The incunabula period (to 1500) was a golden age of relative freedom, as the Church had not yet fully grasped the revolutionary potential of the-printing-press. By the mid-sixteenth century, that innocence was lost — but so was any hope of effective suppression. The Reformation proved definitively that print could not be censored, only delayed. The censorship apparatus of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries was the last gasp of a manuscript-based world order, fighting a war it could not win against a technology that had already changed the rules.
