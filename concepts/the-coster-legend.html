The Coster Legend

The Coster Legend (Dutch: Koster-legende) is the historical claim that Laurens Janszoon Coster (also spelled Koster) of Haarlem invented movable-type printing before johann-gutenberg of Mainz. For nearly three centuries — from the late 1500s well into the 1800s — the legend was accepted as patriotic history in the Netherlands and was debated across Europe. Modern scholarship has rejected Coster priority entirely, but the story remains one of the most instructive episodes in the historiography of printing.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, Chapter XVII, pp. 326-346]

The Junius Account (1588)

The first detailed, named account of Coster’s invention appears in Hadrianus Junius (Adrian de Jonghe), Batavia (1588). Though earlier hints exist — a 1561 preface by Jan van Zuren and Dirck Volckharts Coornhert, and a 1567 description by Luigi Guicciardini — Junius was the first to give the inventor a name, a date, a house, and a printed book.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, pp. 328-331]^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md, lines 1196-1206]

Junius writes that about 128 years before he wrote (i.e., c. 1440), there lived in Haarlem, near the market square, a man named Laurentius Joannes, surnamed Coster (from custos, keeper or sexton). The story unfolds as follows:

  • The walk in the wood: While strolling in the Haarlem woods (het Hout), Coster carved letters from the bark of a beech tree as a pastime. Impressing them on paper, he produced a few lines of text as a keepsake for his grandchildren.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, p. 331]
  • Development: Encouraged by his son-in-law Thomas Pieterzoon, Coster developed a thicker ink (to prevent blotting), then progressed from wooden letters to lead and finally to tin, which was harder and more durable.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, p. 331]
  • The printed book: Junius identifies the Spieghel onzer behoudenisse — a Dutch edition of the block-book Speculum Humanae Salvationis (Mirror of Salvation) — as Coster’s first typographic work. He notes that early copies were printed on one side only and the blank pages pasted together.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, pp. 331-332]
  • The treacherous servant: A workman named Johann — whom Junius insinuates was Johann Fust (Gutenberg’s financier) — stole the equipment on Christmas Eve while the household attended church. He fled to Amsterdam, then Cologne, and finally to Mainz, where he set up shop and began printing, using Coster’s own types to produce the Doctrinal of Alexander Gallus and the Treatises of Peter of Spain around 1442.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, pp. 332-334]

Junius cites as his sources: the bookbinder Cornelis (who had been Coster’s apprentice and told the story weeping, even at age 80), his own tutor Nicholas Gallius, and the burgomaster Quirinius Talesius.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, pp. 333-334]

Earlier Hints

Before Junius, the legend existed as vague civic pride. In 1561, Jan van Zuren and Dirck Volckharts Coornhert — printers opening a shop in Haarlem — published a preface implying Haarlem had invented printing but naming no inventor. In 1567, Luigi Guicciardini, a Florentine resident of Antwerp, wrote in his Description of the Low Countries that “according to the common tradition of the inhabitants” printing was invented in Haarlem, with a servant carrying the art to Mainz after the inventor’s death. Guicciardini cautiously declined to decide the truth.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, pp. 329-330]

Growth of the Legend: The Patriotic Campaign

Once Junius had given the story a named hero, Dutch patriots elaborated on it enthusiastically.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, Chapter XVIII, pp. 347-359]

Scriverius (1628)

Peter Scriverius published Laurecrans voor Laurens Coster (1628), a poetical elaboration. In his version, Coster cut letters from an oak branch, wrapped them in paper, fell asleep in the woods, and was awakened by a thunderclap, discovering that rain had transferred the letters’ impress to the paper. Scriverius moved the date of invention back to 1428 and added the detail that Coster was a sheriff of Haarlem.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, pp. 348-349]

Boxhorn (1640)

Marcus Zuerius Boxhorn pushed the date further back to 1420 in his Dissertation on the Invention of Typography (1640), without producing new evidence — only patriotic confidence.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, p. 350]

Meerman (1765)

Gerard Meerman, a distinguished Dutch scholar, published Origines Typographicae (1765), the most learned defense of the legend. He conceded nothing to mechanics (he knew nothing of typefounding) and proposed a “contributive invention” theory: Coster invented wooden movable type (c. 1428-1430), Johan Gensfleisch (Gutenberg’s brother) cut metal types, Peter Schoeffer invented cast types. Gutenberg and Fust were mere printers who invented nothing. Meerman was warned by the typefounder John Enschede that his wood-type theory was mechanically absurd, but he ignored the advice.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, pp. 354-356]

Koning (1816)

Jacobus Koning won a prize essay competition with The Origin, Invention and Development of Printing (1816). He brought archival research to bear — discovering records of a Laurens Janszoon Coster in Haarlem tax rolls — but he confounded two different men: a civic official named Laurens Janszoon (who died in 1439) with Lourens Janszoon Coster (a tallow-chandler and innkeeper who appears in records until 1483). Koning willfully suppressed evidence that contradicted his case.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, pp. 356-357]

De Vries (c. 1830)

Dr. Abraham de Vries proposed a date of 1423 and argued that Coster invented printing with movable cast letters, giving the example to Mainz. His chronology was as unsupported as the others.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, p. 358]

The Downfall: Van der Linde’s Archival Demolition

The legend’s decisive refutation came from Dr. Antonius van der Linde, a German-Dutch scholar whose research was published in 1870 as The Haarlem Legend of the Invention of Printing. He was subsequently driven out of Holland for his pains.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md, line 291]

Van der Linde examined the archives of Haarlem — town accounts, church registers, and the membership book of the Holy Christmas Corporation — and established the following facts:^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, pp. 360-368]

  • Lourens Janszoon Coster was a tallow-chandler (candle-maker), supplying the town hall with oil, soap, and candles from 1441 onward.
  • After 1447, he gave up the chandler’s business and became a tavern-keeper. The tavern (at the same address Junius describes) was consistently known as a drinking-house for centuries afterward.
  • He was alive in 1483, when he paid ferry toll to leave Haarlem — contradicting the legend that he died in 1439 or 1440.
  • There is no record — in any archive — that he was ever a printer, a sheriff, a sexton, or a treasurer.
  • The famous Coster pedigree — a vellum document from c. 1550 describing Coster as the man “who brought the first print in the world” — was shown to have been falsified: the date 1446 had been altered to read 1440. The document was written a century after the events it claimed to record, by Gerrit Thomaszoon, a vain innkeeper who lived in Coster’s old house.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, pp. 361-365]

Van der Linde showed that the legendary Laurens Janszoon Coster the printer was a conflation of two men: (1) Laurens Janszoon, a wealthy civic official who died in 1439, and (2) Lourens Janszoon Coster, the chandler/innkeeper who lived until at least 1483. Neither had anything to do with printing.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, p. 368]

De Vinne’s Typographic Critique

Theodore Low De Vinne, in The Invention of Printing (1876, chapters XVII-XIX), reinforced Van der Linde’s archival work with a typographic critique:^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

  • No dated Coster print exists: None of the books attributed to Coster (the Speculum editions, Donatus fragments, etc.) bear a date, a printer’s name, or a place of publication.
  • The Speculum editions are typographic, but not necessarily pre-Gutenberg: They were printed with cast metal types, not wooden ones as Junius claimed. Their type and paper suggest a date in the 1460s or 1470s, well after Gutenberg’s invention.
  • Internal contradictions: Junius says Coster used wooden types, but the Speculum types are unquestionably cast metal. The “Cornelis the bookbinder” cited by Junius was born around 1442 — after the alleged theft — and could not have been an eyewitness. The alleged thief’s first books cannot be traced to Mainz; fragments of the Doctrinal were found in the Netherlands, not Germany.
  • The story of theft is inherently improbable: If Coster had functioning types and trained workmen, the knowledge of the art would not have vanished overnight. Yet Haarlem produced no typographic work between the alleged invention (c. 1440) and the arrival of Jacob Bellaert as the city’s first documented printer in 1483 — a gap of over 40 years.

The Physical Evidence

Portraits of Coster

A revealing anachronism: every known “portrait” of Coster shows him holding a Roman-form matrix of the letter A. But Roman type was not used in the Netherlands in the 1440s — all books attributed to Coster are in Gothic (Textura) letterforms. The portraits were invented long after Coster’s death, based on imagination rather than likeness. Van der Linde suggested the earliest Scriverius engraving might be a portrait of Gerrit Thomaszoon, pressed into service as Coster.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, pp. 371-372]

The Enschedé Typefoundry

The Enschedé family of typefounders in Haarlem played a peculiar role in preserving the legend. John Enschede (the elder) owned a collection of early type specimens, including the Enschede Abecedarium — fragments of an early alphabet that some claimed as Coster types. While Enschede correctly warned Meerman that wood types were unworkable, his collection was later used by patriotic historians as “Costeriana.” The fragments are now recognized as the work of an anonymous Dutch printer working after 1460.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, p. 290, 355]

Monuments and Public Memory

Despite the total lack of evidence, Haarlem erected multiple monuments to Coster:^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, pp. 358-359]

  • A statue in the Doctors’ Garden (early 1600s), without a date of invention — suggesting even then the authorities were unsure.
  • A cenotaph in the Haarlem Wood (the Hout), bearing the arms of the sheriff Laurens Janszoon and the year 1423.
  • A marble tablet in St. Bavo’s Cathedral, placed in 1824 by King William I, acknowledging Coster as the inventor.
  • A memorial stone on the rebuilt “Coster House” (1851) on the market square.
  • The grand statue in the Grote Markt (Market Square), unveiled July 16, 1856, the day accepted as the anniversary of the invention. The pedestal reads: “LOURENS JANSZOON COSTER / INVENTOR OF THE ART OF PRINTING / WITH MOVABLE LETTERS / CAST OF METAL / HOMAGE OF THE NETHERLAND NATION / MDCCCLVI.” The date of the invention was deliberately omitted, reflecting unresolved disagreements among the monument committee.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, pp. 360-361]

The Speculum Salutis Editions

The Speculum Humanae Salvationis (Mirror of Human Salvation) editions — four in total (two Latin, two Dutch) — are central to the Coster claim because they are early, undated, and printed in the Netherlands. Lorck notes their importance: “The book is of Lower Rhine origin, and the Dutch declare this late product to be a work printed by Koster with movable types.”^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md, line 461]

De Vinne devotes his entire chapters XIV-XVI to the Speculum. His conclusion: the Speculum editions are genuine typographic works of the 1460s or 1470s, printed by an unknown Dutch printer whose types show affinities with those of Jacob Bellaert and John Brito in Haarlem. They are valuable as early Dutch typography, but they cannot be dated before Gutenberg’s Bible (c. 1450-1455) and therefore do not establish Coster priority.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, Chapter XVI, pp. 308-325]

Why the Legend Persisted

The Coster Legend survived so long for several reasons:^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, pp. 329, 346, 358]^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md, lines 636-656]

  1. National pride: The Dutch Republic, having fought off Spanish rule, cultivated heroes. Coster became a symbol of Dutch ingenuity.
  2. Undated Dutch incunabula: A body of early undated Dutch typography — the “Costeriana” — could not be securely assigned, leaving room for speculation.
  3. The Speculum puzzle: The Speculum editions blur the line between block-books and typography. Early bibliographers struggled to classify them, and their hybrid nature made the Coster claim arguable.
  4. The lack of Gutenberg documentation: Before the 19th century, Gutenberg’s own priority was poorly documented. The 1439 Strasbourg documents and the Helmasperger Instrument were not widely studied until the 1700s.
  5. Civic investment: Haarlem’s monuments, museums, and festivals created a self-perpetuating tradition. The Costerian Museum displayed type fragments and “relics” that, though worthless as evidence, impressed visitors.

Modern Consensus

Modern scholarship is unanimous: Laurens Janszoon Coster did not invent movable-type printing before Gutenberg. The archival work of Van der Linde, the typographic analysis of De Vinne, and subsequent 20th-century research have established:^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md, lines 652-656]

  • Gutenberg’s priority is confirmed by the 1439 Strasbourg documents (the Dritzehn lawsuit), the 1448 Mainz loan, and the 1455 Helmasperger Notarial Instrument.
  • The “Costeriana” — early Dutch typographic fragments — represent the work of anonymous Dutch printers working after Gutenberg’s invention spread from Mainz.
  • The Speculum editions are transitional works of the 1460s-1470s, combining woodcut illustrations with cast-metal type, valuable for understanding the early spread of the-printing-press to the Low Countries, but not evidence of an independent Dutch invention.
  • Coster himself (the tallow-chandler and tavern-keeper) was an innocent man — he never claimed to have invented printing; the claim was made on his behalf by others a century after his death.

As Haarlem’s chief archivist told John Man: “We know he did not pre-empt Gutenberg. It’s a nice story, nothing more.”^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md, lines 654-656]

Significance in Historiography

The Coster Legend is more than a historical footnote. It illustrates:

  • How nationalist historiography can create and sustain false claims for centuries.
  • The importance of archival evidence over oral tradition and family pedigrees.
  • How typographic analysis — examination of type bodies, paper, ink, and presswork — can date and place printed works with greater precision than textual tradition.
  • The role of civic boosterism in shaping public memory: Haarlem’s monuments remain tourist attractions, even though the story they commemorate is false.

The Coster Legend is, as De Vinne put it, “a snowball that became a snow-heap” — built from a grain of ambiguous tradition by generations of well-meaning but uncritical patriots. Its demolition is a landmark in historical method.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md, p. 346]
