Paper and the Introduction of Paper in Europe

Paper was the material foundation upon which the entire edifice of fifteenth-century printing was built. Before Gutenberg could cast a single type, before a single sheet of the gutenberg-bible could be printed, a reliable and affordable supply of paper had to exist. The story of paper’s journey from China to the printing shops of Mainz spans fourteen centuries and three continents. Without it, movable-type would have remained an expensive curiosity, and the great explosion of the incunabula age — the 15—20 million volumes printed before 1501 — would have been unthinkable. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Invention in China

According to Chinese tradition, paper was invented in ad 105 by the imperial counsellor Cai Lun (Ts’ai Lun), who “conceived the idea of making paper from the bark of trees, hemp waste, old rags and fish nets.” ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] A fifth-century official history records that he mashed his materials into a slurry with a mortar and set it to dry on fish-net webbing. Before this invention, Chinese writing had been done on bamboo strips, silk, or cotton cloth. The timing is significant: the Chinese invention of printing (block-printing) preceded the invention of paper by about 145 years, so the earliest Chinese printed works were on fabric. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Spread Through the Arab World

The secret of paper-making traveled slowly along the Silk Road. Around ad 610, it reached Korea and then Japan. Five hundred years after Cai Lun, Buddhist monks carried the knowledge further east. The pivotal moment came in 751 ad, at the Battle of Talas on the border of modern Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan, when Arab forces defeated a Chinese Tang army. Among the prisoners taken were Chinese paper-makers, who were brought to Samarkand, where the first paper mill outside China was established. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] From there, the art spread through the Islamic world: to Baghdad (793), Damascus, Cairo, and across North Africa. The Arabs improved the process, using linen rags as raw material alongside cotton. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Arrival in Europe: Spain and Sicily

Paper-making reached Europe through two gateways: Moorish Spain and Norman Sicily. The Moors, who had direct intercourse with the East, established the first European paper mills in the Spanish towns of Xativa (near Valencia) and Toledo as early as the mid-twelfth century (the mill at Toledo is recorded in 1085 passing from Moorish to Christian hands). ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] The paper made at Xativa was widely commended for its whiteness. A family of paper-makers is also recorded in Sicily as early as 1102. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

For many years the Moors were the largest manufacturers and consumers. In various cities of Spain, seventy libraries were opened for public instruction during a period when the rest of Europe was “without books, without learning and without cultivation.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Lorck notes that the manufacture of cotton paper (Baumwollen-Papier) was brought to Europe by the Arabs and first took root in Spain, mainly in Xativa (Xàtiva), Valencia, and Toledo. Initially only raw cotton was used, then cotton rags, later a mixture of cotton and linen rags, and finally, by the fourteenth century, linen rags alone. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Fabriano: The Italian Papermaking Revolution

As Arab political power in Spain declined, paper-making shifted to Italy. By the late thirteenth century, paper mills were established around Milan, Venice, Florence, Bologna, Parma, Padua, and Treviso, but the key center was Fabriano, in the Marche region of central Italy. Fabriano’s mills, established by 1276, became the most sophisticated paper-making center in Europe. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Fabriano’s innovations transformed paper from a coarse, fragile commodity into a material fit for fine books and printing:

  • Water-powered hammers (stampers): Instead of the Moorish method of grinding raw cotton (which cut and weakened fibers), Fabriano used water-driven trip-hammers to beat rags into pulp, preserving fiber length and producing stronger paper. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]
  • Gelatin sizing: European quill pens required a harder surface than the soft, absorbent Chinese paper. Fabriano developed animal-gelatin sizing, creating a firm, impervious surface that could take writing — and later, printing — on both sides. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]
  • Watermarks: Fabriano introduced wire watermarks (filigranas) embedded in the paper mold as a maker’s mark, a practice that spread across Europe and now serves as a crucial tool for dating and locating early printed books. In Lorck’s account, the ox-head and the letter P are among the most widely distributed early watermarks; the P likely stands for “Papier.” ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Early Quality Problems and Resistance

The earliest paper in Europe — what De Vinne calls “cotton card” or charta gossypina — was thick, coarse, woolly, yellow, and fragile. It was so inferior to papyrus and parchment that professional copyists and scribes rejected it almost entirely. The fabric was too thick and the surface too rough for the display of ornamental penmanship or illumination. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

This resistance was not without basis. Frederick II, Holy Roman Emperor, in 1221 issued a decree making all public documents written on cotton paper invalid, ordering them transcribed onto parchment within two years. Peter II of Spain, in 1338, commanded the paper-makers of Valencia and Xativa to restore their product to earlier quality standards. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Vellum producers and scribes had every reason to oppose the new material. Vellum was regarded as the only substance fit for writing, even when it was so scarce that palimpsests — manuscripts scraped clean and reused — were common. As De Vinne observes, paper “came before its time and had to wait for recognition.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

From Cotton to Linen: The Shift in Raw Materials

The shift from cotton to linen rags was crucial. The Moors made paper by grinding raw cotton, which cut and weakened fibers, producing a tender, woolly paper. The Spanish and Italian method used stamping with water-powered pestles, preserving fiber length and producing stronger, more flexible paper. This “linen paper” was sometimes called “parchment cloth” for its strength. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Lorck confirms that initially only raw cotton was used, then cotton rags, then a mixture, and finally, by the 14th century, linen rags alone. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Cost Advantage

The economic advantage of paper over vellum was decisive for printing. A book printed on paper cost about one-third of a vellum equivalent. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] De Vinne provides even starker figures: in the library of the Louvre, manuscripts on paper compared to those on vellum were in a ratio of one to twenty-eight in the fourteenth century; the increase in paper books reflected its growing popularity, but vellum was still considered superior for valuable works. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The scale of savings is best illustrated by the gutenberg-bible. The thirty to thirty-five vellum copies of the Bible required approximately 5,000 calfskins, each needing a month or more to prepare. The paper copies — around 150 of them, roughly 200,000 pages worth — were printed on paper hauled overland from Italy, as the watermarks reveal. German paper was not yet good enough for the Bible. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Paper in Germany: Ulman Stromer’s Mill

Paper-making in Germany lagged behind Italy. The first German paper mill was established in Nuremberg by Ulman Stromer around 1390, with workers brought from Italy. Stromer was so protective of his trade secrets that he obliged all his workmen to swear oaths not to reveal the process, fearing that knowledge would give rivals a monopoly. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] Despite this mill, the first Nuremberg printed works were still printed on Italian paper. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Basel, an important early printing center, had a paper mill as early as 1440, operated by Hans Halbysen; the industry received a boost around 1470 from the Gallician brothers, immigrants from Spain. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Lorck notes that the supply zones of European paper followed distinct trade routes: Italian paper supplied southern Germany, even reaching as far as Saxony, while western and northern Germany sourced paper from France and Burgundy, whose products also supplied England. The major paper markets were in Bruges, Antwerp, and Cologne. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Paper’s Essential Role in Printing

The relationship between paper and the development of printing was symbiotic. Early Italian block-printers (who made playing cards, images, and fabrics) had the technique but lacked a suitable material to print on — parchment was too expensive and stiff, cotton paper too coarse and absorbent. De Vinne states this plainly: “The only materials available were parchment, papyrus and stiff cotton paper, all of which were unsuitable. Printing can be done to advantage only on paper, but paper was sparingly used in the fourteenth century. When paper came, printing followed.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The better-quality linen paper from Fabriano and other Italian mills had the strength, flexibility, and surface characteristics that made sustained typographic printing feasible. Early printers quickly discovered that unsized paper — about half the price of the sized variety — was easier to print, absorbing the oily ink better and producing clearer impressions. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The Supply Chain for Early Printers

For the first decades of European printing, paper was a logistical challenge. Italy dominated production, and the spread-of-printing across Europe depended on access to Italian paper. Gutenberg’s Bible, printed in Mainz, used paper transported across the Alps from northern Italy — a journey of some 600 kilometers over mountain passes. The watermark evidence from surviving copies points to paper from the Piedmont region and Fabriano. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

This dependence on imported paper added significant cost and risk to early printing ventures. As the industry matured, local production grew: paper mills appeared in Augsburg (1468), Kempten (1477), and Ravensburg (fortified by abundant water power), though the quality of Ravensburg paper was judged inferior to Italian imports. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] By the 1470s, France had developed substantial production in Troyes (since 1348), and Lyon’s paper mills supplied the thriving printing industry of that city.

Summary

Paper was the silent partner in Gutenberg’s invention. The journey from Cai Lun’s fish-net mold (105 ad) through Samarkand (751), Xativa (1151), Fabriano (1276), and Nuremberg (1390) spans the entire medieval period. Each stage brought improvements: Arab mill technology, Italian water-powered stampers and gelatin sizing, the shift from cotton to linen rags. By Gutenberg’s time, Italian mills could produce paper of sufficient quality, consistency, and quantity to make large-scale printing feasible. The cost advantage over vellum — approximately threefold — was the decisive economic factor that allowed printing to transform from a rich patron’s novelty into a commercial industry, and ultimately to enable the production of millions of incunabula that reshaped European intellectual life.

Paper in the Sixteenth and Seventeenth Centuries

The paper industry matured alongside printing after 1500, shifting from Italian dominance to a diversified European production base.

The Shift to Northern Europe

In the incunable period, Italian paper supplied most of Europe. By the mid-sixteenth century, France and Germany had developed substantial domestic production. Lyon’s paper mills supplied the thriving printing industry of that city, and German mills at Nuremberg, Augsburg, Kempten, and Ravensburg gradually reduced dependence on Italian imports. Basel, with paper mills from 1440, became a major producer for the Swiss and south German markets. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Watermarks remained the primary tool for dating and locating paper. Lorck notes that the ox-head and the monogram P were among the most widely distributed watermarks, but that the same mark could appear in paper from different regions, making identification suggestive rather than definitive. The proliferation of local mills meant that by 1600, a printer in most European cities could source paper within a few hundred kilometers rather than importing it across the Alps. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Paper as a Strategic Resource

Paper supply was a persistent constraint on early printing. In Denmark, where paper-making never prospered, Lorck reports that “even famous writers had to abandon the printing of their works because the necessary paper could not be obtained.” The first Danish paper mill was established on Zealand in 1576 but “did not prosper.” Denmark and Norway imported both types and paper from Germany and Holland throughout the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

In Sweden, when Queen Christina recruited the Amsterdam printer Johann Jansson to Stockholm, she granted him among other advantages “free paper import,” recognizing that Swedish paper could not meet his needs. The Swedish printing industry remained dependent on imported paper well into the seventeenth century. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Dutch Paper and the Golden Age

The Dutch Republic became a major paper producer in the seventeenth century, supplying not only its own thriving printing industry (Elzevir, Blaeu, Janssonius) but also export markets. Dutch paper was known for its consistency and whiteness, and Dutch paper-makers innovated in sizing and finishing techniques. The quality of Dutch paper was one factor — alongside typographic excellence and press freedom — that made Amsterdam and Leyden the printing capitals of the seventeenth century. Lorck notes that Dutch paper production was so extensive that it supported not only domestic printing but also significant exports to England and Scandinavia. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Paper and the Cost of Books

The economic relationship between paper and printing remained stable: paper typically accounted for roughly half the total cost of producing a book, a ratio that held from Gutenberg’s time through the seventeenth century. This meant that the cost of books was fundamentally constrained by the cost of paper, and that regional differences in paper prices directly affected the competitiveness of local printing industries. Venice’s access to cheap, high-quality paper from the Fabriano region and the Alpine valleys was a decisive advantage in the fifteenth century; Amsterdam’s paper industry served the same function in the seventeenth. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Watermarks as Historical Evidence

The study of watermarks became an essential tool for incunable and early modern bibliography. Lorck devotes attention to the interpretation of watermarks, noting that while they are not infallible (because the same design was used by multiple mills and paper from different locations could be bound in the same volume), they remain “noteworthy contributions to the assessment of the origin of older printed works.” The systematic cataloguing of watermarks — beginning in the nineteenth century with the works of Sotheby, Weigel, and others — made it possible to trace the paper supply of individual printing shops and to date undated editions. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]
