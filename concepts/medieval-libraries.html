Medieval Libraries

Based on Putnam, Books and Their Makers in the Middle Ages, Vol. I, Chapter II (“Some Libraries of the Manuscript Period”), lines 1510–1700+.

Earliest Ecclesiastical Libraries

The earliest ecclesiastical library was probably that collected by Bishop Alexander in Jerusalem at the beginning of the third century. Fifty years later, a library was founded at Caesarea by Origen, described as extensive and important 1. Collections were also made at an early date at Hippo, at Cirta, at Constantinople, and at St. Peter’s and the Lateran in Rome. All of these earlier libraries were connected with churches, and in most cases places had been found for them within the church walls. Clark quotes from a narrative of the persecution of 303–304: the officers “went to the church where the Christians used to assemble, and spoiled it of chalices, lamps, etc., but when they came to the library (bibliothecam), the presses (armaria) were found empty” 2. This indicates that vessels and books were in different parts of the same building.

The library of St. Augustine was bequeathed to the church of Hippo, and preserved within the church building.

In the Greek Church, the Patriarch of Constantinople had a library in Thomaïtes said to be of considerable importance, often referred to in Synod transactions. It was destroyed by fire in 780 but speedily replaced 3. The monastery of Mt. Athos possessed one of the most important Greek collections, with some treasures preserved to the present day. During Basilius Macedo (867–886), much scribal work was done there 3. In Egypt, literary activity in monasteries continued until the Arab conquest; the monastery of St. Catherine of the Sinai preserved early manuscripts, most notably the Testament discovered by Tischendorf.

Benedictine Library Regulations

The regulations of libraries in all Benedictine monasteries were based upon the Rule of St. Benedict. As new Orders were founded, care for books steadily developed. St. Benedict gave only general directions for study. The Cluniacs prescribed the selection of a special officer to take charge of the books, with an annual audit and assignment to each Brother of a single volume for the year’s study. The Cistercians and Carthusians provided for loans to outsiders under certain conditions, a practice later adopted by the Benedictines. The Augustinians prescribed the kind of press (armarium) for keeping books, and both they and the Premonstratensians permitted books to be lent on receipt of pledges of sufficient value.

The Mendicant Friars, who originally restrained themselves from holding possessions, found books indispensable, and their libraries came to excel those of most other Orders. Richard de Bury, in his Philobiblon, said of the Mendicants: “These men are as ants, ever preparing their meat in the summer, or as ingenious bees continually fabricating cells of honey… although they lately at the eleventh hour have entered the Lord’s vineyard, they have added more in this brief hour to the stock of sacred books than all the other vine-dressers.”

Armarius System

Clark points out that the word “Library” was used by the Benedictines long before any special room was assigned for book storage. Until the 13th and 14th centuries, books were mostly kept in the cloisters (the only areas except the refectory and occasionally the califactorium where monks could congregate). Books in the cloisters were shut up in presses called armaria — the same term used by the Romans for their book-cases. The monk charged with the care of the books took his name not from the books but from the presses, and was generally styled armarius.

In the Customs of the Augustinian priory of Barnwell (late 13th century): “The press in which the books are kept ought to be lined inside with wood, that the damp of the walls may not moisten or stain the books. This press should be divided vertically as well as horizontally by sundry partitions, on which the books may be ranged so as to be separated from one another: for fear they be packed so close as to injure each other, or to delay those who want them.”

The catalogue of the House of the White Canons at Titchfield in Hampshire (dated 1400) shows books kept in a small room on shelves called columpnae set against the walls — a closet of storage, not a working place. By the early 15th century, larger monasteries had accumulated many hundred volumes, and separate rooms were constructed for collections. The presses in the cloisters were still used for books in daily reference.

Annual Audits

A statute of Archbishop Lanfranc for the English Benedictines (dated 1070), based on general monastic practice, gives the following regulation: “On the Monday after the first Sunday in Lent, before Brethren come into the Chapter House, the librarian [here called custos librorum , not armarius] shall have a carpet laid down and all the books got together upon it, except those which the year previous had been assigned for reading. These the Brethren are to bring with them, when they come into the Chapter House, each his book in his hand. Then the librarian shall read a statement as to the manner in which Brethren have had books during the past year. As each Brother hears his name pronounced, he is to give back the book which had been entrusted to him for reading; and he whose conscience accuses him of not having read through the book which he had received, is to fall on his face, confess his fault, and entreat forgiveness. The librarian shall then make a fresh distribution of books, namely a different volume to each Brother for his reading.”

Two classes of volumes are evident: one for distribution for separate reading, and one reserved for reference in a separate room (first called armarium, later bibliotheca), where books were fastened with iron chains to lecterns or reading-desks.

The statutes of Oriel College, Oxford (1329) borrowed directly from monastic customs: “The common books (libri communes) of the House are to be brought out and inspected once a year, on the feast of the Commemoration of Souls (November 2d) in the presence of the Provost or his deputy, and of the scholars (Fellows). Each one of the scholars, in the order of seniority, may select a single book… This he may keep until the same festival in the succeeding year.”

Similarly, the Brothers of Common Life ordered that at least once a year all books not out on loan should be called in and inspected in the presence of the Brothers.

Carrells

In monasteries where literary studies were pursued with special ardour, readers and scribes were provided with small wooden compartments or studies called carrells. The Rites of Durham describes them:

“In the north syde of the cloister, from the corner over againste the church dour to the corner over againste the Dorter dour, was all fynely glased from the hight to the sole within a little of the ground into the Cloister garth, and in every window iij Pewes or Carrells, where every one of the old monks had his carrell, several by himselfs, that, when they had dyned, they dyd resort to that place of Cloister, and there studyed upon these books, every one in his carrell, all the after nonne, unto evensong tyme… In every carrell was a deske to lye there books upon, and the carrell was no greater than from one stanchell of the wyndowe to another, and over againste the carrells againste the church wall, did stande certain great almeries (or cupbords) of waynscott all full of bookes (with great store of ancient manuscripts to help them in their study) wherein did lye as well the old anncyent written Doctors of the Church as other prophane authors, with dyverse other holie men’s wourks, so that every one did studye what Doctor pleased them best, havinge the Librarie at all tymes to goe studye in besydes there carrells.” 4

Chained Library System

The system of chaining, as adopted in England, allowed books to be readily taken down from shelves and placed on a lectern for reading. One end of the chain was attached to the middle of the upper edge of the right-hand board or cover; the other to a ring which played on a bar set in front of the shelf. The fore-edge of the books, not the back, was turned to the front. A swivel, usually in the middle of the chain, prevented tangling. Chains varied in length according to the distance of the shelf from the desk 5.

A copy of Locke’s Treatise on the Epistles (printed 1711) bears an inscription complaining about thieves in libraries, noting that chains were necessary “to secure the innocent books, and even the sacred volumes themselves” 6.

At Clairvaux, the library (described in 1723) was “stocked with a large number of manuscripts, fastened by chains to desks.” At Bordesholm (1477), Brother Liborius gave over his works on jurisprudence “with the provision that they were to be placed in chains and to remain forever in the reading-room” 7.

At the Sorbonne (statutes of 1321), all books were chained to the wall or reading-desks. Books not protected by chains were called libri vagantes and could, under restrictions, be loaned out; Wattenbach believed only duplicates were placed in this class.

The College of Plessis statutes of 1455 prescribed that all books except Missals must be chained, and unchaining required authorisation of the master of all bursars.

Cataloguing Practices

Isidore, Bishop of Seville (c. 560–636) possessed probably the largest collection of books in Europe at the time, contained in fourteen presses or armaria, each ornamented with a bust and inscribed with verses.

The monastery of Vivaria (founded by Cassiodorus) classified writings according to contents, arranged in a series of armaria.

At Reichenau, librarian Reginbert prepared in 821 an exhaustive catalogue of the collection, with notes showing many manuscripts were his own work. Only five sheets of one book and a portion of the catalogue remain.

The first catalogue of St. Gall includes beneath the titles various critical notes, with record of loans to Emperor Charles III., Frau Rickert, and Liutward, Bishop of Vercelli 8.

At Pomposa, Henricus Clericus (writing in 1093) prepared a catalogue of the library and at the close found it necessary to apologise for the abbot including heathen books. These libri scholastici were by no means exceptional; copies of Virgil, Ovid, and Cicero were found in many monastery collections. A separate catalogue of libri scholastici was made in 1297 for the cathedral library of Lübeck. An inventory of Neumünster (near Wurzburg, 1233) includes a special list of Classics.

At St. Père-de-Chartres, a catalogue inscribed in four rolls (1367) gave titles of 201 volumes 9. At Admunt, the library was catalogued in 1380 by Brother Peter of Arbonne. At Bordesholm, a catalogue prepared in 1498 contained more than five hundred titles 7.

Great Monastic Collections

Bobbio

After the beginning of the 7th century, the most noteworthy collection was that of Bobbio. A portion remained as late as 1618 and was taken by Paul V. for the Vatican Library; another portion went to Turin. Bobbio received books from wandering Irishmen, as indicated by an inscription recording that Dungal bestowed a book upon St. Columba 10.

Reichenau

In Germany, the monastery of Reichenau was noted as early as 821 for its excellent collection of manuscripts. Librarian Reginbert prepared an exhaustive catalogue in 821.

St. Gall

Of nearly as early a date is the first catalogue of St. Gall, with various critical notes beneath titles and records of loans.

Pomposa (Lombardy)

Abbot Jerome brought together in the 11th century a great collection of manuscripts, despite grumblings from the monks. Henricus Clericus wrote in 1093 that in no church, not even in Rome, could such a wonderful group of books be found 11.

Christ Church, Canterbury

As early as the 14th century, the collection comprised as many as 698 books. A library at Canterbury was built about 1425 by Archbishop Chichele 12.

Clairvaux

One of the two great Cistercian foundations in France. The Queen of Sicily (who visited in 1517) described: “On the same side of the cloister are fourteen studies, where the monks do their reading and writing, and over these studies, one mounts by a broad spiral staircase to the new library. This library is 189 feet long by 17 feet wide. It contains 48 seats (bancs) and in each banc four shelves (poulpitres) furnished with books on all subjects, but chiefly theology; the greater number of the said books are of vellum and are written by hand, richly storied and illuminated.”

A 1723 description from the Voyage Littéraire speaks of “fifteen little cells, all in a row, where the Brethren formerly used to write books, for which reason they are still called the writing rooms. Over these cells is the library, the building for which is large, vaulted, well lighted, and stocked with a large number of manuscripts, fastened by chains to desks; but there are not many printed books.”

St. Germain des Prés

The library dates from 1513. The collection was one of the earliest in France; as early as the beginning of the 13th century, there is record of it being consulted by strangers. At the time of the French Revolution, it contained 7000 manuscripts and 4900 printed books 12.

Citeaux

The library was placed over the scriptorium and dates from 1480 12.

Durham

The library at Durham was built about the same time (c. 1425) by Prior Wessyngton 12.

Corbie

The library existed throughout the lifetime of the monastery. After 1350, the monks gave up the work of writing. Étienne de Conty is recorded as a special benefactor, collecting books and employing special scribes 13.

Fleury

Léon Maitre says each new scholar was expected to present at least two codices.

St. Père-de-Chartres

Abbot Alveus (died 955) presented a book Pro Vita Æterna 14. His successor Fulbert instituted a new room for the collection and kept the monks working; a 1367 catalogue in four rolls listed 201 volumes 9.

Scottish Monastery (near Vienna)

A record of gifts kept in the 13th century includes a long list of book presents. The monastery degenerated later and presents ceased. In 1418, Scottish monks were driven out and Germans took possession; from this date, gifts of books resumed. In 1453, it received the entire library of Dr. Johannes Polzmacher as a bequest 15.

Evesham (Worcestershire)

A statute enacted in 1215 provided that certain tenths should be reserved for buying parchment and increasing the library. The following year, the amount available was five solidos, eighteen deniers 16.

Ely

Account books show that in 1300, the monks purchased five dozen sheets of parchment, four pounds of ink, eight calf-skins, four sheep-skins, five dozen sheets of vellum, and six pairs of book clasps. They paid six shillings for a Decretal and two shillings for a Speculum Gregor. In 1329, the Precentor received six shillings and seven pence to go to Balsham to purchase books; four shillings were paid for twelve iron chains. Between 1350 and 1356, purchases included no less than seventy dozen sheets of parchment and thirty dozen sheets of vellum 17.

San Giovanni in Naples

Not an old monastery collection; its origin, according to Blume, was with Janus Parrhasius. The Augustin monks presented it in 1729 to Emperor Charles VI to avoid visits from zealous scholars 1819.

Admunt

Catalogued in 1380 by Brother Peter of Arbonne.

Leyden — Chapter of S. Pancras

Received in 1380, through a bequest of Philip of Leyden, a collection of 80 manuscripts, whose catalogue has been preserved 20.

St. Ulrich (near Augsburg)

The Benedictine monastery retained its importance until the invention of printing; in 1472 a printing office was instituted in connection with it.

Sponheim

Abbot Trithemius presented in 1480 the sum of fifteen hundred ducats for the enlargement of its library.

Beuggen (near Rheinfelden)

A library was founded in 1345 through the exertions of Wulfram of Nellenberg, who directed that all books left by deceased Brothers throughout Elsass be brought there, and urged living Brothers to present their own books 21.

Dargun (Rostock)

Prince Borwin of Rostock in 1240 presented a hide of land, the proceeds to be used for the repairing and preservation of books 22.

Penfont (Rennes)

Adam, treasurer of the Chapter of Rennes, in 1231 presented his library to the abbey, with the condition that books never be diverted and that copies be lent only against adequate pledges.

Other Notable Collections

  • Verona (library of the Town Hall): one of the most important Italian collections where old manuscripts have been preserved to the present (Wattenbach).
  • Hexham (England): originally collected by Bishop Acca in the year 700; referred to by Bede 23.
  • York: first described by Alcuin 24.
  • Lupus of Ferrières wrote to Pope Benedict III (855) begging for loan of texts including Jerome on Jeremiah, Cicero de Oratore, Quintilian, and Terence 25.

Brothers of Common Life Libraries

The Brothers of Common Life planned their collections expressly with reference to the service of students in their schools. These libraries contained a much larger proportion of books in the vernacular than were found in other monasteries. In some Brotherhood Homes, the library was divided into a collection for the Brothers and a collection for the students. It was ordered that at least once a year, all books not out on loan should be called in and inspected in the presence of the Brothers.

Monastery Reform

The Monastery Reform, instituted at the beginning of the 15th century, exercised a decided influence on interest in books and the development of libraries.

  • Tegernsee: The once noted library had fallen into ruins. Abbot Casper (1426–1461) reorganised it, restored manuscripts, bought new codices, and put hired scribes to work. His successor Conrad V purchased for eleven hundred pounds heller no less than 450 volumes, in addition to securing gifts 26.
  • Salzburg: Archbishop Johann II (1429–1441) caused a new library building to be erected and collected many beautiful manuscripts.
  • Bergen (near Magdeburg): Abbot Bursfelder (1450–1478) organised a library, utilising an old chapel.
  • Bordesholm: Prior Martin instituted a library in 1477. Brother Liborius (professor in Rostock) gave over his works on jurisprudence in 1405, with the provision that they be chained and remain forever in the reading-room. A catalogue of 1498 contains more than 500 titles 7.

Imperial Library of Constantinople

Of the libraries of antiquity, only a single one — the Imperial Library of Constantinople — continued in existence as late as the Middle Ages.

  • Founded in 354 by Emperor Constantius.
  • Largely added to by Julian the Philosopher.
  • Under Emperor Basiliscus, the original library — said to have contained no less than 120,000 volumes — was destroyed by fire.
  • Afterwards reinstituted by Emperor Zeno, with Julian (prefect of the city) giving personal supervision.
  • References are made to it in 1276, and again early in the 14th century, when John Palæologus was able to present from it certain manuscripts (probably duplicates) to the manuscript dealer Aurispa of Venice.
  • It is probable that the manuscripts were to some extent scattered before the fall of the city in 1453. Manuscripts that escaped destruction during the siege were hidden by scholars in various monasteries and out-of-the-way corners, from which they were brought out by degrees during the following two or three centuries.
  • Large quantities found their way promptly to Italy, chiefly through Venice. Not a few Greek scholars driven from Byzantine territories brought into Italy collections of manuscripts as their sole valuable possessions, used as texts for lectures or for transcribing for sale.

Gifts and Bequests

While the principal increase in monastery libraries came through scribal work, exchanges, and occasional purchases, a considerable proportion came through gifts or bequests. The gift a novice customarily made on entering a monastery very frequently took the form of books.

  • 1055: Priest Richlof, placing his son with the Benedictines, gave a farm and books; his mother gave a copy of a treatise of St. Ambrose 27.
  • Tegernsee (late 11th century): A noble cleric entering as a monk brought so many books that, when placed by the principal altar, they covered it from top to bottom 28.
  • Dietrich Schreiber of Halle gave in 1239 to the preaching Brothers of Leipzig a canonistic manuscript, with the condition that either of his sons could redeem it for five marks 29.
  • Robert of Lille (died 1339) left in his will to his daughters an illuminated calendar, with the condition that after their death it go to the nuns of Chikessaund 30.
  • Bequests of annual income: The Abbot of St. Père-de-Chartres ordered in 1145 that tenants must take up each year for the support of the library eighty-six solidos 31.

Public and University Libraries in the Monastic Tradition

The collections in Western monasteries, brought together for the requirements of the monks and restricted (at least in theory) to devotional or doctrinal books, were in large measure placed at the disposal of scholars outside the monasteries as literary interest extended beyond the class of ecclesiastics. Long after monks and ecclesiastics ceased to exercise control over books, the most convenient space for collections was still found in church buildings; many came to be known as cathedral libraries.

The provisions of the statutes affecting the libraries at Oxford and Cambridge colleges were borrowed directly from the customs of the monasteries. The Sorbonne (dating from 1253) had its librarium instituted in 1289, specified for common use of instructors and students; a catalogue of 1290 contained 1017 titles 32. The Sorbonne also took the initiative in inviting the first printers to Paris in 1469, with the prior and librarian finding work and support for two printers from Mayence; the first book printed in Paris was the letters of Gasparin of Bergamo (1470), bearing the imprint in aedibus Sorbonnae 33.
