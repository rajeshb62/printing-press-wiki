Seventeenth-Century Printing

The sixteenth century made printing an industry. The seventeenth century made it a medium.

By 1600, the press was no longer a new technology. The basic processes — punchcutting, matrix-striking, typecasting, composition, impression — had been stable for over a century. What changed in the 1600s was not how printing was done but what it was used for, who controlled it, where it was centred, and how far its reach extended. The newspaper, the scientific report, the novel, the children’s book, the systematic atlas, and the first copyright laws all belong to this century. The wooden hand press reached its mature mechanical form, typefounding became an independent industry, and the geographic centre of European printing shifted definitively to the Dutch Republic.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md]

The Press Reaches Maturity

The wooden screw press that Gutenberg had adapted from the winepress remained essentially unchanged for nearly 170 years. The first significant improvements came around 1620, when Willem Jansz Blaeu (1571-1638), the Amsterdam printer and instrument-maker, introduced a series of mechanical refinements. Hoe describes the Blaeu press: the spindle passed through a square block guided in the wooden frame, the platen was suspended by wires and cords from this block, preventing twist and ensuring equal motion across the entire impression. A device for rolling the bed in and out was added, along with a new form of iron hand lever for turning the screw.^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md]

Lorck notes that Blaeu, described as “a skilled mechanic” (ein tuchtiger Mechaniker), built nine presses of this improved construction, named after the nine Muses. The Blaeu press became the standard throughout Europe and remained so until the Earl of Stanhope’s iron press in 1798. It was substantially the same press that Benjamin Franklin worked on as a journeyman in London a century later.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]^[raw/articles/hoe-history-of-printing-press.md]

Blaeu’s printing house on the Damrak in Amsterdam, opened in 1637 and enlarged in 1639, was considered the most significant and beautiful in Europe, regularly employing over forty workers. It was destroyed by fire on 22 February 1672 — strict Protestants called it divine punishment because Blaeu printed many breviaries and missals for Catholics. The firm continued under his sons Peter and Johann, but the 1672 disaster, combined with the general Dutch economic decline of the late seventeenth century, ended its pre-eminence.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]^[raw/articles/blaeu-review-nellen.md]

Typefounding Becomes an Independent Industry

In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, printers cut their own type. By the seventeenth, typefounding had become a distinct trade, and the Dutch typefounding industry supplied all of Europe. The key figures:

Christoffel van Dyck (c. 1600-1669) was described by contemporaries as “the first punch-cutter of his time.” He cut the types used by the Elzevir family, whose clean, sharp letterforms defined the look of the scholarly duodecimo. Van Dyck also cut types for Joseph Athias, a Spanish Jew who printed in Amsterdam and produced a celebrated Hebrew Bible whose types “still count among the best.”^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Enschedé family in Haarlem became the most famous typefounding dynasty of the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Isaak Enschedé (born 1681) opened a printing house around 1703. His son Johannes acquired the Wetstein foundry in 1743 and later the Blaeu and Elzevir foundries, assembling the largest collection of historic types in Europe. The Enschedé Specimen de caracteres typographiques anciens preserved type specimens that no longer survive in their original matrices.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The separation of typefounding from printing was a quiet industrial revolution: it meant that a printer in London could buy the same Dutch types as a printer in Paris or Copenhagen, creating a standardisation of typography across Europe that had not existed in the sixteenth century.

The Dutch Republic Becomes the Printing Capital of Europe

The centre of gravity that had shifted from Venice to Antwerp in the sixteenth century moved again in the seventeenth — to the Dutch Republic. The fall of Antwerp to Spanish forces in 1585 sent Protestant printers, scholars, and capital north to Amsterdam, Leyden, Utrecht, and Haarlem. The Dutch Republic offered what no other European state could match: relative religious toleration, a highly literate urban population, excellent trade networks, and a legal system that — while far from granting full press freedom — was more permissive than the Index-controlled Catholic states or the Star Chamber-controlled English press.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]^[raw/articles/blaeu-review-nellen.md]

Four cities dominated:

  • Amsterdam: The largest. Home to the Blaeu firm, the Janssonius firm, Joseph Athias (Hebrew printing), and dozens of smaller presses. Amsterdam printed everything: Bibles for export, scholarly works in Latin, newspapers in Dutch, maps for the East India Company, and clandestine Catholic liturgical works.
  • Leyden: The university city. The Elzevir family established their press here, securing the post of university printer and bookseller. The Elzevirs’ duodecimo classics were known across Europe for their elegance and affordability.
  • Haarlem: The Enschedé typefoundry. Also the centre of the Coster legend — the claim that Laurens Janszoon Coster, not Gutenberg, invented printing. Lorck devotes substantial space to refuting this claim, which was a matter of Dutch national pride.
  • Utrecht: A smaller but significant academic and theological printing centre.

The dominance of the Dutch Republic meant that Dutch became a major European print language for the first time, and that the format and typography of Dutch books — especially the Elzevir duodecimo and the sharp van Dyck types — set the standard that the rest of Europe imitated.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Newspaper is Born

The most consequential new product of the seventeenth-century press was the newspaper. Before 1600, news had been transmitted through occasional pamphlets, handwritten newsletters, and word of mouth. The idea of a regularly published serial giving current news was an invention of the early 1600s.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The first regularly published newspapers appeared almost simultaneously across Europe: Strasbourg (1605), Berlin (1615, printed by Christoph Runge), Antwerp, London, Paris, and Copenhagen. Lorck notes that by the first quarter of the eighteenth century, London alone had three daily newspapers, ten published three times weekly, and five weeklies, supported by seventy-five printing offices in the capital and twenty-eight in the provinces.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The newspaper created new pressures on the press:

  • Regular deadlines demanded faster production and a larger workforce.
  • Subscription economics replaced single-sale book economics — a publisher needed a steady subscriber base, not one-off buyers.
  • Censorship became harder because newspapers reported on current events that could not be anticipated or pre-approved.
  • The news trade created a new relationship between print and power: governments wanted to control the news, but they also wanted their own version printed.

The first English newspapers appeared during the Civil War period: the Mercurius Aulicus (Royalist) and the Mercurius Britanicus (Parliamentarian) in the 1640s. The London Gazette (1666) became the first official government newspaper. The Tatler (1709) and the Spectator (1711) by Steele and Addison created the English periodical essay, which influenced European journalism for a century. The 1712 stamp tax — a halfpenny per half-sheet — doubled newspaper prices and reduced circulation, establishing a pattern of taxation-as-censorship that lasted into the nineteenth century.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

In the American colonies, the Boston News-Letter (1704) was the first newspaper, followed by the Boston Gazette (1719), printed by James Franklin. The newspaper as a genre was now a global phenomenon: Mexico and Peru likely had newspapers from the end of the seventeenth century, and issues of the Mexico-Gazette from 1728-1730 survive.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Atlas Reaches Its Peak

The sixteenth century had invented the atlas (Ortelius 1570, Mercator 1595). The seventeenth century made it monumental. Johann Blaeu’s Atlas Maior (1662-1665), eleven volumes in the grandest edition, was the largest and most expensive printed book of its century. Lorck describes it as “a magnificent Atlas in 12 large folio volumes” followed by several equally grand works.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]^[raw/articles/blaeu-review-nellen.md]

The Atlas Maior represented the integration of cartography, copperplate engraving, and commercial publishing at the highest level. It included maps of every known region, city views, and extensive descriptive text. The cost of production — the copperplates alone represented a huge investment — was so great that it burdened the Blaeu firm’s finances. The 1672 fire that destroyed Blaeu’s printing office was the final blow.^[raw/articles/blaeu-review-nellen.md]

The atlas market was intensely competitive. Blaeu competed directly with Willem Janssonius and Jacob Aertsz Colom, both of whom worked within sight of Blaeu’s shop on the Damrak. The same map plates were bought, sold, and reused across firms. The atlas was the prestige product of the seventeenth-century press — too expensive for most readers, but essential for any printer who wanted to be taken seriously.^[raw/articles/blaeu-review-nellen.md]^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Bible Printing in the Vernacular

The seventeenth century saw the printing of the two most important English and Dutch Bibles.

The King James Bible (1611) was the work of fifty-four scholars appointed by James I, divided into six companies working at Westminster, Cambridge, and Oxford. It replaced the Geneva Bible as the standard English translation. The first edition was printed by Robert Barker. Lorck records the details of the commission and the 1611 publication.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The “Wicked Bible” of 1632, printed by George Baker and George Lucas, famously omitted the word “not” from the Seventh Commandment (“Thou shalt commit adultery”). The edition was confiscated and the printers fined 3,000. The fine money was used to purchase Greek matrices and types for the royal printers — a remarkable example of a printing error shaping the typographic resources of the English press.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Dutch States Bible (1637) was the first official Dutch translation authorised by the States General, serving the same consolidating function for Dutch Protestantism that the KJV served for English. It was printed by several presses in large editions.

Bible printing was the most capital-intensive and politically sensitive branch of the trade. A large Bible edition required months of press time, expensive paper, and careful correction. The profit on a successful Bible edition was substantial, but the risk of error or political offence was severe.

The Novel and the Shakespeare First Folio

Two literary landmarks belong to this century.

Miguel de Cervantes’ Don Quixote (Part I, 1605; Part II, 1615) was printed in Madrid by Juan de la Cuesta. It is often described as the first modern European novel — a sustained work of vernacular prose fiction with developed characters and a unified narrative. It was translated into every major European language within decades and became the most influential work of fiction of the century.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

William Shakespeare’s First Folio (1623) was printed by Isaac Jaggard and Edward Blount. The first collected edition of Shakespeare’s plays, it preserved thirty-six plays, eighteen of which had never been printed before. Only about 750 copies were printed; the fact that two editions sufficed until 1664 (barely 1,000 copies total) tells us how small the market for English drama was in the seventeenth century, compared to the hagiographic status of the playwright today.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Other notable literary publishing: John Bunyan’s The Pilgrim’s Progress (1678), continuously reprinted; Daniel Defoe’s Robinson Crusoe (1719), which had forty-one editions in its original form. Lorck also notes that at the end of the seventeenth century, Robert Clavel’s General Catalogue of Books (1676, continued to 1700) provided the first systematic English-language book trade bibliography.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The English Press and the Struggle for Control

The seventeenth century in England was the great age of press control and the beginning of press freedom.

The Stationers’ Company (chartered 1556) had given the Crown effective control over English printing. Under Elizabeth I, all books required official approval. The Star Chamber decrees of 1637, under Archbishop Laud, reduced the number of London printers to twenty, type foundries to four, and limited each to two apprentices. Only London could print, except for the university presses at Oxford and Cambridge.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Civil War and Interregnum (1642-1660) temporarily broke this system. Parliament and the Crown each tried to control the press, and both failed. The number of presses expanded rapidly. John Milton’s Areopagitica (1644) was the most eloquent defence of press freedom of the century, though even Milton did not advocate complete freedom — he excluded Catholics and Unitarians.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

After the Restoration (1660), the Licensing Act of 1662 restored pre-publication censorship and required the registration of all books. Three copies of every work had to be deposited in libraries. But the system was leaky, and enforcement was inconsistent. When the Licensing Act lapsed in 1694, it was not renewed. Lorck states: “1694: the last restrictive measures against the press were lifted — England can be considered to have a free press from this time.”^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Copyright and the Statute of Anne

The first exclusive privilege for a printed book had been granted to Richard Pynson in 1518. But throughout the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, “copyright” was a right of the publisher, not the author. It was protected by the Stationers’ Company register and enforced by the Crown. The author had no legal right in his own work.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Statute of Anne (1710, referred to in the sources as 1709 under old-style dating) changed this. For the first time, copyright was recognised as the author’s right, not the publisher’s. The term was fourteen years, renewable for another fourteen if the author was still alive at the end of the first term. Putnam describes it as the foundational copyright legislation of Europe. Lorck notes that “in these privileges are found the first traces of recognition of intellectual property rights, though more in the material interest of the publisher than the author.”^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

In the Dutch Republic, the situation was different. Privileges (printing patents) were granted by individual provinces and were only effective within the issuing jurisdiction. An expired privilege made a book “vogelvrij” (fair game) for reprinters. Blaeu could not obtain a privilege for Grotius’s De iure belli ac pacis (1631) due to political sensitivities — a reminder that copyright was never merely a legal question but a political one.^[raw/articles/blaeu-review-nellen.md]

The Frankfurt-Leipzig Book Fair Shift

The Frankfurt Book Fair had been the centre of the European book trade since the late fifteenth century. In the seventeenth century, it was supplanted by Leipzig.

The first Leipzig fair catalogue appeared in 1594. In 1595, Leipzig listed 68 works against Frankfurt’s 117. By 1604, Leipzig had overtaken Frankfurt. The Thirty Years’ War (1618-1648) accelerated the shift: by 1632, Leipzig had 221 works to Frankfurt’s 68. Leipzig’s advantage was partly geographical (central Germany, out of reach of the Catholic censorship that constrained Frankfurt) and partly commercial (a more efficient fair organisation).^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The total Leipzig press output between 1641 and 1740 was 19,711 items, with the strongest year being 1698 at 401 works. By 1689, Leipzig listed 310 works to Frankfurt’s 90; by 1699, 319 to 109. The Thirty Years’ War also brought a general decline in book quality — poor type, sloppy proofreading, bad paper — from which the German trade recovered only at the very end of the century.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Stereotyping: The First Attempt at Mass Production

The idea of making a single cast of an entire page — stereotyping — was experimented with around 1700. J. van der Mey and the German preacher Johann Müller in Leyden produced several “stereotyped” works, including a Bible in folio and quarto, a New Testament in English and Greek, and a Syriac dictionary. Four of their plates survived to be exhibited at the Caxton Jubilee in London in 1877. The Enschedé firm printed a Bible in folio in 1727 using the van der Mey-Müller process.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

William Ged in Scotland experimented with stereotyping in partnership with the typefounder Thomas James around 1710. But printers viewed stereotyping with suspicion, seeing it as a threat to their compositors’ livelihood. Ged’s experiments brought James “direct and indirect losses,” and the technology was abandoned for nearly a century. True economic stereotyping had to await the mass-demand for Bibles and newspapers of the nineteenth century.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Copperplate Illustration Becomes Standard

In the sixteenth century, woodcut was the standard technique for book illustration. In the seventeenth, copperplate replaced it for all serious work. Lorck notes the decisive shift: “From the Papillons onward, woodcut declined completely and only copperplate engraving was used for book illustration” — not just as separate plates but also for vignettes printed in the text.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Jean Papillon (1660-1710) was the founder of a wood-engraving family, but even his work could not reverse the trend toward copperplate. The Dutch in particular excelled in copperplate engraving and etching. The Plantin-Moretus Museum’s collection of 7,000 to 8,000 copperplates testifies to the scale of the copperplate trade. The integration of copperplate illustration with letterpress text — requiring separate presses and careful planning — was now standard practice for any ambitious book.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Children’s Books and Education

John Amos Comenius (1592-1670), the Czech educational reformer, published his Orbis Sensualium Pictus (The Visible World in Pictures) in 1658. It was the first children’s picture book — a Latin-German illustrated encyclopaedia designed to teach language through labelled woodcut scenes. It was reprinted for two centuries and translated into every European language. The idea that children needed books designed specifically for them was itself a product of print culture. Comenius’s earlier treatise on language teaching was published by the Elzevir press in 1642 and “had a great popularity.”^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The French Royal Printing Office and State Patronage

Under Louis XIII and Cardinal Richelieu, the Imprimerie Royale (Royal Printing Office) was founded in Paris. In 1631, Richelieu assigned the printing of liturgical and sacred texts in Oriental languages to a Paris consortium. Under Louis XIV, the press was patronised but also restricted. The Revocation of the Edict of Nantes (1685) drove French papermakers and printers to emigrate to England and the Netherlands, boosting English papermaking but devastating the French trade.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Moxon’s Mechanick Exercises (1683)

Joseph Moxon’s Mechanick Exercises on the Whole Art of Printing (1683) was the first complete textbook describing the printing process in detail. Moxon, a London printer and maker of globes, described the hand mould, the press, the process of composition, and the organisation of the workshop. John Man notes that Moxon’s book was the first to explain the hand mould in over two hundred years — no one had bothered to describe it in writing since Gutenberg. It remained the standard reference for a century.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Legacy

The seventeenth century is the century in which printing became recognisably modern. The physical press reached its mature mechanical form. Typefounding became an independent industry. The newspaper, the atlas, the novel, and the children’s book all appeared as new genres. Copyright was defined as an author’s right for the first time. The centre of the trade shifted to the Dutch Republic and remained there for a century. And the first successful arguments for press freedom were made — arguments that would shape the eighteenth-century Enlightenment and the American and French revolutions.

Lorck called the period 1500-1750 the “Glanzperiode und Verfall” — the period of splendour and decline. For the seventeenth century, it was more splendour than decline. The press was still a wooden hand press, but it was now printing newspapers, novels, atlases, and scientific reports for a reading public far larger and more diverse than anything the sixteenth century had known.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]
