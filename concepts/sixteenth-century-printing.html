Sixteenth-Century Printing

The year 1500 is an artificial boundary. The incunable period did not end on 31 December 1500 because anything suddenly changed in the trade. It is a bibliographic convenience, imposed by Johann Saubert’s catalogue and accepted by scholars ever since. But the sixteenth century was genuinely different from the incunable period in almost every dimension of printing: the content of what was printed, the format and price, the organisation of the trade, the technology of typefounding and illustration, and the political relationship between print and authority.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

This page synthesises the major shifts that transformed European printing between 1500 and 1600.

The Reformation and the Vernacular Explosion

The single most transformative event for sixteenth-century printing was the Reformation. In the incunable period, 77% of editions had been in Latin, and 45% of output was religious — mostly liturgical books for priests (missals, breviaries, psalters) and patristic theology for scholars. The Reformation changed all of this.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Luther’s Ninety-Five Theses (1517) were printed in German, not Latin, and they spread through pamphlet networks, not through the established book trade. In the years 1517-1525, Luther published over 300 pamphlets, many in editions of 4,000 copies that sold out in days. Putnam notes that the Reformation “gave employment to the presses of Germany for a generation.” The Catholic Church responded with its own pamphlets, creating a Europe-wide polemical literature that had no precedent in the manuscript age.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

This had lasting structural effects:

  • Vernacular printing became a major market. German, French, English, Italian, and Spanish all grew from small shares to substantial print languages. The reader was no longer necessarily a Latin-literate cleric or scholar.
  • Pamphlets became the dominant format for new ideas. Cheap, short, fast to produce, easy to distribute. The Reformation was, in John Man’s phrase, “the first movement in history to be powered by print.”
  • Censorship became systematic in response. The Index of Prohibited Books (1559), the Roman Inquisition, the Paris parlement, the English Stationers’ Company (1557) — all were attempts to control the flood of vernacular religious printing.^[raw/articles/censorship-of-the-press.md]
  • Vernacular Bibles became the norm. Tyndale’s New Testament (1526, 18,000 copies through six editions), Luther’s German Bible (1534), Coverdale’s English Bible (1535), the Geneva Bible (1560). The printed vernacular Bible, once rare and suspect, became ubiquitous.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The Book Gets Smaller and Cheaper

In the incunable period, most serious books were folios. The Aldine octavo (from 1501) pioneered smaller formats for classical texts, but it was the Elzevir family in the early seventeenth century who perfected the duodecimo — a format small enough to hold in one hand, priced low enough for a student or merchant to afford. Lorck calls the Elzevirs “the heirs of Aldus” in the small-format market.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The format shift was also a price shift. A folio Bible or law commentary might cost several ducats — a year’s wages for a labourer. An Elzevir duodecimo of Virgil or Horace cost a fraction of that. The personal library, once the preserve of princes and monasteries, became accessible to scholars, professionals, and the literate middle class. This expanded the market for printing far beyond what the incunable period had known.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

By the late sixteenth century, the small-format book — octavo, duodecimo, even sextodecimo — had become the standard format for most reading matter. The folio was reserved for reference works, liturgical books, and prestige projects. The “portable book” that Aldus had pioneered became the norm.

Typography Becomes a Profession

In the incunable period, printers cut their own type. The punchcutter was a craftsman within the printing shop. By the mid-sixteenth century, typefounding had become a separate trade.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Claude Garamond (c. 1510-1561) was the first type designer to sell his types to multiple printers rather than working for a single shop. His Roman types, cut for Robert Estienne’s Greek Royal types (commissioned by King Francis I of France), became the standard across Europe. Lorck treats the Estienne family as “the generation that reached the highest typographic honours” alongside the Aldi.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The separation of typefounding from printing was an industrial development: it meant that a printer in Lyon could buy the same typefaces as a printer in Antwerp, which contributed to the standardisation of book design across Europe. It also increased specialisation — a punchcutter could focus on perfecting his letterforms while the printer focused on production and sales.

Other typographic developments included:

  • Italic type became standard for certain genres (poetry, prefaces, annotations).
  • Fraktur became the standard German typeface after Schonsperger’s Theuerdank (1513) and remained so into the twentieth century.
  • Greek types improved dramatically, notably through the Aldine press and the Estienne foundry, enabling the printing of Greek classics and the New Testament in its original language.
  • Hebrew types were developed for scholarly and missionary printing.

Music Printing Becomes Viable

Before 1501, music could only be printed from woodblocks or left blank for hand-rubrication. Ottaviano Petrucci in Venice (1501) developed a method of printing polyphonic music from movable type using three impressions: staves, text, and notes were printed separately, requiring precise registration. This was technically brilliant but slow and expensive.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Later innovators — notably Pierre Haultin in Paris and the Plantin press in Antwerp — developed single-impression music type, which could print staves, notes, and text in one pass. This made music printing commercially viable.

The consequences were significant:

  • The Catholic Church, after the Council of Trent (1545-1563), could standardise liturgical music across Europe through printed graduals and antiphoners.
  • The Reformation could distribute printed hymnbooks (Luther’s hymns, the Genevan Psalter).
  • Secular music could be printed and distributed, creating a market for madrigals, chansons, and instrumental works.

New Genres

The sixteenth century created new categories of printed work that had no real manuscript precedent.

Atlases. The concept of a uniform-format collection of maps was invented by Abraham Ortelius (1570) and named by Gerard Mercator (1595). The atlas was a new kind of book — a reference work designed to be consulted, not read linearly. It required the integration of copperplate illustration with letterpress text, pushing the technical boundaries of both.^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

Navigation manuals and sea atlases. The practical needs of European maritime expansion created a new genre of printed works for navigators: pilot books, navigation manuals, island books, and sea atlases, written in the vernacular and illustrated with coastal profiles, compass bearings, and sailing directions.^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]^[raw/articles/blaeu-review-nellen.md]

Scientific and medical books with systematic illustration. The landmark work was Vesalius’s De humani corporis fabrica (Basel: Oporinus, 1543), which was impossible in manuscript: its copperplate anatomical plates required a level of precision and standardisation that only print could provide. Vesalius had spent years in Venice commissioning woodcuts from Jan van Calcar, a pupil of Titian, to illustrate human anatomy “with great talent.” He was so concerned about print quality that he sent his own pressman from the famous Bomberg printing shop in Venice to Basel to supervise production. The resulting work — with woodcuts so fine they were mistaken for Titian originals — demonstrated the value of the woodcut “for scientific purpose and the easier understanding of a work.”^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Copernicus’s De revolutionibus orbium coelestium (Nuremberg: Johannes Petreius, 1543) was the other pillar of the scientific revolution in print, its diagrams and tables integrated with the text. The 1617 Amsterdam Astronomia instaurata shows the afterlife of that print event: Mulerius advertises the edition as a restoration of a text damaged by earlier printers and numerical corruption, with notes, tables, errata, and a format suited to Blaeu’s type and companion Frisian tables. John Man notes that printing allowed specialists “to agree on their agendas and feed off each other” for the first time.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]^[raw/articles/copernicus-astronomia-instaurata.md]

Between these two landmarks, the astronomer Regiomontanus (Johann Muller, 1436-1476) was the first to demonstrate how a scholar could use the press for original scientific work. He set up both an observatory and a printing press in Nuremberg in the 1470s and published his own detailed observations of the moon and planets. In 1474 he published a new method for calculating longitude by measuring lunar distance — a system that proved too complex for practical use at sea, but whose observational tradition fed directly into Copernicus’s work seventy years later.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Architectural treatises were another new genre made possible by systematic printed illustration. Vitruvius’s De architectura — the only surviving architectural treatise from classical antiquity — could now be reproduced in all major languages, and architects armed with the printed works of his Renaissance disciples (Vignola, Palladio) could recreate classical forms in building projects across Europe.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Herbals and natural history also demanded accurate plant drawings that only print could standardise and distribute, creating a market for illustrated botanical works that became one of the most commercially successful scientific genres of the sixteenth century.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Travel and exploration narratives. Ramusio’s Navigationi et Viaggi (1550-1559) collected accounts of voyages to the Americas, Asia, and Africa in a single printed work. Hakluyt’s Principal Navigations (1589) did the same for English exploration. These collections had no manuscript equivalent — they were compilations of materials that could only be gathered, edited, and distributed through the print network.^[raw/articles/karrow-mapmakers-16th-century.md]

The learned journal. The Journal des scavans (1665) and the Philosophical Transactions (1665) were the culmination of a sixteenth-century trend toward periodic scholarly communication, which had begun with the letter-books of humanist scholars and the regular newsletters of the book fairs.

The vernacular novel. Amadis de Gaula (1508), Lazarillo de Tormes (1554), Montaigne’s Essais (1580). These were new forms that print enabled — long-form vernacular prose for a lay readership.

The emblem book. Andrea Alciato’s Emblemata (1531) created a new genre combining image, motto, and epigram. Emblem books became one of the most popular genres of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, endlessly reprinted, translated, and imitated.

Illustration Becomes Systematic

In the incunable period, illustration was optional and expensive. By the late sixteenth century, many genres demanded illustration: herbals (which required accurate plant drawings), anatomical works, travel books, atlases, architectural treatises, and emblem books.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The woodcut remained the workhorse technique for most illustrated books. But copperplate engraving, pioneered for maps by Sweynheym (1478), became standard for high-end scientific and cartographic work. The copperplate press — a rolling press separate from the screw press used for text — became a standard piece of equipment in larger printing houses. Lorck notes that the Blaeu firm in Amsterdam had nine presses “of improved construction, named after the nine muses,” and that their main activity was “the publication of astronomical and map works.”^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The cost of illustration was now a budgeted line item, not an exceptional expense. Plantin’s Polyglot Bible (1568-1572) included engraved plates, ornamental initials, and complex typographic layouts across eight volumes in five languages. The Nuremberg Chronicle’s 1,800 woodcuts (1493) had been exceptional; by 1600, a work of that scale of illustration was merely ambitious.

Censorship Becomes Systematic

The incunable period had seen sporadic censorship — Berthold of Mainz’s 1486 edict against vernacular liturgy, the occasional burning of heretical works. The sixteenth century made censorship systematic.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The key institutions:

  • The Index of Prohibited Books (first Roman Index 1559, Tridentine Index 1564) listing approximately 1,000 titles. Expanded steadily through the century.
  • The Venetian privilege system, which granted exclusive printing rights for periods of ten to twenty-five years, but also gave the state a mechanism for approving books before publication.
  • The Paris parlement, which controlled printing in France through a system of licensing and inspection. Chevillier documents how the University of Paris exercised authority over the book trade.
  • The English Stationers’ Company (1557), which gave the Crown effective control over all printing in England.
  • The Spanish Inquisition, which maintained its own Index and conducted regular inspections of bookshops and libraries.^[raw/articles/censorship-of-the-press.md]

Censorship and printing grew up together. The technology that made the Reformation possible also made the Index necessary. Every advance in the speed or reach of the press provoked a corresponding effort to control it. This dialectic between print freedom and print control is a defining feature of sixteenth-century printing that had no real equivalent in the incunable period.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Centre of Gravity Shifts North

In 1500, the centre of European printing was Venice and the German cities. By 1600, it had shifted to the Low Countries. This reflected the broader shift of European trade from the Mediterranean to the Atlantic.

Antwerp became the leading printing city of the mid-sixteenth century. Christopher Plantin (c. 1520-1589) built the largest printing and publishing firm Europe had seen, with multiple presses, a typefoundry, and an international network of agents and correspondents. His Polyglot Bible (1568-1572), commissioned by Philip II of Spain, was the most ambitious scholarly printing project of the century.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Amsterdam succeeded Antwerp after the Spanish sack of 1576 and the fall of Antwerp to Spanish forces in 1585. The Blaeu family and the Elzevir family made Amsterdam the printing capital of the seventeenth century. Blaeu’s Atlas Maior (1662-1665), in eleven volumes, was the largest and most expensive printed book of its century. The Elzevirs’ duodecimo classics set the standard for affordable scholarly publishing.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]^[raw/articles/van-netten-blaeu-review.md]

Leiden became a centre of academic printing through the university and the Elzevir firm. Geneva became a centre for French-language Reformed printing. Frankfurt remained the centre of the book fair system.

This northern shift was not just geographic. It represented a change in the nature of the trade: from the artisanal, scholar-printer model of the incunable period (Gutenberg, Aldus, Froben) to the industrial, capital-intensive model of the seventeenth century (Plantin, Blaeu, Elzevir). The printing firm had become a business that required sustained investment, international distribution, and professional management.

The Book Trade Matures

In the fifteenth century, printing was a risky trade dominated by individuals — Gutenberg needing Fust’s capital, Sweynheym and Pannartz going bankrupt in Rome, Koberger operating more like a merchant than a publisher. By 1600, the book trade had institutional structures:^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

  • Regular book fairs at Frankfurt (established by 1500 as the centre of the trade) and Leipzig (beginning to rival Frankfurt in the late sixteenth century). The Frankfurt fair catalogue (first published 1564) listed all new books, creating a Europe-wide awareness of what was being published.
  • Guilds and companies regulating the trade: the Stationers’ Company in London, the guilds of Frankfurt and Leipzig, the printers’ guilds in Paris and Lyon.
  • International distribution networks maintained by the largest firms. Plantin had correspondents across Europe; Koberger had branches in a dozen cities; the Giunti firm had offices in Venice, Florence, and Lyon.
  • The exchange of books between publishers as a standard practice, allowing a publisher in Basel to sell Parisian books without maintaining a Paris office.
  • The beginnings of copyright through the privilege system. Extended from single-city privileges to empire-wide protections, and from ten-year to twenty-five-year terms.^[raw/articles/literary-property-and-copyright.md]

By 1600, printing was no longer a new technology finding its feet. It was a mature industry with established business practices, professional specialisation, international trade networks, and regulatory frameworks. The essential structure of the European book trade would remain stable from 1600 to the industrialisation of printing in the nineteenth century.^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Legacy

The sixteenth century was the period in which printing became what it would remain for the next 300 years. The incunable period invented the technology and proved it could work. The sixteenth century made it an industry. It created:

  • The genres that printing would sustain for centuries (the novel, the learned journal, the atlas, the travel narrative, the polemical pamphlet, the hymnbook)
  • The division of labour (typefounder, printer, publisher, bookseller) that would characterise the trade until the Industrial Revolution
  • The relationship between print and authority (censorship, privilege, licensing) that defined the politics of publishing
  • The geography of the trade, which shifted from the Mediterranean to the North Sea and then remained remarkably stable until the rise of the American book trade in the nineteenth century
  • The economic structure of the book as a product: high fixed costs, variable scale, tiered pricing, international distribution

Lorck calls the period 1500-1750 the “Glanzperiode und Verfall” of printing — the period of splendour and decline. The splendour came first, in the sixteenth century, when the press was still new enough to be revolutionary but established enough to be an industry.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]
