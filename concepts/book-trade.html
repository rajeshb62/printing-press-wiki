Book Trade

The early printed book trade joined workshop production to long-distance commerce. Printers needed readers, paper, capital, transport, agents, and places where books could be displayed and sold. The most successful printing centers were therefore not only technically skilled cities but commercial nodes: mainz, Cologne, Basel, Nuremberg, Paris, Lyon, venice, Bruges, and Westminster. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

From Scriptorium to Market

Manuscript books were often made for a known patron or institution. Printed books were made in multiple copies for a market that had to be anticipated. This transformed the book from a commissioned object into stock: an inventory of saleable copies moving through shops, fairs, agents, universities, monasteries, courts, and urban readers. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Distribution Networks

The spread of printing followed trade routes because books had to move. Rhine routes, Alpine crossings, Italian maritime commerce, French fairs, and university towns all helped connect printers to buyers. The same networks that carried cloth, paper, metal, spices, and letters could carry printed sheets and bound books. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Book fairs and commercial cities mattered because they concentrated buyers and intermediaries. Frankfurt and Lyon became especially important for later book distribution, while Venice’s maritime and paper networks made it the leading fifteenth-century printing capital. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Markets and Genres

The early book trade served overlapping markets: clerical, liturgical, legal, university, humanist, school, devotional, and vernacular. Latin remained dominant because it crossed regional boundaries and served universities, law, theology, and church institutions. Vernacular printing grew where urban readers, courts, reform movements, or national literary traditions created demand. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Specialization

Different cities developed different strengths. Venice became strong in humanist, classical, typographic, and commercial publishing; Paris in university and theological production; Lyon in trade-linked publishing; Nuremberg in large-scale illustrated and commercial production; Westminster in English vernacular works through william-caxton. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Significance

The book trade turned movable-type from an invention into an infrastructure. Without distribution, capital, and market demand, a press could only produce local curiosities. With them, printing became a Europe-wide industry by 1500. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Agent Systems and Distribution Networks of the Major Publishing Houses

The European book trade of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries was not carried by isolated printers but by organized publishing houses that built agent networks, exploited international fairs, and developed sophisticated distribution systems. The three most instructive examples are the Kobergers of Nuremberg, the House of Plantin in Antwerp, and the Elzevirs of Leyden and Amsterdam. The following sections are drawn from George Haven Putnam’s Books and their Makers during the Middle Ages, Vol. II (1896). ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The Kobergers: Nuremberg’s Publishing Empire (1440-1540)

Anthoni Koberger (c. 1440-1513) was the leading publisher of his generation. At the height of his activity, around 1509, he employed about twenty-four presses with over a hundred workmen, including compositors, pressmen, binders, correctors, illuminators, and designers — a fully organized and disciplined book-manufacturing concern within half a century of Gutenberg’s invention. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Koberger did not limit himself to his own presses. He gave contracts for important works to printers in other cities: Grüninger of Strasburg printed Pirckheimer’s translation of Ptolemy’s Geography for him, and Amerbach of Basel (who began as a corrector with Koberger) printed multiple works, including the seven-volume Hugo Bible of 1504, the most costly of Koberger’s undertakings. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Agent network. Koberger’s correspondence reveals agents or active representatives not only in the other book-centers of the empire — Frankfurt, Leipzig, Vienna, Basel, Strasburg, and Cologne — but in distant cities such as Paris, Buda-Pesth, Warsaw, Venice, Florence, Rome, Antwerp, Bruges, and Leyden. In organizing connections and distribution machinery across the Continent, Koberger had a decisive advantage over his great contemporary Aldus Manutius, who found lasting difficulty in maintaining satisfactory arrangements for distributing his books north of the Alps. Aldus depended mainly on direct correspondence with individual scholars; Koberger secured larger results by using the services of the book-trade, whose organization in Germany and France was then taking shape. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Role as bookseller and cataloguer. Koberger was a bookseller as well as a publisher and printer, selling both to the trade and at retail. He was the first bookseller in Germany, and possibly in Europe, to issue a classified catalogue of current publications (a Sortiments-Catalog). Kapp describes his shop as the best-equipped repository for standard literature in Germany. Possessing knowledge of all divisions of book-making and book-selling, Koberger became the recognized leader of the German book-trade. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Cooperative publishing. Koberger’s cooperation with Amerbach in Basel for the Hugo Bible demonstrates an early model of shared investment: the plan originated with Koberger and the larger portion of the capital was supplied by him, while the printing was done in Basel. The undertaking ultimately proved a commercial mistake, the sales failing to return the outlay. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Decline. After Anthoni’s death in 1513, the concern continued under his nephew Johannes and his sons Anthoni and Melchior until 1540, but the energy and initiative departed. Only fifty-three works were added in the final twenty-seven years. The capital was impaired by withdrawals to heirs, several of whom returned to the old family trade of gold- and silver-working. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The House of Plantin: Antwerp’s International Enterprise (1555-1867)

Christophe Plantin (1514-1589) established his press in Antwerp in 1555 and built it into the longest-lived publishing house in history, continuing under his descendants until 1867, when the city of Antwerp purchased the premises to create the Plantin Museum. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Scale and fair consignments. Within two years of founding, Plantin’s consignment to the Frankfurt Fair in 1557 comprised 1,200 volumes plus a large assortment of copper-plate prints. By 1579, he consigned 5,212 copies of sixty-seven works to the same fair. In 1570 alone, he sent books worth 19,000 florins to his Paris agent. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Agent network. Plantin maintained a permanent agent in Paris; Sonnius managed the Paris branch, receiving a forty per cent commission. For Spain, Perez of Salamanca acted as his agent, with a branch office first run for the Antwerp concern and later as an independent house; annual sales through Salamanca ranged from 5,000 to 15,000 florins. In 1579, Plantin planned a London branch, but the war prevented it; his sales to one London correspondent in 1568 had amounted to 4,400 florins. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Frankfurt Fair as clearing house. Plantin’s business depended heavily on the semi-annual Frankfurt Fair, through which he reached the book-trade of Germany and beyond. The fair served as a clearing house for publishers across Europe, and Plantin used it both to market his own productions and to acquire stock for his Antwerp shop. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The Polyglot Bible and royal patronage. Plantin’s greatest undertaking was the Biblia Polyglotta (the “Bible Royale”), edited by Arias Montanus, in eight volumes. King Philip II promised a subvention of 21,000 florins to be repaid in copies. The work required privileges from the Pope, the King, and the Theological Faculty of Louvain. After Pope Pius V blocked publication, the undertaking stalled for years, leaving Plantin in financial difficulty. The first edition of 1,213 copies sold poorly; five years later many copies remained unsold. Philip II never completed payment of the promised subsidy. Despite its financial failure, the Polyglot Bible remains one of the greatest monuments of sixteenth-century publishing. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Leyden connection. During the crisis of the “Spanish Fury” (1576), when Antwerp was sacked and Plantin’s presses dwindled from twenty-five to one, he fled to Leyden at the invitation of the historian Lipsius. There he purchased the press of Louis Elzevir and put Elzevir in charge of his Leyden operations. Plantin received the appointment as Printer to the University of Leyden in 1584 (with an annual stipend of 200 florins), but returned to Antwerp in 1585, transferring the Leyden press to his son-in-law Raphelengius. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Accounting and organization. Plantin’s account books (still preserved) record payments to authors, editors, and correctors. His permanent staff included scholarly correctors: Ghisebrecht, with a knowledge of Latin and Greek, received board and lodging plus sixty florins yearly; Van der Eynde was paid 150-210 florins yearly. Plantin’s catalogue of 1566 included works in Latin, Greek, Hebrew, French, German, Spanish, Italian, Flemish, Dutch, and English, requiring a multilingual staff of editors and compositors. He was the first medieval publisher to deliberately plan books for what would now be called a popular circulation. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Censorship and the proto-typographer office. In 1570, Philip II appointed Plantin as proto-typographer (supervisor of printing for the Netherlands), a post that brought no salary but freed him from quartering soldiers and helped secure approvals for his own publications. Plantin thereafter used the title “Printer to the King.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The Elzevirs: A Family Federation of Book Agencies (1587-1688)

The Elzevirs of Leyden and Amsterdam created a family-based distribution network that, for nearly a century, held a leading place in European publishing. The principal authority on their history is the monograph of Alphonse Willems (Brussels, 1880). ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Founding and university connection. Louis Elzevir (c. 1540-1617), a Protestant binder from Louvain, fled to Leyden in 1580 and set up as a bookbinder and bookseller. He never mastered printing himself — the typographical work of his early publications was done under contract — but he secured an official appointment as bookseller and publisher to the University of Leyden, with a shop constructed within the University court. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

International correspondence and fair participation. Louis Elzevir’s correspondence throughout France, Germany, and Italy established Leyden’s connections with the book-producing centers of Europe. For most of his lifetime, Louis was the only publisher and bookseller in Holland with such foreign relations. His name first appears in 1595 in the list of publishers at the Frankfurt Fair, and from 1602 he made regular annual sojourns in Paris. The Frankfurt Fair remained central: the Elzevirs instituted a permanent depot there in 1595, and a representative attended every single semi-annual fair during the Thirty Years’ War (1618-1648) despite the dangers of travel. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The family agency system. Within fifteen years of Louis’s death (1617), the firm had direct representation in nearly every book-selling center of Europe. Many agencies were placed in the charge of family members — it became the routine for younger Elzevirs to receive their business training through foreign postings. The large family circle (eleven Elzevirs worked as typographers and publishers over three generations) facilitated a federation of book-selling agencies whose purpose was to make Elzevir publications known throughout Europe. Branch houses or agencies existed in The Hague (from 1590), Utrecht, Amsterdam (from 1638), and Copenhagen (from 1632). ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Copenhagen branch and northern expansion. The Copenhagen branch, established in 1632 by Louis the second, proved a valuable outlet. A separate catalogue for the Copenhagen shop was printed in 1642. In 1650, Daniel Elzevir visited Sweden and Denmark with the author Nicholas Heinsius. Queen Christina of Sweden invited Daniel to establish a printing concern in Stockholm, but the proposal was declined. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Paris connections and restrictions. Louis Elzevir established relations with Paris as early as 1602, but French book-trade regulations restricted foreign dealers: they could visit only once a year, stay a maximum of three weeks, and accept orders only from members of the Paris Booksellers’ Guild. Despite these restrictions, Bonaventure Elzevir made regular annual sojourns in Paris after 1624, collecting material from French authors and scholars. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Small-format market revolution. Following Aldus’s example, the Elzevirs initiated series of Latin classics in small, convenient formats (twelvemo and sixteenmo) at prices far below standard folio and quarto editions. A Virgil of 1636, a Pliny of 1640, or a Cicero of 1642 (each about 500 pages) sold for one florin (approximately 43 cents). Quintus Curtius (1633) cost sixteen sous. These well-edited, low-priced editions won the appreciation of scholars and students across Europe. Matthew Berneggerus, professor of Strasburg, called the Elzevirs “unquestionably the first typographers of the world.” Some critics, like the scholar Erycius Puteanus, objected that great authors deserved “dignified” folio formats, but the market favored the new, economical forms. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

English relations. The Elzevirs had important relations with England, buying and selling books and publishing works by English authors. Some English publishers commissioned Elzevir editions specifically to benefit from the superior typography, which could not be matched in England at the time. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Unauthorized publications and disguised imprints. The Elzevirs occasionally issued works without imprint or under false place names. For French Jansenist writings they used Lugduni (Lyons) instead of Lugduni Batavorum (Leyden). Pseudonyms included Jouxte, Jean Sambix, and Nic Schouten of Cologne. Works in this category include the Defensio Regia of Saumaise, Milton’s Defensio Populi Anglicani, and Selden’s Mare Clausum. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

University press arrangements. The agreement with Isaac Elzevir specified that one press (and during certain seasons, two) was held at the disposition of the University faculty for academic materials. Larger works by professors were passed by the curators and senate for publication through the University Press at the charge of the University treasury. The printer was obligated to secure correctors competent in any required language. One copy of every work was deposited in the University library. The printing offices stood on University-owned land but the buildings were paid for by the Elzevirs. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Advantage of Dutch censorship freedom. During the seventeenth century, the press of the Dutch Republic was more untrammeled than that of any state in Europe. The Elzevirs carried on their work under comparatively slight government restrictions, with few references to church censorship. This relative freedom was a major competitive advantage that allowed the House to publish authors and works that would have been impossible or dangerous to produce under Catholic censorship regimes. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Typography and the Van Dyck font. The exquisite Elzevir fonts were designed and engraved by Christophe Van Dyck, working between 1630 and 1640. Typographical experts consider them to have surpassed anything previously produced in Europe in beauty of proportion, delicacy of outline, and grace of general effect. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

End of the House. The Leyden concern continued from 1621 to 1712 (ninety-one years), but declined under the incapable Abraham Elzevir, who died in 1712. The plant was sold at auction in 1713. The Amsterdam branch wound up after Daniel’s death in 1680; its last publication bore the imprint of Anna, Daniel’s widow, in 1680. The emblem of the Elzevirs in Amsterdam was an elm. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The Frankfurt and Leipzig Fairs as Distribution Hubs

The Frankfurt Fair was the central nervous system of the European book trade from the mid-fifteenth through the seventeenth century. Before the age of postal services, catalogues, or regular trade routes, the semi-annual fairs at Frankfurt (Lent and Michaelmas) concentrated buyers, sellers, and stock from across the continent in a single marketplace for a few weeks each year.

Origins in the Manuscript Trade

Frankfurt was already a center for the manuscript trade before printing existed. Putnam records that books first appeared in the schedules of goods sold at the fairs of Frankfurt and Nordlingen in the early fifteenth century. Between 1445 and 1450, an important item of the manuscript trade at the Frankfurt Fair was fortune-telling books and illustrated almanacs. The earliest record of printed books being sold at the fair dates from 1480, and in 1485 the printer Peter Schoeffer was admitted as a citizen of Frankfurt, signalling the city’s growing importance to the print trade. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Printed Catalogue Revolution

A crucial innovation came in 1564, when George Willer, a bookseller of Augsburg, printed the first general catalogue of books offered at the Frankfurt Fair. This catalogue — listing newly published works available at the fair — transformed the European book trade by making it possible for buyers who could not attend the fair to order books directly. After 1595, the semi-annual fair catalogues always contained a substantial listing of Elzevir publications, and the catalogues became standard reference tools for scholars and booksellers across Europe. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

How the Fair Worked

The fair was the primary mechanism for international trade in printed books. A publisher like Plantin would consign hundreds or thousands of volumes to each fair:

  • 1557: Plantin’s consignment comprised 1,200 volumes plus a large assortment of copper-plate prints
  • 1579: He consigned 5,212 copies of sixty-seven works ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Publishers and their agents travelled from across Europe to present their stock, settle accounts with distant correspondents, acquire works from other publishers for resale, and gather intelligence on what competitors were printing. The fair was also the main clearing house for inter-publisher credit. The Elzevirs maintained a permanent depot there from 1595, and a representative “attended every single semi-annual fair during the Thirty Years’ War (1618-1648) despite the dangers of travel.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The fair’s importance was such that when wars blocked the Alpine passes and Venetian books could not reach Frankfurt in 1510, a professor lamented: “When the Fair was opened, there was not a single volume from Italy. We shall be able this spring to do nothing in our lectures.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

Censorship and Control

The fair also served as a point of control. When Duke George of Saxony blocked Protestant books from the Leipzig book trade, he forbade their “transportation to the Frankfort Fair” as part of his anti-Reformation campaign. The fair’s openness was both its strength and its vulnerability: authorities could attempt to intercept shipments, but the sheer volume of traffic made thorough inspection impossible. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Shift to Leipzig

From the mid-seventeenth century, Frankfurt’s importance declined. The reasons included the devastation of the Frankfurt region during the Thirty Years’ War, the increasing restriction of press freedom in imperial cities under Catholic influence, and Leipzig’s more favourable position as a crossroads of northern and eastern European trade. By 1700, “the book-trade business had transferred entirely to Leipzig, which became the best-organized book-trade in Europe.” The Leipzig booksellers’ association, organised in 1525, remains effective to this day. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Significance

The Frankfurt Fair system solved the fundamental distribution problem of early printing: how to connect a printer in Venice or Antwerp with a buyer in Prague or Oxford. It created a regular rhythm of production and exchange, standardised pricing through catalogue listings, and built the trust relationships that allowed credit-based trade across long distances. Without the fair, the international book trade of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries would have been impossible.

Significance

The book trade turned movable-type from an invention into an infrastructure. Without distribution, capital, and market demand, a press could only produce local curiosities. With them, printing became a Europe-wide industry by 1500. The houses of Koberger, Plantin, and Elzevir each built, in their century, an integrated system of agents, fairs, and international contacts that transformed the book from a local craft product into a commodity traded across the entire continent. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The Aldine Distribution System

The Aldine press under Aldus Manutius developed one of the most sophisticated distribution networks of the early sixteenth century. Venice’s maritime and overland trade routes gave Aldus access to markets across the Mediterranean and through the Alpine passes into Germany, France, and the Low Countries. But distribution remained a persistent problem: getting sufficient copies of each edition into the right markets required agents, fairs, and careful management of risk. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Aldus faced intense competition from Lyons, where printers rapidly pirated his editions, producing cheap versions that reached the market before his own could be distributed. He complained through his prefaces about spies who “sniff at every new publication” and pirates who “steal the text before the ink is dry.” The Lyons piracy problem was so severe that it forced Aldus to accelerate production and seek stronger privilege protections from both Venice and Rome. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Distribution relied on a patchwork of agents and merchants. Some were salaried agents circulating in university towns with books for sale. Others were professors who acted as informal distributors. The Frankfurt Book Fair was the central clearing-house for international trade, where Aldus’s agents would meet buyers from across Europe, take orders, and arrange shipments. Gerolamo Strozzi’s agents in Rome, Siena, and elsewhere distributed books through the relatively sophisticated methods of international merchant banking. Aldus himself kept stock worth 500 ducats in Pavia alone, with agents in most major Italian cities and across the Alps. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The practical difficulties were immense. Printers needed to judge demand months in advance, print sufficient copies to make the edition profitable, then distribute those copies before pirates could undercut them. A single shipwreck or bandit attack could destroy an entire season’s stock. The development of the octavo format (small, portable books) was partly a response to these distribution challenges: smaller books were cheaper to transport, easier to hide from customs, and reached a broader market of travelling scholars and students. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]
