Early Typefaces

The history of typefaces in the first fifty years of printing (c. 1450—1500) is the story of how printers moved from deliberately imitating manuscript hands to developing distinctive typographic forms that would shape book design for centuries. Every printed letter began as a steel punch, hand-engraved in reverse by a skilled punchcutter, struck into a copper matrix, and cast in the adjustable hand mold — a process so laborious that a full font of type could take a year to produce.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] The choices printers made about letterform design were at once aesthetic, technical, and commercial: a successful typeface had to please readers accustomed to scribal books, work reliably on the press, and distinguish its printer’s output in a rapidly expanding market.

Textura (Gothic Blackletter)

The first typeface ever printed was a Textura — the formal blackletter script of German liturgical manuscripts, dense and angular, with strokes of nearly uniform thickness and a texture so compact that fifteenth-century scribes called it textura (“woven” in Latin).^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Gutenberg’s Bible Type

When johann-gutenberg began work on the gutenberg-bible around 1450, he made a deliberate decision to make his printed book look as much like a manuscript as possible. The market — churches, princes, monastic libraries — would only accept a printed Bible that matched or exceeded the beauty of scribal copies. Gutenberg modelled his type on the formal liturgical hand used in the finest Mainz manuscripts.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The result was a typeface of extraordinary complexity. Gutenberg’s font contained approximately 290 distinct characters — not just the 24 letters of the Latin alphabet in upper and lower case, but multiple variant forms of the same letter (ten different forms of ‘a’ alone), 83 ligatures (tied letters like ‘fi’, ‘fl’, ‘ff’), and numerous abbreviations and contractions.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] These abbreviations were crucial: they mimicked the scribal practice of saving space and parchment, and also helped Gutenberg achieve justified columns — the even left and right margins that manuscript readers expected.

The type was cast in a size equivalent to approximately 20-point modern type, with a pronounced black colour on the page. Hairlines were few; the strokes were heavy, the counters (interior spaces of letters like ‘o’ and ‘e’) small. De Vinne describes the forbidding appearance of early printed pages as “blackness and obscurity,” noting that the Textura was designed to be seen as a woven texture of dark ink on light paper rather than read as discrete letters.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Diffused Gothic

After Gutenberg, early German printers developed what is sometimes called Diffused Gothic — a slightly more open variant of Textura with regional variations across mainz, Strasbourg, Cologne, Basel, Augsburg, and Nuremberg. These types retained the basic blackletter structure but varied in weight, width, and the sharpness of their angular breaks. The first generation of printers who spread from Mainz after the 1462 sack of the city carried their type styles with them, creating a European family of Gothic types that persisted in German-speaking lands for centuries.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Roman Type

The most significant typographic development of the fifteenth century was the creation of Roman type — a letterform based not on Gothic manuscript hands but on the clear, rounded scripts of Italian humanist scribes, themselves inspired by Carolingian minuscule and the monumental capitals of Roman inscriptions.

Sweynheim and Pannartz at Subiaco (1465)

The first printers to break with the Gothic tradition were Conrad Sweynheim and Arnold Pannartz, two German clerics who set up a press at the Benedictine monastery of Subiaco, east of Rome, in 1465. Their first book — a edition of Lactantius’s Divinae Institutiones — used a typeface that was neither purely Gothic nor fully Roman: a transitional form that blended the rounded humanist letter with certain Gothic features. It is often called the Subiaco type, and it represents the first deliberate attempt to create a printed letterform inspired by Italian rather than German manuscript models.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

When Sweynheim and Pannartz moved to Rome itself in 1467, they abandoned the Subiaco type in favour of a purer Roman face, closer to the humanist handwriting of the papal chancery. Their Roman editions of classical authors — Cicero, Virgil, Livy — established a market for books printed in the new style.

Nicolas Jenson’s Roman (1470)

The perfection of Roman type was the achievement of Nicolas Jenson, a French engraver and printer who had been sent by King Charles VII to learn the art of printing in Mainz. Whether Jenson ever reached Gutenberg’s workshop is unclear, but by 1470 he had established himself in Venice and produced his masterpiece: the Roman type used in his edition of Eusebius’s De Praeparatione Evangelica.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Lowry recounts that Jenson was trained in the French royal mint, which would explain much about his metallurgical skill and his workshop’s value.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] Contemporaries considered him extraordinarily wealthy: the diarist Marin Sanudo called him “richissimo,” and he died in 1480 as a papal count palatine and the head of an international company.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] In reality, Jenson’s will disposed of approximately 4,000 ducats — a respectable fortune, but a mere tenth of what a single spice-dealer might make from one year’s shortage, and nothing beside the hundreds of thousands left by Venice’s merchant-noblemen.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Jenson’s workshop contents were valued at the very high figure of 500 ducats, specifically excluding the steel punches which he bequeathed separately to one of his partners.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] The punches themselves may also have been used to strike matrices for re-sale to other printers: Andrea Torresani claimed to have worked with Jenson’s types, and some thirty founts similar to his in design can be traced in various Italian centres.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] Jenson stands as the most striking example of the pioneer-printer in full control of the means of production.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Jenson’s Roman type is one of the most admired typefaces in the entire history of typography. It combined:

  • Evenness of colour: The weight of the strokes was perfectly balanced across all characters, giving the page a uniform grey texture that was far more legible than the dense black of Textura.
  • Clean serifs: Sharp, horizontal serifs derived from Roman monumental inscription capitals, providing a clear baseline.
  • Generous proportions: Open counters and generous spacing made each letter distinct without sacrificing the colour of the page.
  • Harmonious letterfit: Adjacent letters sat together without awkward gaps or collisions.

De Vinne writes that Jenson’s Roman “supplanted” the Black letter in Italy, France, Spain, and eventually England.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] The type was so successful that it was revived by William Morris in the nineteenth century for the Kelmscott Press, and inspired the Centaur typeface designed by Bruce Rogers in 1914. Jenson’s original roman remains a benchmark of type design to this day.

It was Jenson’s type that established the convention that Latin and vernacular texts would be printed in Roman letters, while German-language works continued in blackletter — a typographic divide that persisted well into the twentieth century.

The Gothic/Roman Tension in Venice

The emergence of Roman type in Italy was not merely a technical or aesthetic development but part of a profound cultural movement — the reform of writing that lay at the heart of Renaissance humanism. Lowry traces this directly to the humanist script revolution of the early fifteenth century.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

As Petrarch and his followers searched for lost Latin classics about a century before Aldus’ birth, they came across manuscripts written in the clear minuscule script of the ninth century (Carolingian minuscule). Since they knew no earlier letterforms, they mistakenly concluded they had rediscovered the truly classical or “Roman” style.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] They adopted this Carolingian model in their own copies. Developed by the early fifteenth-century humanists of the Florentine circle — notably Poggio Bracciolini and Niccolo Niccoli — a clear, rounded hand had by 1430 become the hallmark of the committed classical antiquarian, “pitting what he imagined to be a genuinely Latin script against the angular and ornamental letters of the later Middle Ages which he connected, again quite erroneously, with the ‘Gothic’ destroyers of his beloved Rome.”^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

This fashion passed from Poggio and Niccoli, during the time of Pope Eugenius IV, to the scribes of the papal chancellery, and from them to Sweynheim and Pannartz in Rome, and to Windelin of Speyer and Jenson in Venice.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] In the hands of the first printers of Italy, a lower case modelled on the neo-Carolingian script of the humanist pioneers became the accepted medium for Latin literary texts. When Jenson followed a “humanistic, ‘Roman’ pattern” for his types, he was consciously addressing the students of classical literature who formed his market — just as Caxton chose an ornate Gothic for his Anglo-Burgundian readers of courtly romance.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Simultaneously, a parallel movement affected the design of capital letters. The antiquarian craze revealed not only manuscripts but also monuments, many of them truly “antique.” While Poggio and Niccoli copied Carolingian script, artists such as Donatello and Ghiberti decorated their works with inscriptions based on the square-cut capitals of imperial Rome.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] By the third quarter of the century, Felice Feliciano and Leon Battista Alberti were trying to reduce the formation of Latin capitals to an intellectual system based on mathematical laws of proportion. Around 1470 their ideas were taken up by the Franciscan mathematician Luca Pacioli. All saw the correct formation of letters as part of the cosmic mystery of proportion, “the key to understanding each and every science, known to antiquity but lost during the barbarous centuries that came between that happy age and their own.”^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

This fusion of real and imagined Roman models — the Carolingian minuscule mistaken for ancient Roman script, the monumental capitals recovered from archaeological remains, and the mathematical rationalization of letterform proportions — gave Roman type its ideological charge. In Venice, where German-trained printers (John of Speyer, Windelin, Jenson himself) brought Gothic types but found a market that demanded the humanist Roman, the typographic tension between Gothic and Roman was also a tension between northern and southern Europe, between medieval and Renaissance sensibility, and ultimately between two competing visions of what a printed book should be.

Italic Type

The third major typeface innovation of the incunable period was italic type, created by the punchcutter Francesco Griffo for the Venetian publisher Aldus Manutius around 1500.

Aldus Manutius and Francesco Griffo (1500)

Aldus Manutius had founded his Aldine Press in Venice in 1494 with the mission of producing affordable editions of Greek and Latin classics. For his series of pocket-sized octavo classics (the Libri Portatiles), he needed a compact typeface that would save space while remaining legible at small sizes.

Griffo cut the first italic type for Aldus’s 1500 edition of Virgil’s Opera. The letterforms were based on the cursive humanist script (cancelleresca corsiva) used in the papal chancery — a flowing, slightly slanted hand that could be written rapidly.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] Key features included:

  • Slanted letters: The first typeface with a consistent rightward slant, distinguishing it from the upright Roman.
  • Cursive forms: Letters like ‘a’, ‘e’, and ‘g’ took cursive shapes that echoed handwriting.
  • Compact width: Characters were narrower than their Roman equivalents, allowing more text per line.
  • No capitals at first: The first italic fonts had no capital letters — they were used with Roman capitals, a convention that persisted for decades.

Aldus patented the italic type in Venice, and it quickly spread across Europe. The term “italic” derives from “Italian” (italic in Latin), reflecting its origin. It was initially used not for emphasis (as modern italics are) but for complete books — Aldus’s entire Virgil was printed in italic. Only in the sixteenth century did italic become associated with highlighting, quotation, and secondary text.

Griffo’s italic for Aldus was so highly regarded that it was revived in the twentieth century as the basis for the Monotype Bembo italic and the Blado italic.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Lowry’s Account of Griffo and the Italic

Lowry devotes substantial attention to Francesco Griffo (also known as Francesco da Bologna), calling him “arguably the most important single figure in the establishment of the Aldine company” and “a faceless genius.”^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] His identity remains uncertain: he was once thought to have been the goldsmith Raibolini, but a more probable candidate is the “Franciscus Griffus de Bononia incisor Litterarum stampae” mentioned in a Perugian notarial act of 1512.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Only three fixed points document Griffo’s connection with Venetian printing:^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

  1. A three-line tribute by Aldus in the first text printed in the italic cursive — the Virgil of 1501.
  2. A claim by Gerolamo Soncino of Fano in 1503 that he had secured the services of the expert who had cut all Aldus’ types and deserved the real credit for their invention.
  3. A repetition of that claim by Griffo himself in an edition of Petrarch’s Canzonieri published in 1516 on his own account.

Soncino described Griffo as “a most excellent cutter of Latin, Greek and Hebrew letters, a man whose genius finds no equal in this craft.”^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] He was a specialist working exclusively for the press. The relationship between Aldus and Griffo lasted ten or twelve years at most, with the publishing company in production for only seven of these.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] During this period, Aldus commissioned and used twelve type-founts in three languages: six rounded “Roman” Latin types and the famous italic; four Greek cursives; and one Hebrew fount.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] Lowry concludes: “When we speak of Aldine typography, we are speaking of Griffo’s workmanship.”^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Aldus’ last distinct type was the small Greek cursive introduced for the Sophocles of August 1502.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] Shortly after, on 14 November 1502, Aldus was granted a comprehensive monopoly over all Greek publication and all Latin printing in italic within the Venetian dominions.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] This privilege, while protecting Aldus’ investment, effectively prevented Griffo from selling his most original designs to other printers. From Soncino’s direct charges that Aldus had usurped the credit for Griffo’s inventions, a violent professional jealousy evidently cut the relationship short.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] Griffo left Venice late in 1502. He later attempted independent publishing, but the strains proved too much: shortly after 1516 he brained his son-in-law with an iron bar — possibly an incomplete type punch — faced a murder charge in Bologna with unknown results, and sank into obscurity.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

One of the innovations in Greek typography claimed in Aldus’ petition was a system of “kerning” accents into the top of each vowel-sort, avoiding the need for separately cast accent sorts. Lowry notes that this looks like the device of an expert caster and should probably be attributed to Griffo.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] The wider dispute over who deserved credit for the cursive styles — the scribe who supplied the manuscript model, the type-cutter who executed it, or the intermediary who commissioned it — could not be resolved by the clumsy system of press-privileges, which sought only to protect the investor.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] After Griffo’s departure, experiment ended: no new type designs were introduced by the Aldine Press.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Lowry places the Griffo-Aldus relationship in a broader historical transition: “The pioneering age of Gutenberg and Jenson, when one man could direct every stage of the printing-process on the strength of his own skills, was fading into the past. The more regulated world of Garamond and Plantin, with a limited number of recognised and expert type-cutters supplying the needs of an established industry, still lay in the future. Aldus and Griffo fell between.”^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Fraktur

While Italy, France, and Spain adopted Roman and italic types, Germany developed its own distinctive blackletter variant: Fraktur.

Hans Schönsperger and the Teuerdank (1513)

The emperor Maximilian I (1459—1519) was a passionate patron of printing and book arts. He conceived an ambitious series of illustrated books celebrating his reign and genealogy, the most important of which was the Teuerdank — an allegorical poem describing a knight’s adventures, thinly veiled autobiography of Maximilian himself.

The type for the Teuerdank was cut by Hans Schönsperger of Augsburg, who designed a new blackletter that was more ornate and decorative than the Textura of the earlier German printers. This was Fraktur (from Latin fractura, “a breaking” — referring to the broken, jagged strokes of the letterforms).^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Lorck notes the turning point: “from now on the Gothic script was abandoned and the now-common Fraktur was developed.”^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] Fraktur became the dominant typeface for German-language printing for over four centuries, surviving into the mid-twentieth century (it was officially abolished by the Nazi regime in 1941, though it continued in limited use).

Fraktur is distinguished from Textura by:

  • Ornamented capitals: Elaborate, flowing capital letters with flourishes.
  • Broken strokes: The characteristic “breaking” of curved strokes into angular segments.
  • Contrast: Greater contrast between thick and thin strokes than in Textura.
  • Readability: Slightly more open and legible than the dense Textura of Gutenberg’s Bible.

Greek and Hebrew Types

The printing of texts in non-Latin scripts presented special challenges, as new punches and matrices had to be cut from scratch.

First Greek Type (1465)

The first printed Greek characters appeared in Fust and Schoeffer’s 1465 edition of Cicero’s De Officiis, printed in mainz. The Greek passages were set using crudely engraved wooden types — Schoeffer’s first attempt at non-Latin characters.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The first proper Greek type — cut from steel punches and cast in metal — was produced by the printers of Milan and Venice in the 1470s. The first book printed entirely in Greek was the 1476 Erotemata of Constantine Lascaris, printed in Milan by Dionysius Paravisinus.

Aldine Greek Type

Lowry provides the most detailed account of the technical challenges facing any printer who attempted Greek typography.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] The type-fount was always the most expensive item in a printer’s capital equipment, and a Greek fount posed particular problems. Greek uses “oxytone” or “perispomenon” accents on all words except enclitics, and “rough” or “smooth” breathing-symbols over all initial vowels. These symbols had either to be designed and cast with the appropriate letter-form in every possible combination, or cast as entirely separate types and set in place by the compositor above the letter they were to accent. The first solution vastly multiplied the number of types needed — Victor Scholderer calculated that the font used by Laonicus and Alexandros in Venice during the mid-1480s must have contained over 1,400 separate letter forms.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] The alternative made the compositor’s task much slower and meant wider spacing of lines, requiring more paper and increasing costs.

Aldus Manutius made Greek printing one of his specialities, issuing the Erotemata (1495) and his great series of Greek classics (Aristotle, Aristophanes, Thucydides, Sophocles, Plato) between 1495 and 1498. His Greek types were cut by Francesco Griffo, and Lowry notes that Aldus commissioned four distinct Greek cursive founts, similar to each other in basic conception but different in detail.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Lowry explains that Aldus’ Greek type was based on the cursive hand used by his circle of Greek scholars. His Greek teacher Adramyttenus, and his main scribal assistants Apostolis, Gregoropoulos, and Marcus Musurus all used a cursive hand, and they passed this taste on to their Italian pupils and patrons.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] Lowry inclines to the view that Aldus himself provided the manuscript-model for his Greek types: “in so far as I believe a definite decision to be possible.”^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] The cursive choice was controversial — contemporary critics preferred the uncial (maiuscule) Greek used by Lascaris and Alopa in Florence, but Lowry argues that for the market Aldus served — academics and wealthy patrons trained in the cursive hand — the cursive was the appropriate choice: “Clarity, like so much else, exists in the eye of the beholder.”^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The small Greek cursive introduced for Aldus’ Sophocles of August 1502 was the last distinct type cut by Griffo before his departure.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

First Hebrew Type (1473)

Hebrew type appeared almost as early. The first printed Hebrew book is contested: it may have been printed in Rome in 1469—1472 (tentatively attributed to Obadiah ben Moses) or in Prague shortly after. The earliest dated printed Hebrew book is the commentary on the Pentateuch by Rashi (Solomon ben Isaac), printed in 1475 in Reggio di Calabria, Italy, by Abraham Garton. Another contender is the Arba’ah Turim (Jacob ben Asher’s law code), printed c. 1473, probably in Rome.^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The cutting of Hebrew punches was especially challenging because of the script’s elaborate flourishes and the need for vowel points (nikud) and cantillation marks in liturgical texts. Early Hebrew printing was concentrated in Italy (Soncino family presses in Soncino, Casalmaggiore, and later Constantinople) and the Iberian Peninsula before the expulsion of 1492.

The Economics of Type

Behind every early typeface was a craft that was slow, expensive, and demanding:

  1. Punchcutting: A steel punch for each character was engraved in reverse and in relief on the end of a steel bar, then hardened by heating and quenching. A skilled engraver might cut two or three punches per day; a full font of 200+ characters could take months.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

  2. Striking the matrix: The hardened punch was struck into a bar of copper, creating a sunken impression (the matrix). The matrix was filed to exact dimensions so every type cast from it would be the correct height (type-high, approximately 0.918 inches).

  3. Casting: The matrix was fitted into the adjustable hand mold, molten type metal (lead, tin, antimony) was poured in, the mold was jerked to force metal into the fine details, and the type was ejected.

  4. Dressing: The cast type had its excess metal (the “jet”) broken off and was rubbed smooth on the feet to stand perfectly upright.

A complete font — including all variant letters, ligatures, and abbreviations — might represent a year’s work for a single punchcutter. Gutenberg employed at least three goldsmith-punchcutters (Hans Dunne, Gotz von Schlettstadt, and Hans von Speyer) to complete the Bible type.^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The Privilege System for Type Designs

From the earliest days of printing in Venice, the protection of type designs was linked to the system of printing privileges — early forms of intellectual property. Lowry records that on 18 September 1469, John of Speyer was granted a five-year monopoly over the craft of printing, which he had recently pioneered in the Republic of Venice.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] Such petitions were common in Venice, covering everything from improved windmills to experiments with poison-gas, but few came to anything. John died within months of gaining the monopoly, and his brother Windelin continued his work — but the privilege died with the original holder, and competitors soon thrust themselves forward.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The monopoly system later became central to the italic and Greek types. On 14 November 1502, Aldus was granted a comprehensive monopoly over all Greek publication and all Latin printing in italic within the Venetian dominions.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] Lowry notes that this clumsy system of press-privileges sought only to protect the interests of the investor — the printer or publisher who bore the costs of metal and labour — and did nothing to protect the craftsman who actually designed and cut the types.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] When Aldus used his privilege to outlaw all imitation of his Greek and italic styles, he was effectively, though perhaps unintentionally, preventing Griffo from selling his most original designs to other printers. The dispute over who deserved credit for the cursive styles — the scribe who supplied the model, the type-cutter who executed it, or the intermediary who commissioned it — could not be resolved by a system that only recognized the investor.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

The value of type-founts is illustrated by Lowry’s figures: Jenson’s workshop contents were valued at 500 ducats (with punches excluded and bequeathed separately), and the punches themselves may have been used to strike matrices for re-sale to other printers, since some thirty founts similar to Jenson’s design can be traced in various Italian centres.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] Later, in 1541, Claude Garamond was paid 225 Livres Tournois for cutting Robert Estienne’s Greek Royal types — roughly one third of the pension paid annually to Leonardo da Vinci.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] By this measure, Aldus’ capital outlay on his twelve type-founts must be reckoned in thousands rather than hundreds of ducats.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Type Design as an Art

The first fifty years of printing saw type design emerge as a distinct artistic discipline:

  • Gutenberg made the foundational choice: his Textura was a deliberate imitation of the finest liturgical manuscripts, setting the conservative standard that the first generation of printers followed.

  • Peter Schoeffer refined punchcutting techniques and introduced leading (spacing between lines) in his 1465 Cicero, improving readability. His types, however, were fewer in number and of inferior design to Gutenberg’s, according to De Vinne.^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

  • Nicolas Jenson achieved a perfection of Roman letterforms that has never been surpassed. His type was the first to demonstrate that printing could produce books that were not just as beautiful as manuscripts but more beautiful — clearer, more uniform, and more legible.

  • Francesco Griffo created the first italic, solving a practical problem (fitting more text in a small format) with a design of lasting grace. His clean forms influenced every italic type that followed. Lowry calls him “the faceless genius” whose workmanship defines “Aldine typography.”^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

  • Griffo and Aldus’s Roman type (the Bembo type, first used in Pietro Bembo’s De Aetna, February 1496) proved even more influential in the long run, though it received little emphasis from Aldus himself. Lowry notes that its relative weight was reduced to give the page “a light and harmonious appearance,” enhanced in 1499 by a delicately proportioned fount of capitals based on inscriptional models.^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md] This style, “unobtrusively launched in a sixty-page compliment to a personal friend,” caught the attention of the French type-founders Garamond and Simon de Colines around 1530, spread from their workshops across Europe, and holds its place in Western typography to the present day. “What has proved Aldus’ most permanent contribution to type-design appears to have had the least importance in his own eyes.”^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Significance

The evolution of typefaces in the first fifty years of printing tracks the development of the printed book itself. Textura was the type of the incunable period — backward-looking, imitative of manuscripts, dense and dark. Roman type was the type of humanism — clear, rational, forward-looking, shaped by the Italian Renaissance’s reverence for classical antiquity. Italic was the type of portability and commerce — compact, informal, designed for the pocket book. And Fraktur was the type of German cultural identity — a distinctively northern blackletter that would define German printing for half a millennium.

These four families — Textura (Gothic), Roman, Italic, and Fraktur — were the only typefaces available to printers for the first seventy years of printing. Every book produced in Europe until the early sixteenth century was set in one of them.
