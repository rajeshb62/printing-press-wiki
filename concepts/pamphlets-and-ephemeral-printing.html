Pamphlets and Ephemeral Printing

The printing revolution is usually told through books — the Gutenberg Bible, the Nuremberg Chronicle, the Aldine classics. But the overwhelming majority of what came off early presses was not books at all. It was cheap, short, disposable printed matter: indulgences, broadsides, calendars, school texts, political manifestos, and religious pamphlets. These formats reached far more people than folio Bibles ever could, and they were the formats that drove the Reformation, political propaganda, and popular literacy. Most of them were destroyed. The surviving record represents a tiny fraction of what was printed. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Before Gutenberg: Block-Printed Ephemera

Long before movable type, European printers produced short-format material from carved wooden blocks. These were the first mass-produced printed artifacts in Europe.

Image prints were single-sheet woodcuts of saints, sold to pilgrims and the poor as devotional aids. The earliest dated European print is the St. Christopher of 1423, discovered in the cover of a manuscript at the Chartreuse of Buxheim. These were printed in brown or black ink, often hand-colored, and sold for pennies. De Vinne calls them “the prototype of the modern chromo.” ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Playing cards reached Italy from the Islamic world around 1300 and Germany by the late 14th century. The demand was enormous enough to support guilds of card-makers (Kartenmacher) in Augsburg, Nuremberg, and Ulm by 1410. Cards were printed from carved wood blocks and hand-colored with stencils. De Vinne notes that card-makers also produced religious images — the two trades were closely linked. Playing cards were the first printed items to reach a truly popular market. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Block-books were short books printed entirely from carved wooden blocks, each page cut as a single unit. About thirty distinct block-book titles survive, the most important being the Biblia Pauperum (Bible of the Poor), Ars Moriendi (Art of Dying), Ars Memorandi, and the Speculum Humanae Salvationis. They ranged from 12 to 50 pages, were printed on one side of the paper only (in brown ink, by rubbing rather than pressing), and were intended for priests and the semi-literate. The Donatus, a Latin grammar that became the most frequently printed text of the 15th century, began as a block-book before moving to movable type. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The term Brief (letter) was used for any single-sided printed sheet — a playing card, a saint’s image, an indulgence, a proclamation. The craftsmen who produced them were called Briefmaler (letter-painters) and Briefdrucker (letter-printers). In the Rhine region they were simply called printers. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Gutenberg’s Jobbing Work

Gutenberg’s first printed products were not books but short-format items that could be produced quickly to generate cash flow while the Bible project consumed his resources.

Indulgences were single-sheet documents with blank fields left for the buyer’s name, the date, and the place of issue. They were the first mass-produced administrative documents in European history. In August 1451, Pope Nicholas V granted King John II of Cyprus the right to sell plenary indulgences for three years to fund a crusade against the Turks. Nicholas of Cusa ordered 2,000 for sale in Frankfurt by May 1452. Gutenberg printed them by the thousand in two workshops using two different typefaces. Twenty-three copies survive, all from the period November 1454 to April 1455. They are the earliest dated products of Gutenberg’s press. After their expiry date, bookbinders used them as scrap in covers — which is why they survive at all. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Calendars were among Gutenberg’s early products. His 1457 Calendar, printed on one side of a single sheet for wall display, listed festivals and notable days for six months. His Turk Calendar (1455) was a broadside warning Christendom against the Ottoman threat, with month-by-month exhortations in doggerel to Europe’s leaders, ending with the first printed New Year’s greeting: “Eyn gut selig nuwe jar.” ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The Sibylline Prophecy fragment, a postcard-sized scrap found in 1892 beneath the leather cover of an account book, is a two-column extract of a 750-line apocalyptic poem. It was printed in the D-K type, with poor alignment, no capital W, and the ink matching the 42-line Bible. It may be the earliest surviving printed piece from Gutenberg’s press. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Single-page prayer. Gutenberg also printed a single-page prayer, of which only one copy survives — with a nail-hole in the top, showing its owner hung it on a wall. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

School Texts as Pamphlets

The most frequently printed text of the 15th century was not a book but a pamphlet: the Ars Grammatica of Aelius Donatus, a Latin grammar of about 28 pages. Gutenberg printed it in the D-K type (202 characters, 24 editions over his career). The Donatus was the testing-ground for every early typeface, and De Vinne records that hundreds of thousands of copies were printed across Europe. Almost none survive — they were worn out by schoolboys, used as binder’s scrap, or thrown away. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Abecedarium, an 8-page child’s primer containing the alphabet, the Lord’s Prayer, the Ave Maria, and the Apostles’ Creed, was another standard pamphlet. De Vinne describes these as “cheaply made, adapted to the wants of school-boys, and probably sold for small sums.” The unknown printer of the Speculum editions produced mostly pamphlets: nineteen editions of the Donatus in six typefaces, along with six editions of the Doctrinal and a dozen other short works. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The First Political Pamphlet (1462)

During the 1462 Mainz civil war between rival archbishops Diether of Isenburg and Adolf of Nassau, printers entered politics for the first time. Fust and Schöffer rushed out the emperor’s condemnation of Diether, followed by the Pope’s rejection. In response, Diether and his secretary Konrad Humery published a manifesto. Lorck explicitly calls this “die erste politische Flugschrift” — the first political pamphlet — printed in poster form. More pamphlets followed from both sides, with printers acting as businessmen rather than partisans. This was the first time the printing press was used as a tool of political propaganda, and it set a pattern that would explode with the Reformation. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Broadsides and Posters

Single-sheet printing was the default format for public communication. Calendars were printed as wall posters. Proclamations, indulgences, prayers, and manifestos all appeared as broadsides. Lorck notes that the term Brief in German covered any single-sided printed sheet, from a playing card to a papal bull. The Plakat (poster) format was especially important for political printing, because it could be displayed in public places and read aloud to crowds. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

Broadsides also served commercial purposes. Caxton issued the first English printed advertisement around 1480 — a single sheet announcing the availability of a liturgical manual. Printers used broadsides as publishers’ catalogues, listing their available titles for display at book fairs. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The Reformation Explosion (1517-1525)

The pamphlet came into its own with the Reformation. The Flugschriften — “flying writings” — transformed European publishing. Putnam Vol. II provides the statistics:

  • German-language works rose from 90 in 1513 to 146 in 1518, then leaped to 571 in 1520 and 944 in 1523. The aggregate for the decade 1513-23 was 3,113 works.
  • Of that total, 600 were printed in Wittenberg — a town that before 1517 had not possessed a single printing press.
  • A third of all books printed in Germany between 1518 and 1525 were by Martin Luther.
  • Luther’s Address to the Nobility of the German Nation (August 1520) sold 4,000 to 5,000 copies in five days.
  • His pamphlet against Johann Eck (1518) sold 1,400 copies in two days at the Frankfurt Fair.
  • Over 100,000 copies of Luther’s New Testament were printed in Wittenberg alone during his lifetime.
  • Typical price: one groschen (about 2.5 cents). ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The pamphlets were distributed outside the regular book trade entirely. They were sold by pedlars and colporteurs in market-places, by travelling students working for the Reform cause, and by preachers who carried them in their packs. They were read aloud in public squares, reaching dozens of hearers per copy. The 1520 papal bull excommunicating Luther and ordering his works burned had the opposite effect — it caused a largely increased sale throughout Germany. As Putnam notes, “the immediate effect of this Bull was to cause a largely increased sale throughout nearly all parts of Germany for everything that Luther had written.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

The Reformation also changed the format of publishing. Costly folios and quartos were replaced by inexpensive duodecimos and pamphlets. The economic model shifted: instead of scholars producing expensive reference works for a small elite, printers now produced cheap, topical pieces for a mass audience. Koberger’s business, based on heavyweight theology in folio format, collapsed under the pressure. New men took over German publishing. As Putnam puts it, “German replaced Latin, and the Press became the servant of the people at large.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Chap-Books and Popular Literature

Beyond religious and political pamphlets, cheap popular literature circulated in enormous quantities. Lowry notes that “far more chap-books and pamphlets than we [have records of]” were produced. Chap-books were short, cheap booklets containing romances, farces, almanacs, prophecies, and practical guides. They were the medieval equivalent of mass-market paperbacks, sold by pedlars in market-places and at fairs. Because they were cheap, read to pieces, and never collected by libraries, the survival rate is extremely low. They are the black hole of early printing history: everyone agrees they existed in huge numbers, but almost nothing remains. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

John Man adds that “what sold fast was good old-fashioned dross: astrology, alchemy and esoteric lore,” noting that “books on hermetic lore formed a medieval equivalent of New Age publishing.” ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

Survival Bias

The most important fact about ephemeral printing is that most of it is gone. The surviving record is systematically distorted toward what was preserved: library-quality books. The cheap stuff was destroyed:

  • Binder’s scrap. Indulgences, Donatus leaves, and broadsides were used as stiffeners in book covers. This is how most of Gutenberg’s indulgences and the Sibylline Prophecy fragment survived.
  • Worn out. School texts were used by generations of students. Playing cards were played with. Wall calendars were pasted over. Chap-books were read to pieces.
  • Deliberately discarded. After the indulgence expiry date (May 1, 1455), the sheets were worthless. Political pamphlets from losing sides were destroyed.
  • Destroyed in war. Lorck notes that much of the short-format output from the early Briefmaler and Formenschneider was destroyed during the Thirty Years’ War. ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md] ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]

The result is that historians know far more about the printing of the B42 than about the pamphlets that paid for it. As Lowry puts it, the true volume of ephemeral printing can only be guessed at. ^[raw/articles/lowry-world-of-aldus-manutius.md]

Summary

The pamphlet was the unsung workhorse of the early printing press. It paid for Gutenberg’s experiments, it fought the 1462 Mainz civil war as the first political propaganda, it transformed the German book trade under Luther, and it reached audiences that folio Bibles never could. It was cheap, fast, ephemeral, and almost entirely destroyed. The printing revolution was not just a revolution of books — it was equally a revolution of pamphlets, posters, indulgences, and school texts. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]
