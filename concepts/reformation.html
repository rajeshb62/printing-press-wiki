Reformation

The Reformation was not caused by printing alone, but it became the first great European religious movement to exploit print at scale. movable-type made it possible for arguments, sermons, vernacular Bibles, pamphlets, broadsides, and polemics to circulate faster and farther than manuscript culture allowed. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Printing as an Accelerator

By the early sixteenth century the infrastructure described in spread-of-printing was already in place: presses, publishers, paper supply, book fairs, distribution routes, and a reading public. Reformers entered a communication environment that Gutenberg could not have foreseen. A theological dispute could now become a public controversy across cities and principalities. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

Luther was prompt to recognise the value of the new art for his work. He understood that if the fight against Rome was to secure popular support, it was necessary to reach not only the limited circles of the educated but the masses. For that purpose he put forth his sermons, tracts, and controversial pamphlets at once in the language of the people. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1450] For thousands of Germans, the first practical knowledge of the existence of the printing-press came with the sight of the sheets of the Wittenberg pamphlets or of the volumes of the Wittenberg Testament. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1450] The greater number of the pamphlets were issued at once in two editions, one Latin and one German. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1496]

Luther himself was amazed at the extent of the public he was able to reach when, after 1518, his tracts and sermons came to be printed in German; up to this time they had been originally issued in Latin and only in part translated. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1578]

Flugschriften: The Pamphlet Economy

The revolution in publishing methods brought about in connection with the Reformation was not restricted to the introduction of German as a language for popular publications. Of almost equal importance was the change in the form and price of books: costly folios and quartos were replaced by comparatively inexpensive twelvemos and sixteenmos, and by far the larger proportion of the writings that exercised the most immediate influence on the thought of the time were issued in the form of pamphlets. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1576] These pamphlets, sold in market-places, along highways, and from house to house, by pedlars working for gain and by colporteurs having a missionary purpose, took the place which in modern times is filled by the magazine or weekly paper. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1576] The German term Flugschriften — literally “fly-leaf literature” or “flying writings” — captures their ephemeral, rapidly circulated character. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1498] Every pfennig that came in from the fly-leaves or pamphlets appears to have been at once expended in further printings and in instituting further distributing machinery. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1620]

According to the statistics of book-production collected by Panzer, Weller, and Kuczynski, and tabulated by Kapp, the total number of separate works (principally pamphlets, Flugschriften) printed in German rose dramatically: ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1576]

The aggregate for the decade 1513-1523 was 3,113. Of that total, no fewer than 600 were printed in Wittenberg — a place which before 1517 had not possessed a printing-press. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1576]

Edition Sizes and Speed of Sale

One of the more important of the earlier Flugschriften of Luther was the Address to the Nobility of Germany, printed in August 1520, of which 5,000 copies were sold in five days. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1498] Of the pamphlet containing his controversial address against Eck, printed in 1518, 1,400 copies were sold in two days at the Frankfort Fair. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1498]

Kostlin estimates that by 1520, more than one hundred editions had been printed of the German versions of Luther’s sermons and tracts, including all unauthorised issues as well as the editions printed at Luther’s Wittenberg Press. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1514] During the lifetime of the Reformer, not less than 100,000 copies of Luther’s New Testament were printed in Wittenberg alone. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1548]

Price Point

The usual price for the Protestant tracts was one groschen, equal to two and a half cents, or in purchasing power to perhaps twelve cents today. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1604]

Distribution Networks

The distribution of Luther’s pamphlets was effected only in part through the regular book-trade. Thousands of copies were sold in the market-places by dealers of all kinds, many of whom had never before handled books. Large supplies were distributed among readers out of reach of book-shops and market-places by travelling pedlars and by colporteurs. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1514] Many of the colporteurs were travelling students who were working not for gain but in the cause of the Reform. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1514]

The habit of circulating books and pamphlets by means of pedlars and colporteurs in districts far beyond the reach of the book-shops had both an immediate and an abiding effect upon the reading habits of the German people and did much to bring about the development of the publishing and bookselling business in Germany. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1452]

The circulation of the Lutheran tracts was taken charge of not only by book-pedlars and colporteurs but by a large number of travelling preachers (Pradikanten) — in part old-time priests, in many cases laymen of very varying degrees of education. The Wittenberg tracts gave a supply of ammunition that even the most ignorant preachers could make effective. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1608]

Under the pressure of the widespread interest in the writings of the Reformation, publishing business was done in a number of out-of-the-way little towns that would today hardly support a printing-press. Lutheran tracts were printed in places such as Grimma, Zwickau, and Eilenburg, principally for sale through the pedlars, very frequently without imprint or date. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1598]

The effective circulation of Luther’s pamphlets was very much multiplied by the practice of reading such pamphlets out loud in the market-places to all who might be interested. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1592] Each tract reached, as a rule, a number of readers or hearers, for the old Oriental and Greek practice of reading aloud to an audience was carried on in hundreds of market-places, shops, and other informal auditoriums. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1604] After the Lutheran writings had been put under interdict, such public readings had to be discontinued in towns and districts that remained under Catholic control, but the pamphlets were still widely (though surreptitiously) sold by pedlars and colporteurs, and the readings continued in places less liable to interruption than the inns and market-places. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1592]

Effect of the Bull of Leo X (1520)

In 1520, a Bull of Leo X excommunicated Luther, condemned his works individually and collectively, ordered existing copies to be burnt, and prohibited, under severe penalties, the printing, sale, distribution, or even possession of any of his writings. The immediate effect of this Bull was to cause a largely increased sale throughout nearly all parts of Germany for everything that Luther had written, and to bring about also a very considerable demand for them from other countries. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1514]

As an evidence of the very general distribution secured for Luther’s writings, the publisher Froben wrote to Luther from Basel on February 14, 1519, speaking of large supplies of the Basel editions being called for not only in Germany but in France, Spain, Italy, Brabant, and England. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1590]

Unauthorised Reprints and Luther’s Response

Luther and his associates were greatly troubled at the inaccuracy and incompleteness of much of the material sold over their names by unauthorised printers. In September 1525, Luther wrote to inquire whether his printers had been heedless in permitting sheets to be stolen for use in unauthorised printing elsewhere. “It is bad enough for these rascals to get the advantage, through theft, of my labour and pains, but with that I would be patient, if it were not for the shamelessly false and blundering form in which they issue books described as mine.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1554] He closed his complaint not with a contention for complete control of his material but with a moderate suggestion that the reprinters ought, as a matter of Christian feeling, first to wait a few months to give the original edition a fair chance, and second, to print their own issues with a decent regard for correctness and completeness. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1556]

Luther’s principal annoyance about reprints was in connection with inaccurate and incomplete texts. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1526] He wrote to the magistrates of Nuremberg in September 1525 complaining that proof-sheets of a volume of his sermons had been stolen from his printing-office in Wittenberg and used in Nuremberg for a piracy volume. He asked them to induce local printers to delay bringing out reprints until seven or eight weeks after the publication of the original editions. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1558]

Vernacular Religion

Printing strengthened vernacular religious culture. Texts in German, English, French, Dutch, and other languages reached readers who did not command Latin. This mattered not only for Protestant reform but for Catholic response, censorship, devotional literature, and education. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md] ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md] ^[raw/articles/de-vinne-invention-of-printing.md]

The popular interest excited by the writings of Luther and his associates brought about a great change in the trade of the book-shops. Editions of the Fathers and of the lives of the Saints were pushed aside from the counters or the book-shelves, or stored away in the warehouses, and even the classics were neglected. All the demand was for the writings of the Reformers. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1498] The jurist Scheurl wrote from Nuremberg to Cardinal Campeggi on March 15, 1524: “Every common man is now asking for books or pamphlets, and more reading is being done in a day than heretofore in a year.” ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1594]

The Catholic-Protestant Publishing War

The Reformation did not simply create a Protestant publishing industry. It created a confessional publishing war in which both sides used the press to fight for the same audiences, and the competition reshaped the economics of European printing.

Catholic Repression and the Protestant Advantage

The Catholic authorities responded to the pamphlet flood with censorship — the Index of Prohibited Books (1559), the Roman Inquisition, the Paris Parlement, and the English Stationers’ Company. But censorship had a paradoxical effect. The Bull of Leo X (1520) excommunicating Luther and ordering his works burned “caused a largely increased sale throughout nearly all parts of Germany for everything that Luther had written.” Condemnation acted as advertising. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md L1514]

In Catholic-controlled territories, the repression of Protestant printing created a two-way squeeze. Duke George of Saxony, a Catholic, “seriously hampered” Protestant printers in Leipzig with rigorous censorship. But “the writings of the Romanists found few buyers” — the Catholic market was smaller than the Protestant one in most German cities, and printing for an unpopular minority was commercially unattractive. The result was a depressed book trade in Catholic cities and a boom in Protestant ones. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol1.md]

The Geneva-Lyon Rivalry

The most dramatic example of cross-confessional publishing competition was the relationship between Geneva (Protestant) and Lyon (Catholic). Lyon was the larger and more established printing centre, but Geneva’s Calvinist printers developed a specialized strategy: they manufactured cheap editions of Catholic works — missals, breviaries, books of hours, even Jesuit publications — for illicit sale in Catholic territories. The Lyonese printers retaliated by using “the brand of heretic to secure the confiscation at the frontier of a good many shipments of the books from Geneva” — even when those books were perfectly orthodox in content. The charge of heresy was a trade weapon as much as a religious one. ^[raw/articles/putnam-books-and-their-makers-vol2.md]

Catholic Printers Fight Back

The Catholic Church did not rely solely on repression. It also built its own printing infrastructure:

  • The Jesuit press in Vienna (1559). Ferdinand I called the Jesuits to Vienna in 1551, and the rector Johann Victoria established a press “zum besten der Religion und armer Studenten” (for the benefit of religion and poor students). Its main product was the catechism of Peter Canisius, commissioned by imperial order, translated into many languages and still in use in Austrian schools centuries later. The press operated until 1565. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

  • Jesuit missionary presses. Jesuits operated secret presses in Cordoba (17th century), and presses in Japan (Amacusa, Nagasaki from the late 16th century) and China (Peking, Nanjing) for printing Christian texts in local languages. In Constantinople, Jesuits successfully prevented a Greek Orthodox press from being established. ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

  • The Congregation de Propaganda Fide (1622). Rome established a missionary press attached to the Vatican, equipped with oriental types by the punchcutter Stefano Paoli, printing New Testaments and devotional books “in many languages” — though Lorck notes it “never developed effectiveness commensurate with its considerable resources.” ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Blaeu Paradox

The most striking example of how confessional competition distorted normal publishing economics was the Blaeu firm in Amsterdam. Willem Jansz Blaeu was a convinced Copernican and Protestant who disguised his views in his mariners’ guides. Yet his firm’s most reliable revenue stream — alongside atlases — was the “semi-clandestine production of missals and other Roman Catholic literature” for export. When Blaeu’s printing house burned on 22 February 1672, “strict Protestants called it divine punishment because Blaeu printed many breviaries and missals for Catholics.” ^[raw/articles/blaeu-review-nellen.md] ^[raw/articles/lorck-handbuch-buchdruckerkunst.md]

The Protestant Conversion of Gutenberg

John Man summarizes the ideological reversal neatly: “Gutenberg, once the darling of the Catholics, now became a Protestant hero. Printing, Luther said, was ‘God’s highest and extremest act of grace, whereby the business of the Gospel is driven forward.‘” The technology that had been invented to produce standardized Catholic liturgical texts became the engine of the movement that shattered Catholic unity. ^[raw/articles/john-man-gutenberg-revolution.md]
