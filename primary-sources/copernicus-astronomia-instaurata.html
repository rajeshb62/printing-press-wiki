Astronomia instaurata (Copernicus / Mulerius, 1617)

Nicolai Copernici Torinensis Astronomia instaurata, libris sex comprehensa, qui de revolutionibus orbium coelestium inscribuntur is the 1617 Amsterdam edition of Copernicus’s De revolutionibus, restored and annotated by Nicolaus Mulerius, professor of medicine and mathematics at the new Academy of Groningen. The title page names Wilhelmus Iansonius at Amsterdam, “sub Solari aureo” (at the Golden Sundial), the imprint associated with Willem Jansz Blaeu before the Blaeu name stabilized. The source is therefore useful both as a scientific classic and as evidence for Amsterdam’s early-seventeenth-century learned and astronomical publishing.

Why This Belongs in the Printing-Press Wiki

This is not just a scientific text. It is a concrete example of how the press carried a difficult mathematical astronomy book across editions, corrections, tables, and scholarly apparatus. Mulerius explicitly frames his work as a repair of texts damaged by earlier printers and by corrupt numerical transmission. The edition also sits inside the commercial-scientific world of Blaeu: a printer trained in astronomy, selling maps, globes, nautical works, and learned books from Amsterdam.

Narrative Source Flow

Front Matter and Publishing Frame

The title page presents the work as Copernicus’s astronomy “restored” after injuries by earlier authors and “illustrated with notes” by Mulerius. The printer line reads Amsterdam, Wilhelmus Iansonius, under the Golden Sundial, 1617. This front matter is valuable for the book-trade side of the source: it shows Blaeu/Iansonius marketing an improved, portable, annotated edition rather than merely reproducing the Nuremberg or Basel folios.

A short printer/editor note says Copernicus had already appeared in two folio editions, Nuremberg and Basel, but this form was preferred because the available types suited it and because it could be joined with the Frisian tables printed five years earlier. That is a direct statement about format, type, companion tables, and the packaging of scientific works for students of astronomy.

Mulerius’s Dedication to Groningen and Ommelanden

Mulerius dedicates the edition to the authorities of Groningen and the Ommelanden, praising their support of arms and letters and the newly established academy. He explains that, as one of the first professors called to teach there, he wanted to contribute to public use by restoring important astronomical authors. His complaint is typographical as much as scholarly: the best astronomical authors had been so foully handled by printers that readers either struggled through them or were led into errors in chronological calculation.

This dedication is one of the source’s richest printing-history passages. Mulerius presents editing as a medical repair of corrupted books: authors are “repurged,” corrected, and restored to their former health. He says Copernicus’s astronomy had been orphaned at birth, published in the same year its author died, and exposed to injuries because it lacked a guardian. Tycho Brahe and others had complained of those injuries; Mulerius therefore retells his edition as a scholarly rescue through printing.

Osiander’s Ad lectorem

The edition reprints the famous Ad lectorem de hypothesibus huius operis, the anonymous preface attached to the original De revolutionibus. It warns readers not to treat astronomical hypotheses too literally and presents them as calculating devices. In a printing-history context, its presence matters because the printed object carried not only Copernicus’s argument but an interpretive frame around it.

Copernicus’s Preface to Pope Paul III

Copernicus’s own preface explains his hesitation to publish a system assigning motions to the Earth, anticipating that many readers would reject the novelty and absurdity of the opinion. He describes the pressure from learned men to publish and presents mathematical astronomy as a search for truth as far as reason permits. This section is central for future mining into the scientific revolution and print: a controversial theory became durable and discussable because it could circulate in a stable printed form.

Index of the Six Books

The index maps the six books of De revolutionibus. Book I covers the spherical world, the spherical Earth, circular celestial motion, objections to terrestrial motion, the order of the celestial orbs, and the triple motion of the Earth. Book II treats circles, declinations, ascensions, horizons, oblique sphere tables, and fixed stars. Book III treats precession, solar motion, equinoxes, obliquity, and the year. Book IV covers lunar theory, parallax, eclipses, and distances. Book V covers planetary motions in longitude for Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Venus, and Mercury. Book VI covers planetary latitude.

The printed index is itself useful: it makes a mathematically dense work navigable and shows the mature apparatus of post-incunable scholarly books — numbered chapters, page references, tables, errata, and canons.

The Six Books of De revolutionibus

The main body begins with Book I, Chapter I, on the spherical form of the world, followed by the spherical Earth and the arguments around motion. The OCR preserves extensive diagrams and mathematical exposition only imperfectly, but the structure is clear. Many body pages interleave Copernicus’s text with Mulerius’s notes, making the source a commentary edition rather than a bare reprint.

Books II-VI are heavy with trigonometry, astronomical tables, fixed-star descriptions, planetary observations, and computational canons. These sections are less immediately useful for general narrative history, but they are excellent evidence for the kind of numerical and diagrammatic material early modern printers had to set accurately. The tables also explain why Mulerius stresses correction: a single wrong number or misplaced heading could damage the utility of the whole book.

Errata and Thesaurus astronomicus

Near the end, the edition prints a list of remaining errors, partly inherited from earlier editions and partly introduced in this one. Several entries concern numerical tables and astronomical calculations. The errata page is a high-value printing-history artifact because it makes correction visible: even an edition devoted to repairing earlier typographical and numerical corruption had to publish its own residual mistakes.

The final pages continue with an observational and computational Thesaurus astronomicus. They include planetary observations, references to Ptolemy, Copernicus, Bernard Walther, and Johannes Schoner, and roots of mean motions for chronological calculation. This material connects Copernican theory to the tabular and observational tradition that printed astronomy preserved and standardized.

High-Value Passages to Mine

  • Title page: restored and annotated Copernicus, Amsterdam 1617, Wilhelmus Iansonius under the Golden Sundial.
  • Printer/editor note: Nuremberg and Basel folio editions existed; the new format was chosen because the printer’s types suited it and because it could be bound with Frisian tables.
  • Mulerius dedication: printers had mishandled astronomical authors so badly that readers were misled in time-reckoning; his edition aims to restore the author’s health.
  • Osiander preface: printed paratext framing heliocentric hypotheses as computational tools.
  • Copernicus preface: publication anxiety over Earth’s motion and the decision to bring the work into public light.
  • Six-book index: compact map of the work’s structure and topics.
  • Errata: residual inherited and new errors in a mathematically demanding printed book.
  • Thesaurus astronomicus ending: observations and mean-motion roots showing how printed astronomy functioned as computational infrastructure.
