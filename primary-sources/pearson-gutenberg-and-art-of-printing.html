Gutenberg and the Art of Printing (Pearson)

Gutenberg and the Art of Printing by Emily C. Pearson (published 1871, Andover, Massachusetts) is a narrative biography of Johannes Gutenberg covering his life and the invention of movable type, with later chapters surveying nineteenth-century printing technology. The book runs approximately 300 pages across 25 chapters.

Summary of Contents

The book traces Gutenberg’s life from his childhood in Mainz, his exile in Strasbourg, his early experiments with block-printing and engraving, the partnership with Dritzehn (here spelled “Dritzhn”) and its dissolution through a lawsuit, the invention of movable type, the partnership with Johann Fust (called “Faust”) and Peter Schoeffer, the printing of the 42-line Bible, Fust’s seizure of the press, and Gutenberg’s final years. The final six chapters shift to a survey of nineteenth-century printing technology: typefounding, type-setting machinery, stereotyping, electrotyping, hand-presses, cylinder presses, and a closing philosophical reflection on printing.

Chapter-by-Chapter Detail

Chapter I — Strasbourg and its Cathedral. Gutenberg’s Early Life. Civil Strife. Romantic Lawsuit.

Describes Strasbourg and its cathedral. Covers Gutenberg’s birth in Mainz around 1400, his flight to Strasbourg due to political conflict between the nobility and the burghers, his banishment and loss of property, and the breach-of-promise lawsuit brought by Anna von Isemen Thur (here portrayed as a romantic summons to compel Gutenberg to fulfill his marriage promise).

Chapter II — Gutenberg in Exile. His Trade as Lapidary. Curious Law. Ancient Cuts. A Picture of a Saint. Legend. The Bible for the Poor. A Secret discovered. Gutenberg’s Experiment.

Covers Gutenberg’s exile and his trade as a lapidary (polisher of precious stones). Describes the social status of artisans in medieval Germany. Relates, through invented dialogue between Gutenberg and Anna, an examination of a playing-card that leads Gutenberg to explain wood-engraving. Includes the story of the St. Christopher woodcut (dated 1423) and the legend of St. Christopher. Discusses the Biblia Pauperum (Bible for the Poor) — a block-book of forty engraved pictures with written explanations. Anna discovers Gutenberg’s secret experiments with block-printing.

Chapter III — Ancient Books and their Materials.

A survey of the history of writing and books before printing. Covers early materials (brick, tile, shells, stone, lead), Chinese printing and papermaking, seals and stamping among the Romans, waxen tablets, bark, papyrus, parchment, paper, palimpsests, the scriptorium and monastic copyists, illuminations, the scarcity and costliness of books, Richard de Bury and his library (c. 1344), the statutes of St. Mary’s College Oxford limiting book use, chained books, and the contrast with the abundance of books in modern times.

Chapter IV — An Important Step. Engraving a Name. Engraving Pictures. Superstitions. Difficulties overcome. An Improvement. Experiment and Progress. A New Book. Cheerful Thoughts.

Gutenberg engraves Anna’s name on a wooden block and prints it on paper. He takes up the project of engraving a St. Christopher picture. Describes the search for suitable wood (apple-tree chosen), the making of tools, the improvement of printing ink (oil-based ink developed), the making of the first printer’s dabber (a sheepskin ball), and Gutenberg’s first impressions from engraved blocks.

Chapter V — Pecuniary Troubles. An Expedient. Disappointment. The Jewels. A Sale. Apprentices. Visit to the Cathedral. A New Enterprise.

Gutenberg faces financial difficulties from his experiments. He polishes jewels for the Abbot of the Cathedral to earn money. Takes on apprentices — Dritzhn (Andrew Dritzehn), Hielman, and Riffe. Visits the Cathedral library and sees block-books. Begins planning a new enterprise based on printing.

Chapter VI — Unwelcome Visitors. Unjust Demand. A Compromise. Secret Firm. A Removal. Teaching the Workmen. Block Printing. Success.

Gutenberg’s partners pressure him for results. A secret partnership is formed. The workshop is moved to a more secluded location. Gutenberg teaches his workmen the basics of block-printing. They achieve success with early block-printed products.

Chapter VII — Small Receipts. Printing the “Donatus.” “Ars Memorandi” “Ars Moriendi.” An Interesting Fact. Extract from “Ars Moriendi.”

The firm’s income is limited. They print the Donatus (a Latin grammar by Aelius Donatus), the Ars Memorandi (Art of Memory), and the Ars Moriendi (Art of Dying). An extract from the Ars Moriendi is quoted.

Chapter VIII — Effect of Gutenberg’s Books. His Times and Ours. His Books at the Cathedral. Curiosity of the Monks. Proposition of the Abbot. The “Bible for the Poor.” A Great Work well done. A Good Sale. The Canticles issued. A Difficult Undertaking. Discontent. An Accident. Discovery of Separate Types. The First Font of Movable Type. Difficulties mastered. The Great Helper.

The early printed books attract attention at the Cathedral. The Abbot proposes a project. The firm prints the Canticles. Discontent arises among the partners. An accident leads Gutenberg to the idea of separate, movable types rather than fixed engraved blocks. He produces the first font of movable type.

Chapter IX — Anna’s Disappointment. Dritzhn’s Regrets. Comfort for Anna. Gutenberg’s Progress described. The Great Enlightener. Advantages of Movable Type. Another Book. Obstacles. Criticisms. Invention. A Press contrived. New Cause of Disquiet.

Gutenberg argues the advantages of movable type over block-printing to his skeptical partners (Dritzhn and Riffe). A press is contrived. New difficulties emerge. Contains the frequently cited passage where Gutenberg explains that block-printing wastes time cutting letters that cannot be reused.

Chapter X — A Partner at the Confessional. His Death. Consequences. A Lawsuit. Thieves. Dangerous Curiosity. Destruction of Gutenberg’s Type. Curious Testimonies. Value of the Legal Document. Proof that Gutenberg was the Real Inventor. The Magistrate’s Just Judgment. Public Excitement.

One of the partners (Dritzhn) goes to confession, revealing the secret. He dies shortly after. A lawsuit ensues among the surviving partners. Includes testimony from the actual Strasbourg court depositions (Anna Schultheiss, Conrad Sachspach, Laurence Beildeck, John Dunnius the goldsmith). Gutenberg’s type is destroyed. The magistrate rules in Gutenberg’s favor.

Chapter XI — Benighted. Minstrel of the Hearth. The Black Art. A Barefoot Friar. Popular Prejudice. Hopes and Fears. Gutenberg returns to his Trade. Dissolution of the Copartnership.

Popular accusations of witchcraft and the “black art” surface against Gutenberg and his printing. Gutenberg returns to his lapidary trade. The Strasbourg partnership is formally dissolved.

Chapter XII — Congenial Quiet. Making Type again. Gutenberg issues “Absies.” Peter Schoeffer. Decides to remove to Mentz. Emotions of Gutenberg. Fraternal Sympathy. The Meeting with Faust. Gutenberg reveals his Art. A Rich Partner.

Gutenberg continues making type in quiet retirement. He issues a small work (“Absies”). Peter Schoeffer appears and becomes involved. Gutenberg decides to move to Mainz (here called “Mentz”). He meets Fust (here called “Faust”), reveals his art, and forms a new partnership with Faust as a wealthy financial backer.

Chapter XIII — The Zum Jungen. The Old Valet. A Happy Change. Going over the Process anew. Good Progress. Peter Schoeffer.

Gutenberg and his team establish the workshop at the Zum Jungen property. They review and re-establish the printing process. Peter Schoeffer joins as a skilled workman.

Chapter XIV — Working of the Press. The Medallion. An Acquisition. Experiments. A Failure. Schoeffer’s Invention. Discovery of Cast Metal Type.

Work on the press continues. Schoeffer conducts experiments. After a failure, Schoeffer discovers the method of casting metal type from matrices — cast metal type rather than individually engraved or carved letters.

Chapter XV — Schoeffer admitted to the Firm. A Grand Project. How a Bible was borrowed. The Early Press. Processes in Book-making. Ingenuity of Peter Schoeffer. Industry of the Firm. Ink. Cast Type. Three Ingenious Men. Letter-founding. Faust compliments Peter. The first Printed Page of the Bible. A Memorable Year.

Schoeffer is admitted to the partnership. The grand project of printing a Bible is conceived. A manuscript Bible is borrowed as a copy-text. Describes the early press, ink-making, and letter-founding processes. The first printed page of the 42-line Bible is produced in a memorable year (c. 1455).

Chapter XVI — Fust’s Discontent. Conspiracy against Gutenberg. The Lawsuit. Gutenberg supplanted. A New Firm. Gutenberg’s Sorrow.

Fust becomes discontented with the arrangement. A conspiracy forms against Gutenberg. Fust sues Gutenberg for repayment of loans. Gutenberg is supplanted and loses his press and types to Fust. Fust and Schoeffer form a new firm. Gutenberg is left in sorrow.

Chapter XVII — The Story of Faust’s Visit to Paris. Was it Witchcraft? Popular Excitement. Scene in a Court Room. Issue of the Psalter.

Relates the legend of Fust (Faust) traveling to Paris to sell printed Bibles, where people accuse him of witchcraft because uniform copies could only be produced by diabolical means. Includes a fictional courtroom scene. Describes the issue of the Psalter (the Mainz Psalter, 1457).

Chapter XVIII — New Friends. The Nun. Gutenberg at Work again. Printing of the “Balbus de Janua.” Other Works. A Curious Record. Death of the Great Inventor. Fadeless Laurels.

Gutenberg finds new supporters. He resumes printing and produces the Balbus de Janua (the Catholicon, a dictionary by Giovanni Balbi). Other works are mentioned. A curious record of Gutenberg is noted. Covers Gutenberg’s death and his legacy.

Chapter XIX — Fust and Schoeffer’s Success. More Books issued. An Eventful Year. Greek Type. Struck by the Plague. The Parisians and Faust’s Descendants. Schoeffer’s Death. Testimony to Gutenberg. Extension of the Art. Peculiarities of the First Printed Books. Early Printers. Piety and Chess. Education in the Olden Time. Great Enterprise. Unveiling Gutenberg’s Statue.

Covers the continued success of Fust and Schoeffer after Gutenberg’s departure. More books are issued. Greek type is introduced. Schoeffer dies. Testimonies to Gutenberg as the inventor are collected. The spread of printing to other cities is described. Discusses peculiarities of early printed books (no title pages, no page numbers, no signatures). Early printers are profiled. Covers the unveiling of Gutenberg’s statue.

Chapter XX — Modes of making Type. Varieties of Type. Cylindrical Ink-distributor. A Modern Printing Establishment. Composition Room. Cases. Proofreading.

Shifts to a description of nineteenth-century printing technology. Covers the methods of making type and the varieties of type available. Describes the cylindrical ink-distributor. Takes the reader through a modern printing establishment: the composition room, type cases, and proofreading procedures.

Chapter XXI — Type-setting by Machinery. Its Practicability. Various Machines devised. The Brown Type-setter and Distributer described. Simplicity. Reliability. Speed.

Discusses the practicability of mechanical type-setting. Surveys various machines devised for this purpose. Describes the Brown type-setter and distributor in detail: its simplicity, reliability, and speed of operation.

Chapter XXII — Stereotyping. Plaster Moulds. Planing and Bending. Correcting Stereotype Plates. Process of Electrotyping. The “Guillotine.” Ornamenting.

Describes the process of stereotyping — making plaster moulds from type forms, planing and bending stereotype plates, and correcting them. Then covers the process of electrotyping. Describes the “guillotine” machine for trimming and the ornamenting of printed pages.

Chapter XXIII — The Hand-press. Earl Stanhope’s Press. Improvements. Cylinder Presses. Press-room. Drying Room. Sewing Room. Elevator. Books for the Blind. Type, Press, and Paper invented. Catalogue of Great Exhibition. Estimate of Rapid Labor by Machinery.

Describes the hand-press and Earl Stanhope’s improved iron press. Covers cylinder presses and their advantages. Tours the various rooms of a modern printing establishment: press-room, drying room, sewing room, and the use of elevators. Mentions books printed for the blind. Notes that type, press, and paper were all invented. Cites the Catalogue of the Great Exhibition. Provides estimates of rapid labor accomplished by machinery.

Chapter XXIV — Time of the Great Invention. A First Gift. Discovery of the Alphabet. A New Era. Royal Printers. Knights of Type and Pen. A Mighty Engine. Gutenberg’s Dream. The Press mighty.

A closing philosophical chapter reflecting on the significance of Gutenberg’s invention. Discusses the timing of the invention, the alphabet as a first gift to civilization, the new era printing inaugurated, royal printers and their status, the power of the press as a “mighty engine,” and Gutenberg’s dream realized through the widespread influence of printing.
