The Invention of Printing (De Vinne)

Theodore Low De Vinne, The Invention of Printing: A Collection of Facts and Opinions Descriptive of the Origins and Development of Typography (New York, 1876). 26 chapters, ~550 pages, with over 200 illustrations.

Summary of Contents

The book covers the history of printing from its earliest antecedents to the establishment of typography in Germany around 1500. It is organized in six parts:

Part I — Methods and Antecedents (Chapters I-VI): Definitions of the four printing methods (copper-plate, lithography, typography, xylography). Ancient impression techniques — Babylonian brick stamps, Assyrian cylinders, Roman stamps — and why they did not develop into printing. The mechanical key to the invention: the adjustable hand mold, the punch, and the matrix. The earliest European woodcut prints: St. Christopher (1423), the Annunciation, St. Bridget. The introduction and mass-production of playing cards. Chinese woodblock printing and the limited Chinese use of movable type in clay and wood.

Part II — Materials and Context (Chapters VII-X): The introduction of printing into Italy (Subiaco 1465, Rome, Venice). The history of paper from China through the Arab world to Europe — the Fabriano mills, rag-gathering, watermarks, the cost advantage over vellum. The world of manuscript production: scriptoria, scribes, illuminators, binders, the university book trade, the cost of books before print. The social conditions that created demand for printing: commerce, vernacular literacy, devotional markets, clerical corruption, the inadequacy of the manuscript system.

Part III — Block-Books and Early Typography (Chapters XI-XV): A survey of European block-books: image-only prints (the Eight Rogueries, the Dance of Death), image-and-text block-books (the Biblia Pauperum, Ars Memorandi, Ars Moriendi, the Antichrist, the Life of St. Meinrat). The Donatus grammar fragments — the most frequently printed text of the 15th century and the testing-ground for early typefaces. The Speculum Humanae Salvationis in its four editions, with analysis of its types, paper, and presswork. The anonymous Dutch printers of the third quarter of the 15th century.

Part IV — The Coster Legend (Chapters XVI-XIX): The dating of the Speculum editions by paper-marks and type comparison. The full Coster story as told by Hadrianus Junius (1588): the walk in the wood, carved beech letters, the Speculum as Coster’s first book, the theft by the treacherous servant. The elaboration of the legend by Scriverius, Boxhorn, Meerman, Koning, and De Vries. Van der Linde’s archival demolition — Coster was a tallow-chandler and innkeeper, the Coster pedigree was forged, no mention of printing in Haarlem archives. De Vinne’s own typographic critique.

Part V — Gutenberg and the Mainz Inventors (Chapters XX-XXIII): Gutenberg’s Strasbourg period (c. 1434-1444), the 1439 Dritzehn lawsuit, the mirror partnership, secret arts. Return to Mainz, partnership with Fust (1450), the 42-line Bible, the 1454-1455 indulgence forms. The break with Fust (1455, Helmasperger Notarial Instrument), Gutenberg’s later work with Conrad Humery, the Catholicon (1460), his death in 1468. Fust and Schoeffer’s 1457 Mainz Psalter — first dated printed book, first colophon, first printed music. Schoeffer’s technical improvements in punchcutting and presswork.

Part VI — Synthesis and Tools (Chapters XXIV-XXVI): Review and dismissal of other alleged inventors (Castaldi, Mentel, Jenson, Regiomontanus). The spread of printing after the 1462 sack of Mainz — a timeline of first presses in each major European city. The physical equipment of the early printing house: the wooden hand press, the frisket, the tympan, ink-balls, the composing stick, the type case, the two-pull system.

Chapter-by-Chapter Detail

Part I: Methods and Antecedents (Chapters I-VI)

Chapter I — The Different Methods of Printing (pp. 17-28): Definitions of the four printing methods — copper-plate (intaglio), lithography (planographic), typography (relief from movable type), and xylography (relief from wood blocks). The mechanical differences between each method. The argument that typography’s superiority lies in the mobility of its types and their capacity for infinite re-combination.

Chapter II — Antique Methods of Impression and their Failure (pp. 29-48): A survey of ancient impression techniques: Babylonian brick stamps, Assyrian clay cylinders, Egyptian wood stamps, Roman stamps for pottery and bread, medieval branding irons. De Vinne’s argument that these are stamping, not printing — they lack ink, paper, and the concept of transferring multiple copies of verbal text. The failure traced to the lack of suitable ink, the absence of cheap writing material, and the limited number of readers.

Chapter III — The Key to the Invention of Typography (pp. 49-68): The mechanics of typefounding. The punch (steel letter engraved in relief), the matrix (copper impression struck from the punch), and the mold (adjustable instrument for casting type bodies of uniform width and height). The adjustable hand mold as the essential key to the invention.

Chapter IV — The Image Prints of the Fifteenth Century (pp. 69-87): The earliest European single-leaf woodcut prints — the St. Christopher (1423), the Annunciation, St. Bridget, the Brussels Print, the Berlin Print. Their date, style, and technique.

Chapter V — Printed and Stenciled Playing Cards (pp. 88-108): The introduction of playing cards into Europe from the East. Methods of production: stenciling, woodcut, and combinations. Playing cards as the first mass-produced printed artifacts, creating the technical and commercial foundation for block-book production.

Chapter VI — The Chinese Method of Printing (pp. 109-121): Chinese woodblock printing — paper, ink, book formats, and block-carving technique. The Chinese use of movable type (clay and wood). De Vinne’s conclusion that the Chinese system never developed the punch-matrix-mold mechanism.

Part II: Materials and Context (Chapters VII-X)

Chapter VII — The Early Printing of Italy (pp. 122-132): The introduction of printing to Subiaco (1465), Rome, and Venice. The work of Sweynheym and Pannartz.

Chapter VIII — The Introduction of Paper in Europe (pp. 133-145): Paper from its Chinese invention through the Arab transmission to Spain and Italy. The Fabriano mills. Rag-gathering and paper-making. The cost advantages of paper over vellum. Paper-marks (watermarks) as tools for dating early printed books.

Chapter IX — The Book-Makers of the Middle Ages (pp. 146-170): Manuscript production — the scriptorium, the scribe, the illuminator, the binder. The cost of books. The university book trade (the pecia system). The social conditions that created demand for a cheaper alternative.

Chapter X — The Preparations for Printing (pp. 171-192): The state of education, literacy, and religious devotion in the early fifteenth century. The growth of commerce, the spread of vernacular literacy, the demand for devotional images and books, the corruption of the clergy, the inadequacy of the manuscript system.

Part III: Block-Books and Early Typography (Chapters XI-XV)

Chapter XI — Block-Books of Images without Text (pp. 193-229): Early block-books consisting only of images: the Eight Rogueries, the Dance of Death, other xylographic prints. The technique of cutting the block and the printing process (frotton or screw press).

Chapter XII — Block-Books of Images with Text (pp. 230-253): Major block-books with text and images cut on the same block: the Biblia Pauperum, the Ars Memorandi, the Ars Moriendi, the Antichrist, the Wonders of Rome, the Pomerium Spirituale, the Life of St. Meinrat. Analysis of each for its typographic features.

Chapter XIII — The Donatus, or Boy’s Latin Grammar (pp. 254-263): The Donatus fragments — the Latin grammar by Aelius Donatus, one of the most frequently printed texts of the fifteenth century. The typographic Donatus fragments attributed to Gutenberg (36-line and 42-line types). The Donatus as the testing-ground for early typefaces.

Chapter XIV — The Speculum Salutis, or Mirror of Salvation (pp. 264-281): The Speculum Humanae Salvationis — the block-book/typography hybrid at the center of the Coster controversy. All four editions (two Latin, two Dutch). Analysis of types, illustrations, paper, and presswork. Conclusion that they belong to the 1460s or 1470s.

Chapter XV — The Works and Workmanship of an Unknown Printer (pp. 282-307): Types used in the Speculum editions compared with other Dutch incunabula — the Fables of Lorenzo Valla, the Epitaphs of Pope Pius II, the Enschede Abecedarium. An anonymous school of Dutch printers in the third quarter of the fifteenth century.

Part IV: The Coster Legend (Chapters XVI-XIX)

Chapter XVI — The Period in which the Speculum was Printed (pp. 308-325): Dating the Speculum editions using paper-marks and type comparisons. The paper bears watermarks found in other books of known date from the 1460s-1480s. The types are stylistically later than Gutenberg’s 42-line Bible.

Chapter XVII — The Legend of Lourens Janszoon Coster (pp. 326-346): The Coster story as told by Hadrianus Junius (1588): the walk in the wood, the carved beech letters, the development of ink, the Speculum as Coster’s first book, the theft of the types by the treacherous servant identified as Johann Fust. Junius says wooden types but the Speculum has cast metal. Coster is said to have died in 1439/1440, but records show a Lourens Janszoon Coster alive until 1483.

Chapter XVIII — The Growth of the Legend (pp. 347-359): The elaboration of the Coster story: Scriverius (1628, moving the date to 1428), Boxhorn (1640, moving it to 1420), Meerman (1765, the Origines Typographicae), Koning (1816, confabulating two different Laurens Janszoons), De Vries (c. 1830, claiming 1423).

Chapter XIX — The Downfall of the Legend (pp. 360-374): Van der Linde’s archival evidence from Haarlem records: Lourens Janszoon Coster was a tallow-chandler and tavern-keeper, not a printer. The Coster pedigree was a forged or falsified document from c. 1550. No mention of printing in any Haarlem archive. De Vinne’s typographic critique: no dated Coster print exists, the types are cast metal, and Haarlem produced no printing for forty years after the alleged invention.

Part V: Gutenberg and the Mainz Inventors (Chapters XX-XXIII)

Chapter XX — John Gutenberg at Strasburg (pp. 375-402): Gutenberg’s Strasbourg period (c. 1434-1444). The 1439 Dritzehn lawsuit records. The mirror-manufacturing partnership and secret arts. References to “forms” and “presses” suggesting printing experiments.

Chapter XXI — Gutenberg and his Earlier Work at Mentz (pp. 403-430): Gutenberg’s return to Mainz. The partnership with Johannes Fust (c. 1450). The 800-guilder loan. The 36-line Bible (considered earlier than the 42-line). The 1454-1455 indulgence forms — the first dated products of Gutenberg’s press.

Chapter XXII — The Later Work of Gutenberg (pp. 431-448): The break with Fust (1455, the Helmasperger Notarial Instrument). Gutenberg’s later work with Conrad Humery. The Catholicon of 1460. Gutenberg’s death in 1468.

Chapter XXIII — The Work of Peter Schoeffer and John Fust (pp. 449-479): Fust’s partnership with Peter Schoeffer after the lawsuit. The 1457 Mainz Psalter — first book with a printed date, first with a printer’s colophon, first with printed music. Schoeffer’s technical improvements in punchcutting and presswork.

Part VI: Synthesis and Tools (Chapters XXIV-XXVI)

Chapter XXIV — Alleged Inventors of Printing (pp. 480-491): Review and dismissal of other claimants: Panfilo Castaldi of Feltre, Johann Mentel of Strasbourg, Nicolas Jenson, Regiomontanus, and others.

Chapter XXV — The Spread of Printing (pp. 492-513): The diffusion of typography from Mainz after the 1462 sack of the city. Timeline of first presses in Strasbourg, Cologne, Basel, Rome, Venice, Paris, London, and the Low Countries.

Chapter XXVI — The Tools and Usages of the First Printers (pp. 514-542): The physical equipment of the early printing house: the wooden hand press (screw or lever mechanism), the frisket (paper holder), the tympan (packing), ink-balls (inking pads), the composing stick, the type case, the practice of printing in two pulls.
