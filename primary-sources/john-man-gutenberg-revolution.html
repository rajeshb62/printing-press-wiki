The Gutenberg Revolution (John Man)

The Gutenberg Revolution: The Story of a Genius and an Invention that Changed the World (2002) is a narrative history of Johann Gutenberg and the invention of movable-type printing, covering the period from c. 1400 to the Reformation.

Summary of Contents

The book traces Gutenberg’s life from his birth in Mainz around 1400 through his exile in Strasbourg, his return to Mainz, the invention of printing with movable type, the production of the 42-line Bible, his financial ruin, and the subsequent spread of printing across Europe. It also covers the political and religious context of 15th-century Europe — the Holy Roman Empire, the Great Schism, the Council of Constance, Nicholas of Cusa, and the role of print in the Reformation. Two appendices provide a hypothetical balance sheet for the Bible project and a list of German printers abroad.

Chapter-by-Chapter Detail

Introduction: The Third Revolution

Positions printing with movable type as the third great revolution in human communication, after the invention of writing and the alphabet, and before the internet. Uses the Frankfurt Book Fair as a contemporary symbol of what printing has become, and sets up the central irony of the book: Gutenberg was a businessman who aimed to profit from the Catholic Church’s continent-wide market, but his invention ultimately shattered Christian unity through the Reformation.

Chapter 1: A Golden City, Tarnished

Describes Mainz around 1400 — its streets, guilds, architecture, the Rhine foreshore, and the Gutenberg family house (the Gutenberghof, originally called Judenberg / Jewish Hill). Covers the Black Death and its impact on Mainz (population halved, anti-Jewish pogroms of 1348-49, the burning of Jews outside St Quentin’s Church). Explains the patrician-guild conflict that dominated city politics, the annuity system that bankrupted the city, and the Gutenberg family’s exile to Eltville in 1411 and 1413. Details Gutenberg’s father Friele Gensfleisch zur Laden and his mother Else Wirich, Gutenberg’s probable education (possibly at Erfurt University), his familiarity with coin-making and punch-cutting, and the creation of the 1400 birth-year as a public-relations decision by Mainz city fathers in the 1890s.

Chapter 2: The Strasbourg Adventure

Covers Gutenberg’s move to Strasbourg by 1434. Describes his arrest of Mainz burgomaster Niklaus von Wörrstadt to recover 310 gulden in unpaid annuities. Details his relationship with Ennelin zur Yserin Thure (Little Annie Iron Door) and the breach-of-promise lawsuit brought by her mother Ellewibel. Explains the Aachen pilgrim mirror scheme: Gutenberg planned to mass-produce 32,000 convex metal mirrors for the 1439 pilgrimage, selling at half a gulden each, for a projected 2,500% profit. Describes the partnership formed in 1438 with Hans Riffe, Andreas Dritzehn, and Andreas Heilmann (Gutenberg: 50%, Riffe: 25%, Dritzehn: 12.5%, Heilmann: 12.5%). Covers the postponement of the 1439 Aachen pilgrimage due to plague, Dritzehn’s death in December 1438, the panic to dismantle press and formes to preserve secrecy, and the 1439 Dritzehn lawsuit. Presents the witness depositions including goldsmith Hans Dunne’s testimony about earning 100 gulden “just for that which pertains to the pressing,” the references to “formes,” a press, and the “tool of four pieces” held by two screws that Gutenberg ordered dismantled.

Chapter 3: A Hercules Labouring for Unity

Covers the political and religious context of Gutenberg’s Europe. Introduces Nicholas of Cusa (Nikolaus von Kues, 1401-1464), born near Mainz, roughly contemporary with Gutenberg. Describes Nicholas’s education with the Brethren of the Common Life in Deventer, his studies at Heidelberg and Padua, his career as canon lawyer and statesman, his book De Concordantia Catholica arguing that authority arises from consent, his role at the Council of Basel (1431-1449), the Hussite negotiations (Communion in both kinds), the calendar reform proposal, and his switch from conciliarist to papal champion. Covers the Ferrara-Florence Council (1438-1439) and the failed reunion with the Greek Orthodox Church. Describes Nicholas’s rise to cardinal and bishop of Brixen, the Bursfeld Congregation’s reform of Benedictine monasteries, the push for a standardized missal, and the possibility that Nicholas and Gutenberg met and shared the vision of duplicating texts to achieve Christian unity.

Chapter 4: Something in the Air

Surveys the precursors to Gutenberg’s invention. Covers the Phaistos Disc (1700 BC), Chinese invention of paper (AD 105), block-printing in China/Japan/Korea, Pi Sheng’s movable clay type (11th century), Korean movable metal type (the Prescribed Ritual Texts, 1234), and the Mongol Empire’s possession of paper, movable type, and an alphabetical script (Uigur-derived) without developing printing. Discusses King Sejong of Korea’s invention of the Hangul alphabet (1443-44). Explains why Asian cultures did not develop a printing press: complex writing systems, conservatism, wrong paper type, absence of screw-presses, and no capital markets. Covers two near-misses in Europe: Procopius Waldvogel in Avignon (1444-46), who offered to teach “the art of artificial writing” with two steel alphabets, and the Laurens Coster legend from Haarlem (the story that Coster invented printing and Gutenberg stole it, told by Hadrianus Junius in 1588).

Chapter 5: The Secret Revealed

Explains how Gutenberg’s invention probably worked. Describes the four integrated innovations: (1) the punch — a steel letter engraved in relief on the end of a steel rod; (2) the matrix — the letter punched in intaglio into a softer metal (copper); (3) the hand-held mould — an adjustable two-part device that holds the matrix at the bottom of a rectangular slot, into which molten type-metal is poured, producing a piece of type with the letter standing proud; (4) the type-metal alloy — lead with tin (to improve flow and cooling) and antimony (to harden the metal). Covers the oil-based ink (linseed oil varnish, soot, amber), the adaptation of the screw-press from wine/paper presses, the paper dampening process, and the challenge of consistent pressure. Explains Gutenberg’s departure from Strasbourg in 1444 due to the Armagnac threat, his “hidden years” (1444-1448), and his return to Mainz in 1448 with the help of a 150-gulden loan from his cousin Arnold Gelthus.

Chapter 6: In Search of a Bestseller

Describes Mainz’s financial crisis (373,000 gulden debt, bankruptcy in 1448) and Gutenberg’s return. Covers his first printed work: the Donatus (the Ars Grammatica by Aelius Donatus, a standard Latin grammar), printed in the D-K (Donatus-Kalender) type with 202 characters. Discusses the Sibylline Prophecy fragment (Sibyllenbuch/Weltgericht), a single page found in 1892, printed from D-K type, possibly an early Gutenberg product. Covers the arrival of cardinals Nicholas of Cusa and Juan de Carvajal in Mainz in December 1448 to approve the Bursfeld missal. Describes Gutenberg’s partnership with Johann Fust: two loans of 800 gulden each (1449 and 1452) at 6% interest, secured against equipment. Explains the collapse of the missal project due to the conflict between the Bursfeld Congregation’s text (backed by Rome) and the St Jakob’s text (backed by Mainz’s archbishop). Covers the 1451 Cyprus indulgence — Pope Nicholas V granted King John of Cyprus the right to produce plenary indulgences to raise money for mercenaries against the Turks, and Nicholas of Cusa ordered 2,000 indulgences prepared for sale in Frankfurt in May 1452.

Chapter 7: The Bible

Covers the 42-line Bible (B42) in detail. Describes the physical specifications: two volumes, 1,275 pages, half-folio format (30.7 x 44.5 cm), two columns per page, 42 lines per page (with the first nine folios at 40 lines, then page 10 at 41 lines), 290 different characters including 83 ligatures. Describes the production: three punch-cutters (Hans Dunne, Gotz von Schlettstadt, Hans von Speyer) working for about four months; six compositors working on three pages each, requiring approximately 46,000 characters in use at any time; three presses operating simultaneously, each with two pressmen. Covers the paper (handmade from Italy, revealed by watermarks) and vellum (about 5,000 calfskins for 30-35 copies). Print run: approximately 180 copies (135-150 paper, 30-45 vellum); 37 paper copies and 12 vellum copies survive. Mentions Paul Needham’s computer analysis (with Blaise Aguera y Arcas) revealing dozens of variants of each letter shape, suggesting Gutenberg’s type may not have been produced from punches and matrices in the conventional way. Covers the Turk Calendar (1455) printed after the fall of Constantinople (May 1453). Describes the Piccolomini letter (March 1455): Enea Silvio Piccolomini, the future Pope Pius II, writing to Cardinal Juan de Carvajal from Vienna, reports seeing quinternions (20-page sections) of the Bible at the Frankfurt fair, describes it as “extremely elegant and correct” and readable without glasses, says 158-180 copies had been printed and buyers were already ready. Discusses the design choices — justified right margin, overhanging hyphens, the golden-section proportions of the page, and the letter-spacing adjustments needed to achieve 42 lines.

Chapter 8: Colophon

Covers the Fust lawsuit of 1455. Describes the Helmasperger Notarial Instrument (6 November 1455): Fust claimed 2,026 gulden (the two 800-gulden loans plus unpaid interest and compound interest). Gutenberg argued the second 800 gulden was for “the work of the books” (das werck der bucher) and should not need to be repaid. The judgement required Gutenberg to account for funds not put to mutual use. Fust took over the Humbrechthof workshop, the presses, and the Bible. Describes Peter Schoffer’s defection from Gutenberg to Fust. Covers the Mainz Psalter (1457): 350 pages, two-colour printing (red and blue decorative capitals plus black text), the first printed colophon (attributing the work to Johann Fust and Peter Schoffer), and the first printer’s mark (two shields hanging from a twig). Describes Schoffer’s subsequent success: missals, Durandus (guide to liturgy), canon law books. Covers Gutenberg’s continued work at the Gutenberghof with D-K type: Donatus editions, calendars, the papal bull for a Turkish crusade, a single-page prayer. Describes the 36-line Bible (B36): printed in Bamberg under Albrecht Pfister, using a revised D-K type, expanded to 1,768 pages, about 80 copies (20 on vellum, 60 on paper). Covers the Catholicon (1460): a 746-page Latin dictionary and grammar by Giovanni Balbi, set in the smallest type devised to date (5 million characters, almost twice the length of the Bible), with a colophon praising Mainz and the invention of printing but anonymous about its author. Describes the 1462 sack of Mainz: the conflict between Archbishop Diether von Isenburg and his rival Adolf von Nassau, the battle of 28 October 1462, the expulsion of 800 citizens including Gutenberg and his financier Konrad Humery, and the seizure of the Gutenberghof. Covers Gutenberg’s exile in Eltville (1462-1468), the indulgence printed in 1464 using Catholicon type, the Vocabularius ex quo (1465-67), and Adolf von Nassau’s award of a pension to Gutenberg in January 1465 (2,000 kg of grain and 2,000 litres of wine per year, plus clothing and tax exemption). Gutenberg’s death on 3 February 1468, recorded by Leonhard Mengoss; burial in the church of St Francis, Mainz.

Chapter 9: Pressing to the Limits

Covers the spread of printing from Mainz to the rest of Europe after the 1462 sack. Defines incunabula (books printed before 31 December 1500) and notes the ISTC (Incunabula Short-Title Catalogue) lists almost 30,000 titles. Describes the diffusion: German printers traveled with their tools (punches, hand moulds, formes) seeking work. Strasbourg had 50 printers by 1500; by 1480, 122 towns in Western Europe had presses (50 in Italy, 30 in Germany). By 1500, 236 towns had presses, producing 15-20 million books. Covers specific cases: Heinrich Eggestein and Johann Mentelin in Strasbourg (first German-language Bible translation, banned by the archbishop of Mainz in 1485); Anton Koberger in Nuremberg (Europe’s biggest printer, 24 presses, 100 pressmen, agents across Europe); Hartmann Schedel’s Nuremberg Chronicle (1493, 1,800 illustrations, 2,500 copies in Latin and German editions). Covers Italian expansion: Konrad Sweynheym and Johann Pannartz in Subiaco (Italy’s first printed book, Lactantius, 1465); Johannes and Wendelin von Speyer in Venice (monopoly granted 1467); Nicholas Jenson (Roman typeface, first Italian Bible, 1471); Johann Numeister in Foligno (first edition of Dante’s Divine Comedy, 1472). Describes Aldus Manutius in Venice (Aldine Press, Greek classics, monopoly on Greek printing, Aristotle in five volumes 1495-98). Covers the Hypnerotomachia Poliphili (1499, printed by Aldus), an architectural-erotic fantasy in an invented language, set in Poliphilus type. Discusses French printing: Ulrich Gering and Michael Friburger in Paris (1470, Sorbonne-backed). Covers William Caxton in England: learned printing in Cologne, published the Recuyell of the Historyes of Troye (1471, first printed book in English), set up in Westminster in 1476, published 100 books including Chaucer’s Canterbury Tales. Ends with a postscript on Islam’s rejection of printing: despite paper, ink, alphabetical script, and knowledge of the technology (Jewish refugees printed Hebrew books in Constantinople from 1493), printing was not adopted in the Muslim world for 400 years due to the primacy of oral transmission of the Qur’an, the authority of the ijaza (chain of oral transmitters), and opposition to bida (innovation).

Chapter 10: Christendom Divided, the World United

Traces the role of print in the Reformation. Covers the shift from scribal to print culture, the rise of new specializations in publishing, the spread of scientific knowledge (Regiomontanus’s astronomical observations, Copernicus), architectural theory (Vitruvius, Palladio), cartography (Abraham Ortelius’s Theatrum orbis terrarum, 1570), the invention of the index (first printed index in Fust and Schoffer’s St Augustine, early 1460s; 83 books had alphabetical indexes by 1500), and the Polyglot Bible (Christophe Plantin, Antwerp, 1568-72, in Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Aramaic, and Syriac). The core of the chapter covers Martin Luther: his early life, education at Erfurt, fear of storms and decision to become a monk, his doctrine of justification by faith alone, his horror at Rome’s corruption during his 1511 visit, his posting of the 95 Theses (dated 31 October 1517, though Man notes the nailing-to-the-door story is likely a legend from Melanchthon’s account 30 years later). Describes the indulgence controversy: Pope Leo X’s indulgence to fund St Peter’s Basilica, Johann Tetzel’s sales campaign near Wittenberg (“even if you raped the Virgin Mary”), and Albrecht of Brandenburg’s complex financial arrangement (he borrowed 29,000 gulden from the Fugger banking family to buy the archbishopric of Mainz, to be repaid from indulgence proceeds split 50-50 with the Pope). Covers the rapid spread of Luther’s theses through printing: editions appeared in Leipzig, Basel, and Nuremberg within weeks; a third of all books printed in Germany between 1518 and 1525 were by Luther; his Address to the Christian Nobility of the German Nation sold 4,000 copies in three weeks. Covers the Leipzig Debate (1519) with Johann Eck, Luther’s excommunication (1521), the Diet of Worms (April 1521) where Luther refused to recant, his faked kidnap and seclusion at Wartburg Castle, and his translation of the New Testament into German (1522, completed in 11 weeks) and the Old Testament (1534). Discusses William Tyndale’s parallel work in English: printed his New Testament translation in Worms (1526) by Peter Schoffer the younger, 3,000 copies smuggled into England, six editions and 18,000 copies over the next three years, priced at 4 shillings (compared to 30 pounds for a hand-copied Bible). Covers the Church’s response: the Council of Trent (1545-63), the Index Librorum Prohibitorum (first published 1559, 4,000 books by its end in 1966), and the limits of censorship in the age of print.

Appendix I: The 42-Line Bible — A Possible Balance Sheet

Presents a hypothetical balance sheet based on calculations by German book historian Leonard Hoffmann. Lists costs: six presses (240 gulden), typecases and fittings (60 gulden), three years’ rent (30 gulden), heating (20 gulden), three hand moulds (60 gulden), typecasting metals (100 gulden), ink (30 gulden), paper (400 gulden), vellum (300 gulden), wages (800 gulden), Bible exemplar (80 gulden) = 2,120 gulden for the Bible alone. Adds an estimated 2,200 gulden for the second workshop (Donatus, indulgences, other jobs). Total costs including debts: approximately 4,500 gulden. Projected income: 30-45 vellum copies at 50 gulden each (1,500-2,250 gulden) plus 135-150 paper copies at 20 gulden each (2,700-3,000 gulden) = 4,200-5,250 gulden total. Concludes that the venture was technically insolvent at completion in 1455, with profitability dependent on future sales.

Appendix II: German Printers Abroad — The First Wave

Lists the first German printers in European towns, by country. Includes over 80 towns/centers: Italy (35 towns, from Subiaco 1465 to Barco 1496); France (12 towns, from Paris 1470 to Perpignan 1500); Switzerland (Geneva 1478, Rougemont 1481); Spain (17 towns, from Segovia c.1472 to Montserrat 1499); Portugal (Braga 1494, Lisbon 1495); Africa (Sao Tome 1494); Netherlands (6 towns, from Aalst 1474 to Antwerp 1500); England (London 1476, Oxford 1478); Denmark (Odense 1482); Sweden (Stockholm 1483); Bohemia-Moravia (3 towns, from Winterberg 1484 to Olmutz 1499); Hungary (Budapest 1473); Poland (Cracow 1473-74). Source: Ferdinand Geldner, Die Deutschen Inkunabeldrucker, Vol. 2, Stuttgart, 1970.
