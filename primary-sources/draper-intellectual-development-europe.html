A History of the Intellectual Development of Europe

John William Draper, M.D., LL.D. A History of the Intellectual Development of Europe. Second Edition. New York: Harper & Brothers, Franklin Square, 1864. Copyright 1862; delayed by the Civil War and issued with an 1863 postscript; second edition 1864 (no substantive changes from the first). Single-volume Harper text (xxii + ~631 printed pages in the OCR set; 26 chapters). Local source: Anna’s Archive Hathi-style page-OCR ZIP (oclc 3224700).

Draper (1811–1882), Professor of Chemistry and Physiology in the University of New York, presents the book as the social-historical completion of his Human Physiology: individual life as emblem of national life; European intellectual history ordered by “physiological” ages (Credulity, Inquiry, Faith, Reason, Decrepitude) under immutable natural law. It is Victorian scientific historiography—a continental survey from Greek theology through medieval Christendom, Islam and the Italian system, maritime discovery, the Reformation, and the scientific-industrial Age of Reason—not a monograph on printing. Chapter XX (and the England digression in Ch. XXI) nonetheless contain a dense mid-nineteenth-century synthesis of the invention of printing, early edition geography, the cheapening of books, pulpit vs press, newspapers, and liberty of the press as engines of the Reformation and the Age of Reason.

Summary of the argument (whole work)

Draper’s master claim is that nations, like individuals, pass through determinate intellectual ages under physical conditions and natural law. Greece supplies the type (Ch. I–VII). Rome and early Christianity prepare a European Age of Inquiry closed by councils and a pontiff (Ch. VIII–IX). A long European Age of Faith follows—Byzantine East cut short by Vandal, Persian, and Arab attacks; Western “Italian system” organized by Gregory the Great, challenged by northern moral reform, western (Arab-influenced) intellectual pressure, and eastern military pressure, and reviewed after the fall of Constantinople (Ch. X–XVIII). The Age of Reason approaches through maritime discovery (Ch. XIX) and through criticism, modern languages, printing, and the Reformation (Ch. XX), with England as a case study of Faith’s social residue (Ch. XXI). The European Age of Reason proper is astronomy and the true place of the earth (Ch. XXII), geology and deep time (Ch. XXIII), man’s place in the organic series (Ch. XXIV), the union of science and industry (Ch. XXV), and a conclusion on organizing public intellect (Ch. XXVI). Printing enters as one of the chief instruments that dethrone Latin clerical monopoly of knowledge and make pulpit secondary to press.

Full chapter-by-chapter breakdown

Chapter I — On the Government of Nature by Law

Opens the program: how far Europe’s advance in civilization has been fortuitous, how far determined by primordial law. Gradual acquisition of the idea that nature is governed by law—supported by astronomical, meteorological, and physiological discoveries (Kepler’s laws, trade-winds, bird migrations, balance of vegetable and animal life, variation and permanence of species). Individual man as emblem of communities and nations; they exhibit epochs of life and are under physical conditions, therefore under law. Plan of the work: Greek intellectual history in five characteristic ages, then European intellectual history. Closes on the “grandeur” of government by law.

Chapter II — Of Europe: Its Topography and Ethnology (Greek Age of Credulity)

Europe as geographic peninsula and historical dependency of Asia; topography, meteorology, secular geological movements and their effect on inhabitants; ethnology via vocabularies (peopling from Asia). Comparative theology of Greece: stage of sorcery, anthropocentric stage, linkage to false geography and astronomy (Heaven, Earth, Under World); origin, variation, and progress of Greek theology issuing in Ionic philosophy. Decline of Greek theology under advance of geography and philosophical criticism; secession of poets, philosophers, historians; abortive public attempts to sustain the old theology; duration of decline and fall.

Chapter III — Digression on Hindu Theology and Egyptian Civilization

Foreign influences on Europe at this stage. India: comparative theology through sorcery and anthropocentric phases; Vedaism as contemplation of Matter / adoration of Nature (Vedas, Institutes of Menu)—universe as God, emanation, transmigration, absorption, penitential quietude; Buddhism as contemplation of Force—impersonal supreme power, passage of everything to nonentity, monastic system, practical godlessness. Egypt: mysterious to old Europeans; history, public works, fall; antiquity of civilization and art; philosophy, hieroglyphic literature, Nile agriculture and astronomy; comparative theology from animal and star worship through pantheism, trinities, incarnation, redemption, future judgment, trial of the dead, rituals.

Chapter IV — Greek Age of Inquiry (Rise and Decline of Physical Speculation)

First European philosophizing rising out of contemporary religion. Ionian philosophy (water, air, or fire as first principle) founds psychology, biology, cosmogony, astronomy and ends doubting any criterion of truth. Italian (Pythagorean) philosophy of numbers and harmonies; transmigration. Eleatic advance toward pantheistic / Oriental ideas. Rise of philosophy in European Greece under Mediterranean commerce; Athens as intellectual centre; demoralization alongside art. Athenian higher analysis by the Sophists—rejection of philosophy, religion, even morality, ending in atheism. Political dangers of the higher analysis, with a Middle Ages illustration.

Chapter V — The Greek Age of Faith (Rise and Decline of Ethical Philosophy)

Sophist anarchy pushes the Greek mind from physics to morals—one step from Inquiry to Faith. Socrates rejects physical and mathematical speculation, asserts virtue and morality; life and death. Schools from his movement that teach pursuit of pleasure and self-gratification. Plato and the Academy: three primal principles, personal God, nature of world and soul, ideal theory, reminiscence, transmigration, Republic, proofs of immortality; criticism of the doctrines. Rise of the Skeptics; Pyrrho on uncertainty of knowledge; passage into Epicurean quietude and irreligion; decomposition of Socratic and Platonic systems in later Academies; end of the Greek Age of Faith.

Chapter VI — The Greek Age of Reason (Rise of Science)

Macedonian campaign disastrous politically for Greece but ushers in the Age of Reason. Aristotle founds inductive philosophy—method inverse of Plato’s; powerful in principle, failing in his own hands for want of knowledge, carried out by the Alexandrians. Zeno (Stoic) as ethical counterpart to Aristotle in the physical. Foundation of the Museum of Alexandria—libraries, observatories, botanical gardens, menageries, dissecting houses; Euclid, Archimedes, Eratosthenes, Apollonius, Ptolemy, Hipparchus on geometry, natural philosophy, astronomy, chronology, geography. Decline of the Greek Age of Reason.

Chapter VII — The Greek Age of Intellectual Decrepitude (Death of Greek Philosophy)

Melancholy close: philosophy becomes retrospective; Philo the Jew and Apollonius of Tyana lean on inspiration, mysticism, miracles. Neo-Platonism (Ammonius Saccas, Plotinus, Porphyry, Iamblichus, Proclus)—Alexandrian Trinity, ecstasy, alliance with magic and necromancy. Justinian closes the philosophical schools. Summary of Greek philosophy’s four problems (origin of the world; nature of the soul; existence of God; criterion of truth) solved differently across Inquiry, Faith, Reason, Decrepitude. Law of variation of Greek opinion: national intellect develops as individual intellect does. Final conclusions on God, world, soul, criterion—with illustrations and criticisms.

Chapter VIII — Digression on the History and Philosophical Influences of Rome

Transition from the Greek type to all Europe. Primitive European religious ideas; form of their variations determined by Rome—hence necessity of Roman history. Rise and development of Roman power, Mediterranean supremacy, demoralization of Italy, concentration of power, imperialism, extinction of the true Roman race. Homogeneous thought across Mediterranean countries; imperialism prepares the way for monotheism; momentous transition in religious ideas. Opinions of Roman philosophers; coalescence of new and old ideas; seizure of power by the illiterate and consequent debasement of Christianity in Rome.

Chapter IX — The European Age of Inquiry

Progressive variation of opinions closed by councils and concentration of power in a pontiff. Rise of Christianity distinguished from ecclesiastical organization; demanded by the empire’s condition; brief conflict with paganism; first organization; Eastern vs Western sects; three primitive forms (Judaic, Gnostic, African—only the last continues). Spread from Syria; antagonism to imperialism; Diocletian; Constantine’s policy and personal relations to the Christian party. Trinitarian controversy; Arius; Council of Nicaea. Rise of the Bishop of Rome through Pelagian, Nestorian, and Eutychian disputes; rivalry with Constantinople and Alexandria. Necessity of a Western pontiff and Eastern councils. Period closes at Alaric’s sack of Rome; Augustine’s defense; criticism of his writings; destiny of the three great bishoprics.

Chapter X — The European Age of Faith (Age of Faith in the East)

Constantine’s policy tends to paganization of Christianity. Byzantine union of Church and State; persecution of philosophy; political necessity of Patristicism (science of the Fathers) and its peculiar doctrines. Obliteration of Greek knowledge: libraries and Serapeum of Alexandria; destruction under Theodosius; death of Hypatia; extinction of learning in the East under Cyril and successors. Subdivision announced: Age of Faith in the East, then in the West—the former closed prematurely by external attack.

Chapter XI — Premature End of the Age of Faith in the East

Three attacks destroy the Byzantine system. Vandal attack: loss of Africa; Justinian’s costly recovery and depopulation. Persian attack under Chosroes: loss of Syria, fall of Jerusalem, True Cross carried off; deep moral impression. Arab attack: birth, mission, and doctrines of Mohammed; rapid spread in Asia and Africa; fall of Jerusalem; losses of Christianity to Islam; Arabs become a learned nation. Review of the Koran; reflections on Christendom’s loss of Asia and Africa.

Chapter XII — The Age of Faith in the West

Western Age of Faith marked by paganized forms; Arabian military pressure isolates the Bishop of Rome and permits independence. Gregory the Great organizes the age’s ideas, materializes faith, allies it with art, rejects science, creates the Italian form of religion. Papal–Frankish alliance diffuses that form; political history of the agreement; new dynasty in France; conversion of Europe. Value of the Italian form judged from papal biography. Contrast with the East: the West completes its phases and eventually reaches Reason.

Chapter XIII — Digression: Arabians to Their Age of Reason (Medicine via Nestorians and Jews)

Arab intellectual development guided by Nestorians and Jews, in a medical direction, on a theological basis of alliance. Byzantine antagonism to scientific medicine; suppression of Asclepions; miracle-cure and superstition. Nestorians inherit old Greek medicine (sub-digression: Hippocrates separating medicine from religion; Cnidos; Constantine’s suppressions; Egyptian anatomy and Alexandrian physicians). Jewish physicians emancipated from superstition; colleges; science and letters. Contemporary magic, necromancy, philosopher’s stone, elixir. Arabs originate scientific chemistry (strong acids, phosphorus, etc.); geological ideas; chemistry applied to medicine; approach of conflict between Saracenic material system and European supernatural system.

Chapter XIV — Age of Faith in the West continued (Image-Worship and the Monks)

Arab influence now philosophical as well as military; decomposition of Christianity into Greek and Latin forms; emancipation of popes from Constantinople, new Frankish dynasty, reconstructed Western empire. Origin of image-worship; Saracen wars reveal inutility of images in Asia/Africa; iconoclasm; emperors prohibit, monks and court women sustain, final victory of the latter. Popes sustain images in the West; quarrel with emperor; papal revolt and Frankish alliance. History of monasticism—hermits and coenobites, spread from Egypt over Europe, miracles and legends, humanization of houses; monks materialize religion and impress their ideas on Europe.

Chapter XV — Three Attacks on the Italian System (Northern or Moral)

Geographic bounds of Latin Christianity. Northern/moral attack: German emperor insists on papal reformation; Gerbert (Sylvester II) as representative—both poisoned by Italians (in Draper’s telling). Commencement of intellectual rejection from the Arabian doctrine of Reason over Authority; transubstantiation controversy; rise of Scholasticism; mutiny among monks. Gregory VII spontaneously enforces reform, overcomes the emperor, nearly establishes a European theocracy; popes seize military and monetary resources through the Crusades. (Eastern/military attack reserved for later; Western/intellectual attack next.)

Chapter XVI — Western or Intellectual Attack on the Italian System

Pressure from Arab Spain. Christendom’s barbarism contrasted with Saracen Spain’s condition. Diffusion of Arabian intellectual influences through France and Sicily; Alhazen (science), Algazzali (philosophy). Innocent III prepares to combat these influences; sack of Constantinople by Catholics and results for the West. Mohammedan light literature followed by heresy; crushing of heresy in the South of France by armed force, Inquisition, mendicant orders, auricular confession, casuistry. Rising sentiment embodied in Frederick II in Sicily; conflict with and overthrow by the pope; mutiny spreads among mendicant orders.

Chapter XVII — Overthrow of the Italian System (Combined Intellectual and Moral Attack)

Irreligion among mendicants; publication of heretical books—the Everlasting Gospel and the Comment on the Apocalypse (Joachim / mid-thirteenth-century terror to the hierarchy). Conflict of Philip the Fair and Boniface VIII; outrage and death of the pope; removal of the papacy to Avignon; post-mortem trial of the pope for atheism and immorality. Templars fall into infidelity; trial and punishment. Immoralities of the Avignon court; return to Rome; Great Schism; three popes; disorganization of the Italian system. Council of Constance attempts constitutional monarchy of the Church; murders of John Huss and Jerome of Prague; pontificate of Nicolas V; end of the Italian system’s intellectual influence. (Print-adjacent: heretical books already as weapons before movable type’s full European career.)

Chapter XVIII — Eastern Military Attack; General Review of the Age of Faith

Fall of Constantinople; momentary effect on the Italian system; Greek Church as a “spectre from the dead.” General review: supernaturalism and its logic over Europe; destroyed by Jews and Arabians; total extinction of that intellectual regime. Jewish physicians—acquirements, collision with European imposture-medicine, effect on higher classes, opposition. Two impulses against the medieval order: intellectual from the West, moral from the North; astronomy as vehicle of the former; discovery of Italian affairs; Machiavelli; what the Church had actually done. Entire movement of the Italian system read through four revolts against it.

Chapter XIX — Approach of the Age of Reason (Preceded by Maritime Discovery)

Last division of the work: national maturity. Experimental philosophy emerging already in the Age of Faith; Age of Reason ushered in by maritime discovery and European criticism. Three great voyages: Columbus discovers America; da Gama doubles the Cape and reaches India; Magellan circumnavigates—material and intellectual results of each. Digression on social condition of America: isolated societies show the same succession of ideas and institutions; Mexico and Peru prove law in the development of man. (Communications and geographic knowledge expansion as preconditions that printing will later fix and multiply.)

Chapter XX — Approach of the Age of Reason (Preceded by the Rise of Criticism) — wiki core for printing

Printed chapter head (OCR ~pp. 465–491+): restoration of Greek literature in Italy; modern languages; invention of printing (revolutionizes communication of knowledge, acts on public worship, renders the pulpit secondary); the Reformation (indulgences, individual judgment, political course, Italian effects); arrest of the Reformation (internal Protestant causes; Roman Counter-Reformation, Inquisition, Jesuits; secession of great critics); culmination toward individual liberty of thought.

Prelude: Latin, Greek, Hebrew, modern tongues

  • Unity of the Church required Latin as sacred language; decline of Latin and rise of vernaculars measure papal contraction toward an Italian principality.
  • Hatred of restored Greek and incoming Hebrew; University of Paris long resists Greek for its bearing on Latin theology; Vulgate authority expected to fall (“second crucifixion between two thieves” in Draper’s phrasing of contemporary polemic).
  • Cosimo and Lorenzo de’ Medici, Ficinus, Chrysoloras (Greek teaching dated ~1395), Aurispa’s manuscripts; Turkish pressure and fall of Constantinople drive learned Greeks west.
  • Popularity of The Imitation of Christ read as lay self-reliance and distrust of clergy (“Every Man his own Priest”).
  • Rising critical standards: miracle as proof only of its own circumstance; martyrdom not proof of dogma.

Invention of printing (~A.D. 1440)

Draper declines to settle priority among Coster of Haarlem, Gutenberg of Mainz, or a Venetian–Chinese transmission story; notes a 1441 Venice decree implying prior knowledge of printing there.

Claims and examples he bundles as early history:

  • Coster and the Speculum Humanae Salvationis ~1440; Gutenberg and Fust (Faust) Mainz Bible without date, 1455—“art reached perfection at once,” typography still admired.
  • Early specimens: exhortation against the Turks, 1454; two letters of indulgence of Nicolas V., same date.
  • Block books first (page on wood), then movable type; early sales as manuscripts.
  • Two of Fust’s workmen print in Italy only from 1465 (Lactantius, Cicero de Officiis, Augustine de Civitate Dei).
  • Art to France 1469, then general in large European towns.
  • Printers their own booksellers; typical edition size ~300 copies; folios → quartos → duodecimos from 1501.
  • Price of books reduced by four fifths; University of Paris tariffs sale and supervises content for Church and State.

Interpretive punch lines he draws: Italy in the van; England backward (Italy ~1400 ≈ England ~1500); Paris sixfold over London, and in the next decade Paris 430 vs London 26; as late as 1550 only seven works printed in Scotland, none classical; first Spanish work (1474) on the Conception of the Virgin as local taste.

Two modes of print’s effect

  1. Multiplication and cheapening of books. Scriptorium supply had matched limited demand; ancient world (except China) lacked a mass reading class—information mouth-to-mouth. Printing processes (stamps, bricks, seals) were known; the binding constraint was paper (Spanish Moorish flax paper; Damascus cotton charta Damascena; Chinese varieties). Demand bred readers; cheap books bred more demand. Side cost to authors: shortened longevity of individual works under rapid succession.
  2. Substitution of reading for pulpit instruction. Public worship assumed a non-reading people. The press becomes rival to the pulpit; enables direct government–people communication without religious intermedium—first step, in Draper’s American framing, toward separation of Church and State. Oral/scenic cathedral and village-church teaching had been uniquely powerful; eventually reading’s active frame outruns passive listening. Parallel in civil life: oratory yields to journalism.

Newspapers and liberty of the press

  • Turkish war of 1563: newspapers first appear in Venice (manuscript).
  • Gazette de France 1631.
  • English press establishes itself in the civil wars (Mercuries, Intelligences, etc.).
  • Everywhere early restraint: Rome’s Index Expurgatorius; University of Paris (cross-ref. his p. 472); England from crown judges under common law toward jury determination of libel character—press as reflector of public opinion.

Reformation arc (print-adjacent)

Printing and criticism as preparatory; moral impulse given form by Italian pecuniary needs (indulgences). Political history of Reformation; Italian irreligion among elites vs German sincerity; Leo X’s misreading of Luther; arrest of expansion via Protestant sectarian subdivision (no concentrated constructive power) vs Catholic organization. Roman weapons: moral counter-reformation, intensified Inquisition, Jesuits (1540 bull of Paul III), embellished worship. Criticism enthrones then undermines patristic authority; Bible multiplies “without end” in every language in reformed Europe. Index examples he cites include suppression of the Koran printed in Venice 1530, and Paul IV’s 1559 Index with dozens of Bible editions and printers under ban.

Chapter XXI — Digression: England at the End of the Age of Faith — wiki secondary core

Results produced by the Age of Faith, using England (most interesting to the American reader) as measure. Condition at suppression of the monasteries; condition at close of the seventeenth century—locomotion, literature, libraries; social and private life of laity and clergy; brutality in administration of law; profligacy of literature; the theatre in three phases (miracle → moral → real plays). Estimate of advance made in the Age of Faith vs early Age of Reason.

Print- and media-relevant strands in the digression:

  • Early eighteenth century: thirty-four counties without a printer; only press north of Trent at York; private libraries thin.
  • Drama phases: miracle play → moral play → real play (Shakespeare); pulpit and stage as access routes in a non-reading society; coffee-houses and newsletters; slow growth of liberty of the press.

Chapter XXII — The European Age of Reason (True Position of the Earth)

Age of Reason ushered in by an astronomical controversy: geocentric doctrine (earth as centre and noblest body) vs heliocentric (sun centre; earth a small planet). Struggle between ecclesiastical and astronomical parties; Inquisition; burning of Bruno; imprisonment of Galileo. Invention of the telescope; overthrow of the ecclesiastical idea; rise of physical astronomy; Newton; rapid development of natural philosophy. Universe under dominion of mathematical, necessary laws. Progress from anthropocentric ideas to man’s true insignificance in the universe. (Print-adjacent only insofar as scientific controversy and Inquisition operate in a print world already established in Ch. XX.)

Chapter XXIII — Age of Reason continued (History of the Earth)

After victory on the earth’s place, investigation of the earth’s age—patristic idea of less than six thousand years intellectually crushed. Oriental and Occidental doctrines of the earth in time; weakening by astronomical facts; rise of scientific geology. Impersonal solution chiefly through facts of heat. Limitless duration from inorganic facts (igneous and aqueous rocks) and organic facts (successive creations and extinctions, contemporary distribution). Slowly declining temperature; catastrophe vs law; analogy of individual and race development—both under unchangeable law. Plan of the universe: multiplicity of worlds in infinite space, succession of worlds in infinite time.

Chapter XXIV — Age of Reason continued (Nature and Relations of Man)

Bearing of heliocentric / plurality-of-worlds restoration (Bruno, Galileo, et al.) on man’s nature and relations. Animal life: transitory forms; plants and animals; animals as aggregates of matter expending force from the sun. Organic series: man a member; position by anatomy and physiology of the nervous system—automatic, instinctive, intellectual. Same progressive development in individual man, animal series, and life of the globe—under eternal law. Aim of Nature is intellectual development; human institutions must conform. Summary: sun produces inorganic and organic forms; analogies and differences between animals and man; the soul; the world.

Chapter XXV — Age of Reason continued (Union of Science and Industry)

European progress in exact knowledge compared to Greek Age of Reason. Discoveries respecting air (mechanical and chemical properties; plants and animals; winds; meteorology; sound). Ocean (tides, currents, clouds, decomposition of water). Other substances and progress of chemistry. Electricity, magnetism, light, heat. Mechanical philosophy and inventions; physical instruments; results illustrated by cotton manufacture, steam-engine, bleaching, canals, railways; improvements in machinery; social changes and intellectual activity. Scientific contributions of various nations, especially Italy.

Chapter XXVI — Conclusion: The Future of Europe

Summary of the book’s argument on mental progress of Europe. Intellectual development is the object of individual life and the result of social progress. Nations at maturity instinctively attempt their own intellectual organization—example of China, its imperfections and accomplishments. Organization of public intellect is the end toward which European civilization tends.
