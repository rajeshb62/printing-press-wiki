A Short History of the Printing Press (Hoe)

A Short History of the Printing Press and of the Improvements in Printing Machinery from the Time of Gutenberg up to the Present Day by Robert Hoe (New York, 1902) traces the mechanical evolution of the printing press from Gutenberg’s wooden screw press through the rotary web perfecting presses of 1900. The book is organized as a continuous chronological narrative covering roughly 20 distinct eras of press development.

Summary of Contents

The book describes the mechanical design, operation, and performance of printing presses across four and a half centuries. It covers wooden hand presses (c. 1450-1800), the first iron hand presses (1800-1827), bed-and-platen power presses (1822-1858), flat-bed cylinder presses (1814-1850s), rotary type-revolving presses (1846-1870s), web perfecting presses (1865-1902), and folding mechanisms. Throughout, the text gives specific press speeds (sheets or impressions per hour), mechanical principles (screw, toggle joint, lever, crank, cam), and descriptions of how each machine handles inking, impression, sheet feeding, and delivery.

Detailed Breakdown of Contents

1. Gutenberg’s Wooden Press (c. 1450)

Opening description of the original printing press: two upright timbers with wooden cross pieces, a wooden screw passing through a cross-beam, its lower point resting on the center of a wooden platen which was screwed down onto the type. The form was inked with a ball of leather stuffed with wool, paper spread over it, and a blanket laid on the paper to soften the impression. The mechanical principle is traced to the cheese and linen presses of medieval times. Hoe estimates Gutenberg’s output at approximately 300 sheets per day.

2. The Wooden Common Press (1450-1620)

The screw press worked with a movable bar continued in use for about 150 years without material change. Forms of type were placed on wooden (sometimes stone) beds enclosed in frames called “coffins,” moved in and out by hand. After each impression the platen had to be screwed up with the bar so the printed paper could be removed and hung to dry.

3. The Blaeu Press (c. 1620, Amsterdam)

First recorded improvements by Willem Janszoon Blaeu. The spindle of the screw passed through a square block guided in the wooden frame, from which the platen was suspended by wires or cords — the block preventing any twist in the platen and ensuring more equal motion to the screw. A device was added for rolling the bed in and out, and a new form of iron hand lever for turning the screw. This press was introduced into England and used on the continent; substantially the same press Benjamin Franklin worked on as a journeyman in London.

4. The Common Press in the 18th Century

Little further improvement before 1798. The labor in working one of the old screw presses was described as about equal to that of a plowman. The iron platen had been used on wooden presses before the all-iron press appeared.

5. The Stanhope Iron Press (1800, Earl of Stanhope)

The first major redesign in 350 years. The frame was cast in one piece of iron instead of wood. The screw was retained but a combination of levers was added to give greater power with less exertion. These were the first all-iron printing presses ever constructed. Printers added lever combinations to their wooden presses, which generally destroyed them as the wood could not withstand the strain.

6. Clymer’s Columbian Press (c. 1816, George Clymer)

An iron machine entirely dispensing with the screw. A long cast-iron lever was placed over the platen, one end attached to the upright of the cast-iron frame and the other raised and lowered by a combination of smaller levers worked by the pressman. The impression was given and the platen raised and lowered by a spindle or pin attached to the center of the large cross lever at the top, balanced to facilitate raising. Clymer took his invention to England, where it was known as the “Columbian” press.

7. Smith Press (1822, Peter Smith)

Peter Smith, connected with R. Hoe & Co. in New York, devised a machine with a cast-iron frame that substituted a toggle joint for the screw-and-lever combination.

8. Washington Hand Press (1827, Samuel Rust)

Samuel Rust of New York perfected a press that was a great improvement on the Smith press. The frame had hollowed cast-iron uprights admitting wrought-iron bars riveted at top and bottom, giving additional strength while reducing the amount of metal. The patent was purchased by R. Hoe & Co., who improved on it and manufactured the press as the “Washington” press. Over 6,000 were made and sold by Hoe & Co. alone. Made in seven sizes.

Mechanical description: the bed slides on a track and is run in and out from under the platen by turning a crank with belts attached to a pulley on its shaft. The impression is given by a curved lever acting on a toggle joint; the platen is lifted by springs on either side. Attached to the bed is a “tympan” frame covered with cloth, standing inclined to receive the sheet. Another frame called the “frisket” is attached to the tympan, covered with paper with parts cut away to prevent the chase and furniture from soiling the sheet. The frisket is turned down over the sheet and tympan, and all are folded down when the impression is taken. Automatic inking rollers were attached, operated by a weight raised by the pressman’s pull; the descent of the weight drew the rollers over the type and returned them to the inking cylinder while the pressman placed another sheet. Further improvements in inking apparatus were patented by Hoe & Co., in which ink distribution was effected by an apparatus driven by steam power.

9. Bed and Platen Power Presses (1822-1858)

Daniel Treadwell (1822, Boston): the first steam-powered bed-and-platen press, with wooden frames. Only three or four were constructed.

Isaac Adams (1830, 1836): the best machines of this description. First made with wooden frames, afterward with iron frames. In 1858 Adams’s business became the property of Hoe & Co., who continued manufacture with added improvements. Over 1,000 sold in 57 sizes.

Mechanical description: type placed on an iron bed, which is raised and lowered by straightening and bending a toggle joint by means of a cam, giving the impression against an iron platen fixed above and held in position by upright iron rods secured to the bottom bar. The ink fountain is at one end of the press; inking rollers travel twice over the form in a movable frisket frame while the bed is down; paper is taken in by grippers on the frisket and carried over the form; the bed rises and the impression is given; sheets pass forward from the frisket by tapes to a sheet flier which delivers them on the fly board. Maximum speed: 1,000 sheets per hour for the larger sizes.

Otis Tufts (1834, Boston): also described.

10. Koenig’s Cylinder Press (1814)

Friedrich Koenig (a Saxon who visited England in 1806) devised the first practical flat-bed cylinder press, assisted by mechanic Andrew Bauer. The form of type was placed on a flat bed, the cylinder above having a three-fold motion: the first third of the turn received the sheet upon one of the tympans and secured it by the frisket; the second gave the impression and allowed the sheet to be removed; the third returned the tympan empty to receive another sheet.

Koenig devised the reciprocating motion of the type bed: a pinion carried on the inner end of a long shaft turned by gearing from outside the press frame, with a universal joint in its length allowing an up-and-down motion of the pinion as it revolves. Underneath the bed and fastened to it is a “rack” with a crescent-shaped segment of hard metal at each end. The wheel at the outer end of the shaft revolves the pinion and moves the bed by the teeth in this rack. At reversal, the pinion turns around over a pin or stud, against the segment on the rack, and re-engages its teeth in the opposite side, carrying the bed back.

In 1814 Koenig patented a continuously revolving cylinder press, with the part of the periphery not used for impression slightly reduced in diameter to allow the form to return under it freely. He showed designs for single-cylinder, two-cylinder (printing one side), and two-cylinder perfecting presses (printing both sides). The perfecting press had two forms placed at each end of a long bed; paper printed on one side was carried by tapes over a registering roller to the other cylinder for the reverse side.

Koenig installed two two-cylinder presses at the London Times in 1814, printing on one side only at 800 sheets per hour.

11. Improvements to the Cylinder Press (1814-1840)

Napier (1828, 1830): introduced grippers or fingers for conveying sheets around the cylinder during impression and for delivering them after printing (tapes had previously been used). First to manufacture presses with small impression cylinders making two or more revolutions per sheet. Devised toggles for bringing cylinders down to print on the form and raising them to let the form run back without touching.

Applegath & Cowper: various improvements, mostly simplifying Koenig’s presses. Also improved Koenig’s perfecting press into an efficient machine.

Bacon & Donkin (1813), Cowper (1816, 1818), Applegath (1818): various patents.

12. Hoe & Co.’s Flat-Bed Cylinder Presses (1832 onward)

In 1832, Sereno Newton was sent to England to investigate cylinder press improvements. Resulted in the “Single Small Cylinder,” “Double Small Cylinder,” and Large Cylinder “Perfecting” Press.

Single Large Cylinder: the first flat-bed cylinder press ever used in the United States. The whole circumference of the cylinder was equivalent to the entire travel of the bed forwards and backwards; the cylinder made one revolution for each impression without stopping. A portion of the cylinder took the impression; the remainder was turned down small enough to allow the type on the bed to pass back under it without touching. Hundreds were made. Output: up to 2,000 impressions per hour.

Double Small Cylinder: the travel of the bed was long enough that the form passed backward and forward under both cylinders. Two feeders put in sheets; maximum speed about 4,000 per hour from the two cylinders (2,000 each), printed on one side.

Hoe & Co. patented bed-driving mechanism (1847): two racks facing each other but not in the same vertical plane, separated by the diameter of the driving wheel on a horizontal shaft movable sideways to engage either rack. Bed reversal accomplished by a roller at either end of the bed entering a recess in a disc on the driving shaft, which in a half-revolution brought the bed to a stop and started it in the opposite direction. A crank action operating directly upon the bed from a shaft having a fixed centre.

13. Stop Cylinder Press (Dutartre, 1852)

Devised and patented by a Frenchman named Dutartre in 1852, introduced into the United States about 1853 by Hoe & Co., who patented many improvements. Mechanical description: type secured on a traveling iron bed moving back and forth on steel friction rollers, driven by a simple crank motion, stopping and starting without noise or jar. All running portions made of fine steel. The cylinder is stopped by a cam motion pending the backward travel of the bed; during the interval of rest the sheet is fed down against guides and grippers close before the cylinder starts, ensuring accuracy of register. After impression, the sheet is transferred to a skeleton cylinder with grippers which delivers it over fine cords onto a sheet flier. Ink distribution by a vibrating polished steel cylinder and a flat table at the end of the traveling bed; form-inking rollers vary from four to six. Various sizes. Average output of a press with bed 36x54 inches: 1,000 to 1,500 impressions per hour. A larger version with bed 45x62 inches ran at 1,700 impressions per hour.

14. Hoe Type Revolving Machine (1846)

The landmark rotary press, with patents by Richard M. Hoe. The first was placed in the Philadelphia Ledger in 1846. Type was securely fastened on a central horizontal cylinder by means of cast-iron beds (one for each page of the newspaper) with V-shaped column rules tapering toward the feet of the type, allowing the type to be held firmly in position with the surface forming a true circle. Around the central cylinder, four to ten impression cylinders were grouped.

Sheets were fed in by boys, taken from the feed board by automatic grippers or fingers operated by cams in the impression cylinders, and conveyed around against the revolving form. A patented sheet flier consisted of a row of long wooden fingers fastened to a shaft, operated by a cam and springs; the printed sheet was conducted out underneath each feed board by tapes to the sheet fliers, which laid them in piles on tables.

Inking: composition rollers placed between each impression cylinder; the fountain below underneath the main type cylinder. The portion of the type cylinder surface not occupied by type was used as a distributing table (its surface lower than the type), with inking rollers rising and falling alternately to place ink on the type and receive a new supply from the distributing surface.

Four-cylinder machines achieved about 8,000 papers per hour printed on one side (2,000 per feeder). Ten-cylinder machines achieved about 20,000 papers per hour printed on one side. Adopted throughout the United States and Great Britain; the London Times eventually ordered two Ten-Cylinder presses to replace its Applegath machine. 175 were made in total.

15. Curved Stereotype Plates

The perfection of casting stereotype plates on a curve using flexible paper matrices cast in curved moulds to any desired circle. These plates were placed on the Hoe Type Revolving Machine on beds adapted to receive them. Newspaper publishers could duplicate forms and run several machines simultaneously. In some large offices (New York Herald, London Daily Telegraph, London Standard), as many as five machines ran at once.

16. Applegath’s Vertical Type-Revolving Press (1848)

Constructed for the London Times. All cylinders were vertical instead of horizontal. Type was placed on a large upright central cylinder whose circumference represented flat surfaces for each column (polygonal, not circular). Around this central cylinder were eight smaller vertical impression cylinders; inking rollers inked the type as it passed between impression cylinders. Sheets fed down by hand from eight flat horizontal feed-boards through tapes, grasped by another set of tapes and passed sideways between impression cylinder and type cylinder. Maximum output: 8,000 impressions per hour on one side. Only one was ever made; it was removed to make way for Hoe Type Revolving Presses.

17. Bullock’s Web Press (1865)

William Bullock of Philadelphia constructed the first printing machine to print from a continuous web or roll of paper (1865). Two pairs of cylinders: two form/plate cylinders and two impression cylinders. The second impression cylinder was made large to provide additional tympan surface and lessen offset from the first printed side. Sheets were cut before printing; cutting done by knives in cylinders. Delivery by a series of automatic metal nippers placed on endless leather belts at distances apart to grasp each sheet successively. The machine was put up in several offices but rejected for unreliability, especially in delivery; it was later improved enough to see considerable use.

London Times rotary perfecting press (c. 1868): similar to Bullock’s in printing apparatus, but cylinders all of one size and placed one above the other. Sheets severed after printing, brought up by tapes, and carried down to a sheet flier that moved back and forth. Marinoni (Paris) devised a similar machine with the impression and form cylinder of one size placed one above the other, with separate fly boards for delivery.

18. Hoe Web Perfecting Press (1871)

R. Hoe & Co.’s rotary perfecting press printing from a continuous web. Major difficulties addressed: set-off of the first side (rapid-drying inks were developed), paper strength in rolls (paper-makers improved manufacture), rapid severing of sheets after printing, and reliable delivery.

Stephen D. Tucker’s patented gathering cylinder solved the problem of rapid flat delivery: sheets were not entirely severed by cutters but simply perforated after printing, then drawn by accelerating tapes (which completely separated them) onto a gathering cylinder constructed so that six perfect papers could be gathered one over the other. By means of a switch, these were turned off onto one sheet flier at the proper moment. The gathering and delivery cylinder solved the problem of rapid flat delivery.

First machine placed at Lloyd’s Weekly Newspaper in London; first in the United States at the New York Tribune. When run at full speed: 18,000 perfect papers per hour (printed both sides, cut, and delivered accurately on one feed-board). Average speed in printing offices: 12,000; some at about 14,000 per hour.

19. Folding Mechanisms (1875 onward)

Early folding machines used hand-fed folding mechanisms: sheets carried by tapes running on pulleys under striking blades which forced them between pairs of folding rollers. After the first fold, sheets were carried under striking blades at right angles to the first for a second fold. None could work above 8,000 per hour.

Stephen D. Tucker’s rotating folding cylinder (1875): folded papers as fast as they came from the press, at 15,000 per hour. The first Hoe folding machines were exhibited at the Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia in 1876. They combined a gathering cylinder with a rotary folding cylinder and tapes conveying printed sheets under horizontal folding blades that thrust them between folding rollers at alternate angles, finally delivering them on traveling belts by a small flier.

20. Double Supplement Press (c. 1876)

Produced four, six, eight, ten, or twelve-page papers at 24,000 per hour, and sixteen-page papers at 12,000 per hour, with odd pages inserted and pasted, papers cut at top and delivered folded. Constructed in two parts: cylinders in one portion twice the length of those in the other; short cylinders used for supplements. Paper entered from two rolls through two portions of the machine, carried between two pairs of type and impression cylinders, printed on both sides. The two webs passed over turning bars laid evenly one over the other and pasted together. Webs passed down upon a triangular “former” which folded them along the center margin, then over a cylinder for the final fold — a revolving blade within the cylinder projecting and thrusting the paper between folding rollers, while a knife in the same cylinder severed the sheet. Several hundred were made and operated in the United States, Great Britain, and other countries.

21. Quadruple Press (1887)

Constructed in 1887; first placed in the New York World. Supplement portion increased in width. Produced eight-page papers at 48,000 per hour; ten-to-sixteen-page papers at 24,000 per hour; all delivered cut, pasted, and folded. A “straight-line” form of the Double Supplement and Quadruple machines arranged cylinders in horizontal rows or tiers, one above the other, with two pairs of cylinders in each tier and folding and delivery apparatus at the end of the machine.

22. Sextuple Press (1891, New York Herald)

Constructed for James Gordon Bennett of the New York Herald; occupied about 18 months to build; composed of about 16,000 pieces. Form and impression cylinders all placed parallel. Fed from three rolls, each more than five feet wide. Six plate cylinders, each carrying eight stereotype plates (representing eight pages of the Herald), and six impression cylinders. At full speed, cylinders made 200 revolutions per minute. Consumed 25.75 miles of 63-inch-wide paper per hour (equivalent to 51.5 miles of Herald-width paper). A double folding mechanism and two delivery outlets automatically counted papers in piles of fifty.

Capacities: 90,000 four-page papers per hour; 72,000 eight-page papers per hour; 48,000 ten or twelve-page papers per hour; 36,000 sixteen-page papers per hour; 24,000 fourteen-to-twenty-four-page papers per hour. All printed, cut, pasted, folded, counted, and delivered.

23. Rotary Type-Revolving Web Perfecting Press (1881)

Designed to print from one form of type at greater speed. Forms of type for both sides placed on a central cylinder surrounded by impression cylinders and inking rollers. No feeders and no grippers — the roll of paper passed around impression cylinders at one side of the form cylinder, turned upside down at the lower part, carried upwards, and the opposite side presented in turn between each impression cylinder and the forms. Four impression cylinders: 12,000 per hour; eight impression cylinders: 24,000 per hour. One or two rolls of paper used depending on configuration.

24. Three-Page-Wide Press (1888)

Capacity: two-page papers with one fold at 60,000 per hour; four-page papers with two folds at 24,000 per hour; eight-page papers folded twice at 12,000 per hour; twelve-page papers similarly folded at 12,000 per hour. Outside pages could receive first or last impression at will, enabling large cuts to print without offset. Grippers, horizontal folding knives, and all tapes except short leaders eliminated — movements all rotary. Dimensions: 6 ft 1 in high, 8 ft wide, 15 ft 5 in long.

25. Newspaper and Pamphlet Press (1889)

Plates or forms for both sides placed on one cylinder (one side on each half). One impression cylinder only, extended inking apparatus. Produced sheets of 8 to 128 pages, folded in 12mo, 8vo, 4to, or folio sizes, ready for the binder. Paper supplied from roll, passed over a “former” into the folding machine. Curved electrotypes used. The first press to bring average book and catalogue work within the range of web press work. Speed on a 32-page form: about 16,000 copies per hour. Plates admit of underlays and overlays as on a flat-bed press. No tapes; folding on rollers and small cylinders without smutting. Knives cut the sheet to the right size for folding; papers automatically delivered counted in lots of fifty.

26. Rotary Art Press (1890, De Vinne Press)

Built for Theodore L. De Vinne (Century Magazine). Sixty-four plates bent to the proper curve fastened on one cylinder (60 inches long, about 30 inches in diameter). Sixteen inking rollers supplied with ink from two fountains. Shafts of the impression cylinder and plate cylinders (4.5 inches in diameter) — described as not giving or springing under the strongest impression. Fed by four feeders from single sheets. The first rotary press capable of fine illustration work, combining the quality of stop-cylinder work with rotary speed.

27. Octuple Perfecting Press with Folders

Prints from four rolls, each four pages wide. From four deliveries: 96,000 four-to-eight-page papers per hour; 72,000 ten-page; 60,000 twelve-page; 48,000 fourteen or sixteen-page; 42,000 eighteen-page; 36,000 twenty-page; 24,000 twenty-four-page papers per hour. Further developed into the Improved Combination Octuple (Double Quadruple) and Color Machine, also producing papers in colors.

28. Double Sextuple Press (c. 1898-1902)

Two separate complete printing mechanisms, each fed from three four-page-wide rolls of paper, with gathering and folding apparatus in the center between the two sections. Twelve plate cylinders total, each carrying eight plates the size of a newspaper page. Ink applied by four form rollers after distribution by vibrating rollers and cylinders. Angle bars for transferring half-width webs.

Full capacity (all black, six rolls): 96,000 twelve-page papers per hour. Fourteen and sixteen-page papers at 72,000 per hour; eighteen to twenty-four-page papers at 48,000 per hour. As a color press: 48,000 sixteen-page papers per hour with outside pages in four colors and black. Dimensions: length 35 ft, height 17 ft, width 9 ft; weight about 225,000 pounds; approximately 50,000 parts.

29. Color Printing Developments (late 1890s)

With properly prepared inks and suitable plates, colors could be printed almost simultaneously without mingling. Accurate register maintained when printing from a roll. The largest color press (built for the New York Journal): eleven separate impressions or colors on a single copy (six on one side, five on the other). Speed of about 16,000 eight-page papers per hour. Four to thirty-two-page papers from one, two, or three double-width rolls. Magazine forms (half the page size) at 16,000 to 24,000 per hour, delivered folded, cut, and automatically wire-stitched.
